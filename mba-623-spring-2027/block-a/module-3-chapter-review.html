PART I
The accounting cycle, the FSET, adjustments, and financial statements

LO 3-1 The Accounting Cycle and the FSET

The accounting cycle

Every period, companies move through the same four-step cycle:

StepActivityWhat happens
1Analyze transactions and prepare (and post) entriesEach transaction is analyzed for its effect on the accounts and recorded.
2Prepare (and post) accounting adjustmentsEnd-of-period adjustments bring the accounts up to date — prepaid and unearned items, and accruals.
3Prepare financial statementsThe adjusted balances are organized into the income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, balance sheet, and statement of cash flows.
4Close the booksTemporary accounts are reset to zero, setting up the accounts for the next cycle (period).

Three tools for recording transactions

ToolWhat it does
Financial statement effects template (FSET)Represents relatively complex transactions in a simple, concise form; captures each transaction’s effect on all four financial statements; makes analysis and “what if” scenarios easy.
T-accountsExpand each account into increases and decreases and can keep running totals.
Journal entriesThe classic debits and credits used in practice (shown in the margins of the textbook). See Part II.

The financial statement effects template (FSET)

The FSET is built on the accounting equation, with cash split out from other assets and equity split into its two parts:

Cash + Noncash assets = Liabilities + Contributed capital + Earned capital
Revenues − Expenses = Net income
Apple at September 24, 2022: Assets $352,755 = Liabilities $302,083 + Equity $50,672
  • The balance sheet columns record every transaction’s effect on the accounting equation — and the equation must stay in balance after each one.
  • The income statement columns sit to the right. Every revenue and expense also flows into earned capital (retained earnings), which links the two statements.
  • Entries in the cash column become the statement of cash flows; entries in the contributed and earned capital columns become the statement of stockholders’ equity. One template therefore captures all four statements.
  • Each amount is labeled with the specific account it affects (e.g., INV for inventory, AP for accounts payable, RE for retained earnings).

LO 3-2 Analyzing Transactions with the FSET

To record any transaction in the FSET, answer three questions:

  • What accounts are affected?
  • What is the direction of each effect — increase or decrease?
  • What is the amount of each effect?

Reading the Apple FSET: a few examples
#1 Buy inventory on account. Noncash assets (inventory) +221,912; liabilities (accounts payable) +221,912. No income statement effect — buying inventory is not an expense.
#2 Sell inventory on credit. Two effects: accounts receivable +394,328 with revenue +394,328; inventory −223,546 with cost of goods sold +223,546. Net income and earned capital both rise by the 170,782 gross profit.
#3 Collect receivables. Cash +392,422; accounts receivable −392,422. One asset replaces another — no income effect.
#10 Pay dividends. Cash and earned capital fall by 14,793. Dividends are a distribution to owners, not an expense, so net income is unaffected.
#13 Collect cash in advance. Cash +300 and unearned revenue (a liability) +300. No revenue yet — Apple hasn’t delivered.

LO 3-3 Accounting Adjustments

Companies make accounting adjustments at the end of each period so the financial statements are accurate and complete.

Why adjustments matter: unpaid wages
Suppose employees earned wages during the period but will not be paid until the next period. If the company ignores the wages owed:
Liabilities are understated — wages payable is too low.
Net income is overstated — wages expense is too low.
Both the balance sheet and the income statement would be wrong. An adjustment fixes both.

Four types of adjustments

Timing of cashAdjustment typeDefinition
Cash paid or received before the expense or revenue is recognizedPrepaid (deferred) expensesAdvance cash payments that will ultimately become expenses
Unearned (deferred) revenuesCash received from customers before any goods or services are provided
Cash paid or received after the expense or revenue is recognizedAccrued expensesExpenses incurred and recognized on the income statement even though cash has not yet been paid
Accrued revenuesRevenues earned and recognized on the income statement even though cash has not yet been received

LO 3-5 The Closing Process

Closing (or closing the books) “zeroes out” the temporary accounts by transferring their ending balances to retained earnings.

Temporary accounts — closed each periodPermanent accounts — never closed
Revenues, expenses, and dividends. Each starts every period at $0, so its balance reflects only the current period’s activity.Balance sheet accounts. The ending balance of one period becomes the opening balance of the next.

FSET versus practice

Closing in the FSETClosing in practice
The FSET and T-accounts are teaching tools that show transactions’ effects on the statements.Companies record transactions and adjustments with journal entries.
The FSET is highly stylized, but its simplicity is instructive.Retained earnings is not updated continuously as revenues and expenses are recorded.
Every revenue and expense is automatically transferred to earned capital — no separate closing step is needed.Companies use a formal closing process at the end of each reporting period.

The three closing steps

  • Close revenue and gain accounts to retained earnings.
  • Close expense and loss accounts to retained earnings.
  • Close the dividends account to retained earnings.

After closing, Apple’s retained earnings reflect the full year: $5,562 + $394,328 − $294,525 − $14,793 − $90,186 − $3,454 = $(3,068). The closing journal entries are in Part II.

Revenue, cost of goods sold, and which events change equity

January: a sale to dealers on credit for 2,000 raises assets and equity by 2,000. The cost of those bikes, 1,300, lowers inventory and equity by 1,300. The net effect on equity is +700, the gross profit.

Prepaid expenses

January: Cullowhee paid 240 in cash for 12 months of insurance. Cash fell by 240 and prepaid expenses rose by 240. At month-end 1/12 expired: 240 ÷ 12 = 20 of insurance expense, with prepaid expenses down 20 and equity down 20.

Depreciation and other expense adjustments

January: depreciation of 240. Net property, plant and equipment falls by 240 and equity falls by 240. Cash is unchanged.

Accruals: interest, wages and taxes

January at Cullowhee: accrued interest 26, wages owed 310, and income tax at 25% of pretax income of 104 = 26. Each raises a liability and lowers equity. None uses cash in January.

Receivable and inventory balances

January at Cullowhee ($ thousand): receivables 6,040 + credit sales 2,000 − collections 1,100 = 6,940.

Inventory: beginning 11,700 + purchases 400 − cost of goods sold 1,300 = 10,800.

Putting it together: net income and adjustments

January ($ thousand): 2,000 − 1,300 − 20 − 240 − 26 − 310 = pretax 104. Tax at 25% = 26. Net income = 78.
