Recording Business Events with the FSET: Chapter Summary

A quick review of the key ideas, with Cullowhee examples · Not graded · Print or save as PDF if you like

Use this as a refresher before the Gate Quiz or while you work through the walkthrough. Nothing here is submitted. Textbook: Chapter 3. The short audio overview and review handout for this module are in the Canvas module.

Formulas to know

  • Assets = liabilities + equity after every event
  • Ending receivables = beginning + credit sales − collections
  • Ending inventory = beginning + purchases − cost of goods sold
  • Monthly prepaid expense = payment ÷ months of coverage
  • Pretax income = revenues − cost of goods sold − other expenses
  • Net income = pretax income × (1 − tax rate)

1. The accounting equation and the FSET

Nora wants each January item traced through the template. The template is the accounting equation, one event at a time.

Key ideas
  • The FSET (the “grid”) shows each event’s effect on assets, liabilities and equity. Assets always equal liabilities plus equity.
  • Borrowing or buying on account: assets and liabilities both rise. A credit sale or a share sale: assets and equity rise.
  • Paying a supplier: assets and liabilities fall. Collecting a receivable: one asset replaces another.
Cullowhee example

Cullowhee, January 2027 ($ thousand):

Buy parts on account (400)+400+400none
Collect from dealers (1,100)no net changenonenone
Pay suppliers (350)−350−350none

Each row balances: assets change = liabilities change + equity change.

2. Revenue, cost of goods sold, and which events change equity

Only some events change equity. Spotting which ones is the key to the template.

Key ideas
  • A sale on credit raises assets (receivables) and equity (revenue).
  • The cost of the bikes sold lowers assets (inventory) and equity (cost of goods sold). Revenue minus cost of goods sold is gross profit.
  • Collecting, paying suppliers, buying on account, borrowing and buying equipment do not change equity.
Cullowhee example

January: a sale to dealers on credit for 2,000 raises assets and equity by 2,000. The cost of those bikes, 1,300, lowers inventory and equity by 1,300. The net effect on equity is +700, the gross profit.

3. Prepaid expenses

Paying in advance does not create an expense yet. It buys a future benefit.

Key ideas
  • Paying for coverage or rent in advance is an asset (prepaid expense). Cash falls, prepaid expenses rise, equity does not change.
  • Each month part of the prepayment is used up. That part becomes an expense, lowering the prepaid asset and equity.
Cullowhee example

January: Cullowhee paid 240 in cash for 12 months of insurance. Cash fell by 240 and prepaid expenses rose by 240. At month-end 1/12 expired: 240 ÷ 12 = 20 of insurance expense, with prepaid expenses down 20 and equity down 20.

4. Depreciation and other expense adjustments

At month-end, costs that were used up must be matched to the period.

Key ideas
  • Depreciation spreads the cost of equipment over its life.
  • It lowers assets (through accumulated depreciation) and equity (an expense). No cash moves in the month the expense is recorded.
  • The same pattern holds for amortization and for prepaid insurance that expires.
Cullowhee example

January: depreciation of 240. Net property, plant and equipment falls by 240 and equity falls by 240. Cash is unchanged.

5. Accruals: interest, wages and taxes

Some costs are incurred before they are paid. The statements must show them anyway.

Key ideas
  • An accrued expense has been incurred but not yet paid. Record the expense and a liability; equity falls and no cash moves.
  • Interest accrual = principal × annual rate × fraction of the year. Wages accrual = pay for the days worked but unpaid.
  • Income tax expense = pretax income × tax rate, and it is usually also a liability until paid.
Cullowhee example

January at Cullowhee: accrued interest 26, wages owed 310, and income tax at 25% of pretax income of 104 = 26. Each raises a liability and lowers equity. None uses cash in January.

6. Receivable and inventory balances

A balance sheet account is a rollforward: where it started, what changed, where it ended.

Key ideas
  • Ending receivables = beginning + credit sales − collections.
  • Ending inventory = beginning + purchases − cost of goods sold.
  • Rearrange to find a missing piece: collections or purchases.
Cullowhee example

January at Cullowhee ($ thousand): receivables 6,040 + credit sales 2,000 − collections 1,100 = 6,940.

Inventory: beginning 11,700 + purchases 400 − cost of goods sold 1,300 = 10,800.

7. Putting it together: net income and adjustments

Nora’s request ends with the month’s results. Net income comes after the adjustments.

Key ideas
  • Prepare the statements in order: income statement first (it gives net income), then the statement of stockholders’ equity, then the balance sheet, then the statement of cash flows.
  • Pretax income = sales − cost of goods sold − all other expenses (including adjustments). Net income = pretax income − income tax.
  • An adjustment is needed when a cost has been incurred (or revenue earned) but not recorded, or when a prepaid amount has been partly used. Routine cash events are already recorded.
Cullowhee example

January ($ thousand): 2,000 − 1,300 − 20 − 240 − 26 − 310 = pretax 104. Tax at 25% = 26. Net income = 78.

All people and companies in the Cullowhee case are fictional.
