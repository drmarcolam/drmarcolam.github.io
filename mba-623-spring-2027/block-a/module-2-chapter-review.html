LO 2-1 The Balance Sheet

Balance sheet basics

The balance sheet has three sections — assets, liabilities, and stockholders’ equity (also called owners’ equity or shareholders’ equity) — and reports them at a point in time.

ASSETS = LIABILITIES + STOCKHOLDERS’ EQUITY

Balance sheet accounts are permanent accounts: their balances carry over from one period to the next rather than resetting to zero.

The flow of costs: asset or expense?

Every cost a company incurs ends up in one of two places:

When the cost creates…It is recorded as…Example
An immediate benefitAn expense on the income statementThis month’s utilities, advertising that runs this period
A future economic benefitAn asset on the balance sheet (the cost is capitalized)A delivery truck, a building, inventory not yet sold

As a capitalized asset is used up, its cost moves from the balance sheet to the income statement, where it is recognized as an expense — for example, depreciation on the truck, or cost of goods sold when inventory is sold.

Assets

Assets are resources expected to produce future economic benefits. To appear on the balance sheet, an asset must meet two conditions:

  • It is owned or controlled by the company.
  • It arises from a past transaction or event.

Measuring assets

  • Most assets are reported at historical cost — the original acquisition cost — not current market value.
  • If a company cannot value an asset with reasonable certainty, it does not record it.
  • As a result, many significant “assets” never appear on the balance sheet — especially knowledge-based (intellectual property) assets such as a strong management team, a well-designed supply chain, or superior technology.

Net working capital and the operating cycle

Net working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities

Net working capital (often just working capital) is the liquidity cushion a company needs to run its business. How much it needs depends on its operating cycle — the time between paying cash for goods and receiving cash from customers. The cycle runs:

  • Cash → Inventory. The company uses cash to buy or manufacture inventory, typically using supplier credit (accounts payable).
  • Inventory → Receivables or cash. It sells the inventory either on credit (accounts receivable) or for cash.
  • Receivables → Cash. When receivables are collected, part of the cash settles accounts payable; the rest returns to the cash account for the next cycle.

Apple example: net working capital = $135,405 − $153,982 = $(18,577). Apple’s current liabilities exceed its current assets — a sign of its unusual cash cycle, below.

Non-current liabilities

Non-current liabilities are obligations due after one year:

  • Long-term debt — borrowings scheduled for repayment more than a year out: bonds, notes, debentures, mortgages, and other long-term loans.
  • Other long-term liabilities — obligations such as pension liabilities and long-term tax liabilities that will be settled a year or more in the future.

  • Accumulated other comprehensive income (loss) — cumulative changes in certain asset and liability fair values that bypass the income statement.

Common-size balance sheet

A common-size balance sheet (also called vertical analysis or a right-sized balance sheet) expresses every line item — assets, liabilities, and equity — as a percent of total assets. The percentage columns in Apple’s balance sheet above are an example.

ComparisonWhy common-size helps
Same company, over timeIf the company has grown or shrunk, dollar amounts hide shifts in the relative size of accounts; percentages reveal them.
Across companiesRemoves size differences so a small firm can be compared with a large one — and because percentages are unit-free, it works across currencies.
Against a benchmarkCompares the company to an industry average or other standard.

Book value versus market value

Book value (of equity)Market value (market capitalization)
Stockholders’ equity as reported under GAAPNumber of common shares outstanding × current stock price

Book value ≠ market value, for four reasons:

  • GAAP reports many assets and liabilities at historical cost; the market tries to estimate fair value.
  • GAAP excludes assets that cannot be reliably measured (brands, talent, technology).
  • Market value reflects each company’s market characteristics (industry, risk, growth).
  • GAAP does not consider expected future performance; the market is forward-looking.
Market-to-book ratio
Market-to-book = Market value ÷ Book value of equity
Equivalently, stock price per share ÷ book value per share. The textbook reports that the median market-to-book ratio for S&P 500 companies ranged from 3.02 to 4.05 over 2013–2022.
Apple illustration: with about 15.94 billion shares outstanding and a stock price of roughly $150 in late September 2022, Apple’s market value was about $2.4 trillion — nearly 50 times its $50.7 billion book value, far above the typical company.

Recognizing revenues and expenses: accrual accounting

Revenues and expenses are not determined by when cash is received or paid. Two principles — the foundation of accrual accounting, the system GAAP requires — guide recognition:

  • Revenue recognition principle — FIRST, recognize revenue in the period it is earned: when the company has done what it is obligated to do under the sales contract.
  • Expense recognition principle (the matching principle) — THEN, recognize the expenses incurred to generate that revenue.
TimingRevenueExpense
Same period as cashRevenue recognized and cash received simultaneously (a cash sale)Expense recognized and cash paid simultaneously
Cash comes laterRevenue recognized now, cash received later → accounts receivableExpense recognized now, cash paid later → accrued liability
Cash comes firstCash received in advance, revenue recognized later → unearned (deferred) revenueCash paid in advance, expense recognized later → prepaid expense (asset)

Discontinued operations

Some income statements include a separate component for discontinued operations — the disposal of a business that represents a strategic shift with a major effect on the company’s results.

Income Statement Format with Discontinued Operations
Sales
− Cost of goods sold
Gross profit
− Operating expenses
− Nonoperating expenses (+ nonoperating revenues)
− Tax expense (applies to income from continuing operations)
Income from continuing operations
± Discontinued operations, net of tax (transitory; not expected to recur)
Net income
  • Discontinued operations have two components, often combined: (1) the income or loss from the business before its sale, and (2) any gain or loss on the sale itself.
  • They are reported net of tax, separately from income from continuing operations, because they will not recur.
  • The separation helps analysts isolate the company’s core, recurring profit and cash flow.

Common-size income statement

A common-size income statement (vertical analysis) divides every line item by total revenue (net sales), expressing the statement in percentage terms. Analysts typically prepare one as the starting point of their analysis. It supports three types of comparison:

  • Time-series — the same company across two or more years.
  • Cross-sectional — two or more companies in the same year, adjusting for size and currency differences.
  • Benchmark — the company against an industry average or other standard.

Apple’s common-size story, visualized in the textbook: sales come from iPhone (52%), Services (20%), Mac (10%), Wearables (10%), and iPad (8%). Cost of sales absorbs 57%, leaving a 43% gross margin. R&D (7%) and SG&A (6%) bring operating income to 30%; income taxes take 5%, leaving net income of 25% of sales.

LO 2-3 The Statement of Stockholders’ Equity

The statement of stockholders’ equity reconciles the beginning and ending balances of each equity account:

  • Common stock and additional paid-in capital increase by the proceeds from selling stock.
  • Retained earnings increase by net income and decrease by dividends and by stock repurchased and retired.
  • Accumulated other comprehensive income rises and falls with changes in asset and liability fair values that bypass the income statement.

LO 2-4 The Statement of Cash Flows

Why a separate cash statement?

  • The income statement measures income under accrual accounting — revenue when earned, expenses when incurred — so a company can report income even if no cash has been received.
  • The income statement tells us about the economic viability of the company’s products and services.
  • The statement of cash flows tells us about the company’s ability to generate cash — where cash came from and what the company did with it.

Format: inflows and (outflows)

SectionCash flows from…
Operating activitiesThe company’s transactions and events that relate to its operations
Investing activitiesAcquisitions and divestitures of investments and long-term assets
Financing activitiesIssuing and repaying borrowings and equity (including dividends and stock repurchases)

Analyzing the statement of cash flows

Analysis focuses on the sources and uses of cash. Key questions:

  • Is the company generating cash from operating activities? Is that operating cash flow sustainable?
  • Is it investing to grow its infrastructure (PP&E) or to enter new markets by acquiring other companies?
  • Is it using excess cash to build liquidity (buying marketable securities)?
  • Is it paying down debt, paying dividends, or repurchasing stock?

Apple’s answers: $122 billion of operating cash flow funded modest net investing outflows and $111 billion of financing outflows — largely the $90 billion of buybacks and $15 billion of dividends — with cash falling $11 billion.

Understanding these linkages lets you answer practical management questions more accurately, such as:

  • What are the financial statement effects of buying new PP&E versus renting it?
  • How is ROA affected if the company discontinues certain operations?
  • What are the income statement and balance sheet effects of outsourcing production?
  • How will a proposed merger affect profit margin and asset turnover?

Global accounting: U.S. GAAP versus IFRS presentation

StatementMost visible differences
Balance sheetMany IFRS balance sheets are presented in reverse order of liquidity — long-term assets first, and equity before liabilities.
Income statementU.S. companies present three years of income statements under SEC rules; IFRS requires two (the current year plus one comparative year).
IFRS companies may classify expenses by function (cost of sales, SG&A, R&D) or by nature (raw materials, labor, depreciation).
Cash flow statementDifferences are in presentation only. U.S. GAAP classifies interest paid and interest and dividends received as operating. Under IFRS, companies have historically been allowed to choose; beginning in 2027, IFRS 18 requires most companies to classify interest and dividends received as investing and interest paid as financing.
