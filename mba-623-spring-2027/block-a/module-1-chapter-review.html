LO 1-1 Business Activities

Financial reporting exists to support investment and management decisions. Three groups rely on it most heavily: investors and equity analysts, lenders and credit analysts, and company managers and employees.

Every company, whatever its industry, engages in three types of business activities. Each one leaves a footprint in the financial statements.

ActivityWhat it isWhere it shows up
FinancingRaising the money the company needs — from owners (issuing stock) and from nonowners (borrowing) — and returning it through dividends, buybacks, and debt repayment.Liabilities and equity on the balance sheet; financing section of the statement of cash flows; statement of stockholders’ equity
InvestingUsing that money to acquire the resources needed to run the business — property, plant, equipment, technology, other companies.Assets on the balance sheet; investing section of the statement of cash flows
OperatingUsing those resources to make and sell products and services — buying inventory, paying employees, selling to customers.Income statement (revenues and expenses); operating section of the statement of cash flows
The planning cycle
Business activities happen over a period of time and are shaped by planning and by outside forces (competitors, regulators, the economy).
Prior-year financial statements are an input to planning. Current-year financial statements then report the results — the company’s performance during the year and its condition at year-end. Those statements become the starting point for next year’s plan.

SEC Regulation Fair Disclosure (Reg FD)

Reg FD is designed to stop selective disclosure — companies tipping off favored analysts or investors before everyone else. The rule says:

“Whenever an issuer discloses any material nonpublic information regarding that issuer, the issuer shall make public disclosure of that information . . . simultaneously, in the case of an intentional disclosure; and . . . promptly, in the case of a non-intentional disclosure.”

In practice: if management shares material news with anyone outside the company, it must share it with the whole market — at the same moment if the disclosure was intentional, and promptly if it was accidental.

International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)

  • Companies in more than 145 countries — including the European Union, the United Kingdom, Canada, and Japan — report under IFRS.
  • IFRS are developed by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB). U.S. GAAP is set by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB); the two boards have worked together on joint projects.
  • For most transactions, IFRS and U.S. GAAP are more alike than different, so the analysis skills in this course carry over.

How the statements link across time

The beginning-of-period balance sheet and the end-of-period balance sheet are snapshots. The other three statements explain what happened in between:

  • The income statement explains the change in retained earnings that comes from operations (net income).
  • The statement of stockholders’ equity explains every change in the equity accounts — net income, dividends, stock issuances and repurchases.
  • The statement of cash flows explains the change in the cash balance.

Balance sheet

The balance sheet reports a company’s financial position at a point in time. It lists:

  • Resources (assets) — what the company owns.
  • Sources — how those assets were financed:
  • Nonowner financing (liabilities) from banks, creditors, and suppliers
  • Owner financing (equity) from stockholders
The accounting equation
Resources = Nonowner Financing + Owner Financing
Assets = Liabilities + Equity
The equation holds for ALL companies at ALL points in time.

Assets: short-term versus long-term

  • Assets are the resources a company has at its disposal.
  • They are listed in order of nearness to cash — cash first, then receivables, inventory, and so on, with long-term assets such as property and equipment last.
  • The mix of short- and long-term assets is driven largely by the company’s industry and business model. In the textbook’s comparison, American Express and Best Buy hold mostly short-term assets, while AT&T and Darden Restaurants hold mostly long-term assets.

Financing: owners and nonowners

  • Companies fund their assets with a combination of owner financing (equity) and nonowner financing (liabilities, or debt).
  • Owner financing has two components: (1) resources contributed by owners (common stock and additional paid-in capital) and (2) profits retained in the business (retained earnings).

Operating income and competitive position

Operating income as a percent of sales varies widely. In the textbook’s comparison, Microsoft earns roughly 40% while Amazon earns only a few percent. A company’s ability to build barriers to competitive pressure — through patent protection, effective marketing, and similar advantages — is a key driver of its operating profitability.

Statement of stockholders’ equity

This statement explains the year-over-year changes in the equity accounts on the balance sheet:

  • Common stock and additional paid-in capital — stockholders’ net contributions to the company.
  • Retained earnings — net income over the company’s life, minus all dividends and minus stock repurchases that are not held for resale.
  • Other equity — items covered later in the course.

Managerial choices in financial reporting

  • Managers have discretion in how they apply accounting standards.
  • GAAP historically relied on detailed, rule-heavy guidance. The emphasis now is on whether the statements, taken as a whole, “fairly present” the company’s financial condition.
  • The CEO and CFO must personally attest that the financial statements are accurate and complete.

LO 1-4 Profitability Analysis

Profitability analysis is the crucial first step in assessing prior strategic decisions and evaluating strategic alternatives.

Return on assets (ROA)

ROA and its two drivers
ROA = Net income ÷ Average total assets
ROA = Profit margin (PM) × Asset turnover (AT)
= (Net income ÷ Sales) × (Sales ÷ Average total assets)
Profit margin measures profitability — how much of each sales dollar becomes profit. Asset turnover measures productivity — how many sales dollars each dollar of assets generates.

Worked example: Apple, fiscal 2022

MeasureCalculationResult
Profit margin$99,803 ÷ $394,32825.3%
Average total assets($351,002 + $352,755) ÷ 2$351,879
Asset turnover$394,328 ÷ $351,8791.12 times
ROA$99,803 ÷ $351,87928.4%
Check: PM × AT25.3% × 1.1228.4%

Apple’s total assets at September 25, 2021 ($351,002) come from its fiscal 2022 Form 10-K comparative balance sheet.

Profitability versus productivity across companies

  • Companies reach similar ROAs by very different routes. The textbook plots profit margin against asset turnover for dozens of companies around a curve representing ROA = 9.6%.
  • High margin, low turnover: Microsoft, McDonald’s, Amgen — pricing power, few assets needed per sales dollar relative to profit.
  • Low margin, high turnover: Costco, Walmart, Best Buy — thin margins offset by moving large volumes through their asset base.
  • The trade-off reflects strategy and business model: it is hard to have both a high margin and high turnover, because competition tends to push companies toward the curve.

Return on equity (ROE)

ROE = Net income ÷ Average stockholders’ equity
Average equity = (Equity at start of year + Equity at end of year) ÷ 2

ROE measures the return to stockholders, whereas ROA measures the return for the entire company (all capital providers). For Apple: average equity = ($63,090 + $50,672) ÷ 2 = $56,881, so ROE = $99,803 ÷ $56,881 = 175.5%. ROE far exceeds ROA because Apple finances most of its assets with liabilities and has shrunk its equity base through share repurchases.

Earnings and stock prices

Ball and Brown (1968) tracked stock returns for the 12 months before and 6 months after companies released annual earnings. Firms that reported higher earnings than the prior year earned positive excess returns; firms that reported lower earnings earned negative excess returns. Most of the price movement happened before the annual report was released, as the market absorbed other information. The lesson: accounting earnings capture what drives stock prices.

Ball, R., and P. Brown. 1968. “An empirical evaluation of accounting income numbers.” Journal of Accounting Research (Autumn): 159–178.

LO 1-5 The Competitive Business Environment

Financial statements must be read in the context of the broader business environment. Understanding the forces a company faces sharpens our interpretation, improves forecasts of future performance, and leads to better estimates of company value. Two frameworks help: Porter’s Five Forces and SWOT analysis.

Porter’s Five Forces

ForceEffect on the company
Industry competitionCompetition and rivalry raise the cost of doing business.
Bargaining power of buyersBuyers with strong bargaining power can extract price concessions.
Bargaining power of suppliersSuppliers with strong bargaining power can demand higher prices.
Threat of substitutionAs substitutes multiply, sellers have less power to raise prices or pass costs on to buyers.
Threat of entryNew entrants increase competition; companies must invest in technology and human capital to build barriers to entry and economies of scale.

These forces operate inside a broader environment that also includes stockholders, creditors, government and tax authorities, regulators, labor, investors, distribution channels, governance, global forces, and voters. Source: Porter, M. 1998. Competitive Strategy: Techniques for Analyzing Industries and Competitors.

ESG reporting

Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) reporting has become an integral part of financial reporting for many companies. Boards are paying more attention to governance and engaging more deeply on shareholder resolutions. Investors and other stakeholders are pressing for richer disclosure about operational, supply-chain, emissions, and sustainability risks, and about workplace environments and employee initiatives. The demand for comparable, reliable, and consistent ESG information has drawn regulatory attention.

The SEC’s climate-risk disclosure initiative focused on four areas:

  • The company’s climate-risk management processes
  • How identified climate risks would affect financial performance
  • How those risks are managed and mitigated
  • Any scenario analysis, transition plans, and publicly announced climate goals
Where things stand (as of September 2026)
The SEC adopted final climate-disclosure rules in March 2024, then stayed them in April 2024 pending litigation. In March 2025 the SEC stopped defending the rules in court, and in May 2026 it proposed rescinding them. U.S. public companies are not currently required to comply. Climate disclosure remains relevant through voluntary reporting, investor demand, and non-U.S. and state-level regimes.

LO 1-6 Financial Statement Data and Analytics (Appendix 1A)

Finding filings on SEC EDGAR

The SEC’s EDGAR database (www.sec.gov/edgar) provides free access to every public company’s filings. A typical search:

  • Go to www.sec.gov/edgar and use the company search — enter the company name or ticker (e.g., Apple or AAPL).
  • On the company’s filing page, filter by form type (e.g., 10-K for the annual report, 10-Q for quarterly reports).
  • Open the filing to view the full document, or use the interactive data view to see the financial statements as tables you can download to Excel.

Data analytics

Data analytics uses data to inform decisions through three activities: gather, organize, and analyze. There are four types:

TypeQuestionExample in financial analysis
DescriptiveWhat happened?Revenue grew 8% last year
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Growth came from higher prices, not more units
PredictiveWhat will happen?Forecast next year’s revenue and margins
PrescriptiveWhat should be done?Recommend buying, holding, or selling the stock

Data visualization

  • Data visualization displays numbers graphically — charts, figures, and bars — and is an effective way to convey meaning and trends.
  • The textbook opens each module with a visualization of the focus company or industry and uses visuals throughout.
  • End-of-chapter exercises use visualization and analytics tools to build your data-analytics skills.

LO 1-7 Accounting Principles and Governance (Appendix 1B)

The financial accounting environment

BodyRole
Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)Broad powers to regulate the issuance and trading of securities. Companies with more than $10 million in total assets and a class of equity held of record by 2,000 or more persons (or 500 or more non-accredited investors) — or that list on a U.S. exchange — must file periodic reports, including financial statements, available on EDGAR.
Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)Sets U.S. financial accounting standards: Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP).
Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB)Sets the auditing standards (GAAS) that auditors of public companies follow, and oversees those auditors.

The system in one sentence: companies prepare the four financial statements following FASB’s GAAP, and independent auditors examine them following PCAOB auditing standards — all under the SEC’s oversight.

The audit report

  • Financial statements of publicly traded companies must be audited by an independent audit firm.
  • The board of directors hires the auditors to review the statements and express an opinion on them.

A standard “clean” (unqualified) audit report is consistent across companies and makes these points:

  • The financial statements are management’s responsibility; the auditor’s responsibility is to express an opinion on them.
  • An audit involves sampling transactions, not examining every one.
  • The opinion provides reasonable assurance that the statements are free of material misstatement — not a guarantee.
  • Auditors review the accounting policies and estimates management used.
  • The statements present fairly, in all material respects, the company’s financial condition in conformity with GAAP.

Apple’s 2022 report, for example, states that the financial statements “present fairly, in all material respects, the financial position of Apple Inc. at September 24, 2022.”

Corporate governance and the audit committee

PartyRole in the governance structure
StockholdersElect the board of directors.
Board of directorsSets policy, hires executive managers, and appoints committees — including strategic, compensation, and audit committees. Oversees executive managers.
Audit committee / audit firmThe board (through its audit committee) appoints the audit firm, which reports to the board and the stockholders.
Executive managersRun the company day to day and prepare the financial statements. The company pays the audit fees.

Why this structure matters: the auditor is paid by the company but answers to the board and stockholders, not to the managers whose numbers it is checking. The audit committee is the safeguard for that independence.
