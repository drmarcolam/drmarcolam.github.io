LO 19-1 Traditional Product Costing Systems: Plantwide vs. Departmental Rates

In traditional cost accounting systems, manufacturing overhead is assigned to products using volume-based allocation bases such as direct labor hours or machine hours. While simple, volume-based allocation methods can severely distort product costs when products differ in volume, batch size, or complexity.

1. Single Plantwide Overhead Rates

A plantwide overhead rate allocates all factory overhead using a single company-wide driver (e.g., total direct labor hours). This method assumes that all products consume overhead resources in exact proportion to that single driver. Single plantwide rates are appropriate only when a company produces a single homogeneous product or when all products consume overhead resources identically.

2. Departmental Overhead Rates

Departmental overhead rates assign overhead costs to specific production departments (e.g., Mixing, Bottling) and allocate departmental costs to products using department-specific cost drivers (e.g., direct labor hours in Mixing, machine hours in Bottling). Departmental rates provide better cost accuracy than plantwide rates because they recognize differences in departmental overhead intensity. However, departmental rates still rely on volume-based drivers and fail to capture batch-level or product-level overhead activities.

LO 19-2 Activity-Based Costing (ABC) Concepts and Cost Hierarchies

Activity-Based Costing (ABC) is a refined costing methodology that assigns overhead costs to activity pools and reassigns activity costs to cost objects based on the actual consumption of activities. ABC operates on two core principles:

1. Resource Consumption: Activities perform work to satisfy customer needs, and performing activities consumes costly economic resources.

2. Activity Demand: Cost objects (products, services, customer channels) consume activities in measurable quantities.

The Manufacturing Cost Hierarchy

To establish cause-and-effect relationships between activities and costs, ABC classifies manufacturing activities into four distinct hierarchy levels:

Hierarchy LevelReason / Operational TriggerExamples of Activity Costs
1. Unit-Level ActivitiesPerformed each time a single unit of product is manufactured or sold.Raw materials, operating utilities for equipment, direct assembly labor, unit packaging, sales commissions.
2. Batch-Level ActivitiesPerformed each time a batch of goods is processed, regardless of batch size.Equipment setups, sales order processing, issuing work orders, moving batches between workstations, batch inspections.
3. Product-Level ActivitiesPerformed to support specific product lines, regardless of units or batches.Product design/R&D, product testing, maintaining specialized equipment, product line advertising/marketing.
4. Facility-Level ActivitiesPerformed to maintain general manufacturing capabilities and infrastructure.Plant manager salary, factory depreciation, building maintenance, property taxes, general plant administration.

Customer Cost Hierarchies for Merchandising and Sales Divisions

In addition to manufacturing, ABC extends to merchandising firms and sales divisions through customer cost hierarchies:

  • • Unit-Level Customer Costs: Costs incurred per unit of product sold to an individual customer.
  • • Order-Level Customer Costs: Costs triggered by processing, picking, packing, and shipping an individual customer order.
  • • Customer-Level Costs: Costs incurred to maintain relationships with individual accounts (e.g., sales visits, remote technical support, custom billing).
  • • Facility / Segment-Level Costs: Costs required to maintain general sales offices, regional market segments, or project infrastructure.

LO 19-3 ABC Product Costing Model & Cost Distortion Analysis

The ABC product costing model assigns indirect overhead costs through a two-stage allocation process:

Stage 1: Indirect resource costs (support services, engineering, building/grounds) are assigned to specific Activity Cost Pools (Receiving, Setup, Testing, Maintenance).

Stage 2: Activity costs are reassigned from pools to Cost Objects using activity drivers.

The ABC Activity Rate Formula
Cost per Unit of Activity = [ Total Cost of Activity Cost Pool ] ÷ [ Total Units of Cost Driver ] Example (Kellogg's Cereal Production): • Machine Setup Cost Pool = $27,525 across 300 total batches -> Activity Rate = $91.75 per setup batch. • If Frosted Flakes requires 10 setup batches -> Allocated Setup Overhead = 10 batches × $91.75 = $917.50.

LO 19-4 ABC Implementation Considerations, Challenges, and Limitations

While Activity-Based Costing provides vastly superior cost accuracy for internal management, transitioning from traditional costing to ABC involves significant organizational and economic tradeoffs.

Core Implementation Challenges

1. Measurement & System Expense: Designing and maintaining ABC systems requires interviewing staff, tracking multiple drivers, and maintaining complex software data feeds.

2. Dual System Maintenance: GAAP and IFRS require full absorption costing for external financial reporting and generally disallow ABC models that include nonmanufacturing costs or exclude facility-sustaining costs. Consequently, firms must maintain dual costing systems—traditional for external auditing and ABC for internal management.

3. Organizational Resistance & Buy-In: ABC reveals uncomfortable truths about product and customer profitability. Operational managers may resist ABC if it shows that their favored product lines or legacy customers are unprofitable.

4. Activity-Based Management (ABM) Execution: ABC data generates value only if executives take concrete operational actions—such as repricing products, redesigning processes, or discontinuing unprofitable customer accounts.

LO 19-5 ABC and Customer Profitability Analysis

Activity-Based Costing applies beyond product manufacturing to evaluate customer profitability. Not all sales revenue generates equal net profit—customers differ dramatically in their service requirements, order frequencies, customization demands, and technical support needs.

The 'Whale Curve' Customer Profitability Profile

When customers are ranked from most profitable to least profitable, cumulative customer profits typically form a 'Whale Curve' graph:

  • • Highly Profitable Peak: The top 20% of customers generate up to 150% or more of total company operating profit.
  • • Plateau Phase: The middle 60-70% of customers roughly break even, consuming services in proportion to their sales volume.
  • • Profit Erosion Tail: The bottom 10-20% of highly demanding, low-volume customers consume excessive activity resources, eroding total cumulative profits back down to reported company net income.

LO 19-6 Multi-Level Break-Even and CVP Profitability Analysis

Traditional CVP analysis assumes all overhead is either purely variable or strictly fixed relative to unit sales volume. Activity-Based Costing enhances CVP by incorporating multi-level cost drivers (unit-level, order-level, customer-level, and facility-level).

Multi-Level Break-Even Equation
Unit-Level Break-Even Sales ($) = [ Order-Level Costs + Customer-Level Costs + Facility-Level Costs ] ÷ [ Contribution Margin Ratio ] Case Study Example (Multi-Level Cost Structure): • Unit-Level Variable Cost = 60% of Sales Dollar (Contribution Margin Ratio = 1.00 - 0.60 = 40% or 0.40) • Order-Level Activity Costs = $100,000 • Customer-Level Activity Costs = $150,000 • Facility-Level Sustaining Costs = $430,000 • Total Non-Unit Activity Overhead = $100,000 + $150,000 + $430,000 = $680,000 Unit-Level Break-Even Dollar Sales Volume = $680,000 ÷ 0.40 = $1,700,000.00

Key terms

Essential terminology for mastering Activity-Based Costing, Customer Profitability, and Activity-Based Management:

TermDefinition
Activity-Based Costing (ABC)Costing method that assigns overhead costs to activity cost pools and reassigns them to cost objects based on activity driver consumption.
Cost ObjectAny product, service, customer, brand, or organizational segment for which cost data is accumulated.
Activity Cost PoolA bucket in which costs associated with a specific operational activity or task are accumulated.
Activity Cost DriverA measure of activity frequency or intensity used to assign activity pool costs to cost objects.
Unit-Level ActivitiesActivities performed each time a single unit of product or service is manufactured or delivered.
Batch-Level ActivitiesActivities performed each time a batch of goods is processed, regardless of batch size.
Product-Level ActivitiesActivities performed to support specific product lines, regardless of unit or batch volume.
Facility-Level ActivitiesActivities performed to maintain general manufacturing capabilities and administrative infrastructure.
Customer Cost HierarchyA scheme categorizing activities performed to serve customers into unit, order, customer, and facility levels.
Product Cost DistortionMisallocation of overhead under traditional volume-based systems where high-volume products subsidize low-volume products.
Plantwide Overhead RateA single overhead allocation rate used across an entire facility based on a single volume measure.
Departmental Overhead RateAn overhead allocation rate established for a specific department using department-specific drivers.
Activity-Based Management (ABM)Management approach using ABC cost data to streamline operations, reprice products, and eliminate non-value-added activities.
Customer Profitability AnalysisEvaluation of gross revenue minus cost of goods sold and dedicated customer activity costs to assess account profitability.
Whale CurveA cumulative customer profitability graph showing peak profits generated by top accounts before declining due to unprofitable accounts.
Multi-Level Break-EvenCVP break-even model incorporating order, customer, and facility activity costs into the numerator divided by CM ratio.
