LO 17-1 Relevant versus Irrelevant Revenues and Costs

Managerial decision-making involves selecting from among competing courses of action based on their relative financial and strategic merits. To arrive at optimal decisions, management accountants isolate relevant revenues and costs from irrelevant figures:

Relevant Costs and Revenues: Future revenues and costs that differ among decision alternatives. To be relevant, a cost or revenue must satisfy two criteria simultaneously: (1) it must be incurred in the future, and (2) it must differ between the choices being evaluated.

Irrelevant Costs and Revenues: Revenues and costs that do not differ among decision alternatives, or costs that have already been incurred in past transactions.

Sunk Costs: Past costs that have already been incurred and cannot be changed or avoided by any present or future decision. Sunk costs are NEVER relevant in financial decisions. Example: The original acquisition price and accumulated depreciation of existing machinery.

Opportunity Costs: The potential economic benefit forgone or sacrificed when selecting one alternative over a competing choice. Opportunity costs do not appear on traditional financial accounting ledgers, but they are ALWAYS relevant in managerial decision analysis.

Disposal and Salvage Values: Disposal value represents the immediate cash inflow obtained from selling an existing asset at the time a replacement is purchased. Salvage value represents the expected cash realization at the end of an asset's useful life. Cash inflows from asset disposal are relevant only if the replacement alternative is accepted.

Managerial Behavior Warning: Managers frequently hesitate to dispose of obsolete machinery or inventory because writing off the asset creates a loss on the accounting income statement, making their short-term operational performance look unfavorable even when replacement is economically optimal.

LO 17-2 Differential Analysis & Segment Drop Decisions

Differential analysis focuses exclusively on revenues and costs that differ among alternative options. By filtering out irrelevant common overhead and sunk costs, differential analysis simplifies complex management decisions.

Case Study: Evaluating Profit Plan Changes (Computer Adapter Line)

Suppose HP manufactures computer adapters selling for $20.00 each. Normal monthly volume is 4,000 units. Cost structure: Variable costs per unit = $10.00 ($3 Direct Materials + $4 Direct Labor + $2 Variable Overhead + $1 Variable S&A). Monthly fixed costs = $28,000 ($20,000 Fixed Overhead + $8,000 Fixed S&A). Unit Contribution Margin = $20.00 - $10.00 = $10.00.

Alternative #1 (Increase Advertising): Increase monthly advertising by $1,000, boosting sales volume by 300 units. Incremental CM = 300 units × $10 = +$3,000. Less $1,000 advertising = Net Monthly Profit Increase of +$2,000. (BEST OPTION)

Alternative #2 (Increase Selling Price): Raise selling price by $1.00 (to $21.00), causing sales volume to drop by 300 units (to 3,700 units). Lost CM from reduced volume = 300 units × $10 = -$3,000. Added revenue on remaining volume = 3,700 units × $1.00 = +$3,700. Net Monthly Profit Increase of +$700.

Alternative #3 (Decrease Selling Price & Use Overtime): Lower selling price by $1.00 (to $19.00), increasing volume by 500 units (to 4,500 units), where the last 200 units require direct labor overtime costing $1.00 extra per unit. CM on 500 new units at $19 price ($19 - $10 = $9 CM) = 500 × $9 = +$4,500. Less $1.00 price reduction on regular 4,000 units = -$4,000. Less overtime penalty on 200 units = -$200. Net Monthly Profit Increase of +$300.

Decision Rule for Discontinuing Operations / Segment Drops: A product line or business segment should be discontinued ONLY if its lost contribution margin is LESS than the fixed costs that can be completely eliminated. If HP drops the adapter line entirely, it loses $40,000 in monthly contribution margin (4,000 units × $10 CM). Unless $40,000 of fixed costs are eliminated, dropping the segment creates a $40,000 monthly profit disadvantage.

LO 17-3 Special Orders, Multiyear Contracts, and Capacity Constraints

A special order is a one-time order from a customer outside regular distribution channels. When evaluating special orders, management must analyze incremental revenues, incremental costs, and capacity availability:

Baseline Special Order Evaluation: Suppose a customer offers HP a one-time order for 800 adapters at $16.00 per unit (below normal $20.00 price). HP has excess capacity (capacity = 5,000 units; normal production = 4,200 units). HP will save 25% of its variable S&A expenses ($0.75 per unit instead of $1.00). Incremental Variable Cost = $3 DM + $4 DL + $2 VOH + $0.75 Var S&A = $9.75. Incremental CM per unit = $16.00 - $9.75 = +$6.25 per unit. Total Net Profit Increase = 800 units × $6.25 = +$5,000.00. (ACCEPT ORDER)

Multiyear Special Orders: In multiyear contracts, fixed costs lose their fixed behavior in the long run. Management must evaluate Full Cost per unit ($15.35: $9.75 variable + $4.00 fixed MOH + $1.60 fixed S&A) or include cost escalation clauses. Because the $16.00 special price exceeds full cost ($15.35), the multiyear order remains profitable long-term.

Special Orders at Full Capacity (Opportunity Costs): If HP is operating at full 5,000 unit capacity, accepting 800 special order units requires sacrificing 800 regular sales @ $20.00 price ($10.00 regular CM). Opportunity Cost of lost sales = 800 × $10.00 = -$8,000. Incremental CM from special order = +$5,000. Net Profit Impact = -$3,000 Profit Decrease. (REJECT ORDER)

LO 17-4 Outsourcing (Make-or-Buy) Decisions & ESG Considerations

Outsourcing is the procurement of products, services, or components from an external supplier rather than producing them internally.

Qualitative & ESG Considerations (Scope 3 Emissions): Outsourcing decisions must incorporate Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) evaluations. Scope 3 emission reporting standards require firms to disclose greenhouse gas emissions occurring across vendor supply chains. Partnering with non-compliant suppliers creates reputational and regulatory liabilities.

LO 17-5 Sell or Process Further Decisions

A sell or process further decision evaluates whether a product should be sold 'as is' at split-off or processed further into a refined end-product. Decision Rule: Process further ONLY if incremental revenue exceeds incremental processing costs. Allocated joint costs prior to split-off are SUNK and IRRELEVANT.

Case Study: The GAP Custom T-Shirts

The GAP manufactures plain white t-shirts costing $18.00 each and selling for $30.00 each. Tie-dying the shirts increases total cost to $23.00 (incremental cost = $23.00 - $18.00 = +$5.00), but increases selling price to $38.00 (incremental revenue = $38.00 - $30.00 = +$8.00). Incremental Advantage = $8.00 - $5.00 = +$3.00 Profit Advantage per shirt. (PROCESS FURTHER)

LO 17-6 Optimization of Constrained Resources

When demand exceeds production capacity, management operates under constrained resource bottlenecks (e.g., raw materials, machine hours, labor). Decision Rule: Rank and produce products based on Contribution Margin per Unit of Constrained Resource.

Case Study: Wilson Sports Baseball Bats (White Ash Wood Constraint)

Wilson Sports has 24,000 board feet of white ash wood available due to weather shortages:

Deluxe Bats: Selling Price = $75, Variable Cost = $34, Unit CM = $41.00. Wood required = 3.40 board feet per bat. CM per Board Foot = $41.00 ÷ 3.40 = $12.06 per board foot.

Basic Bats: Selling Price = $54, Variable Cost = $25, Unit CM = $29.00. Wood required = 2.20 board feet per bat. CM per Board Foot = $29.00 ÷ 2.20 = $13.18 per board foot.

Single Constraint Optimization: Produce Basic Bats because $13.18 > $12.06 per board foot.

LO 17-7 Theory of Constraints (TOC) & Performance Reporting

The Theory of Constraints (TOC) posits that every operational process has a bottleneck constraint, and total output cannot exceed throughput at the bottleneck. Throughput = Sales Revenue minus Direct Materials Cost.

Management Bottleneck Obligations: (1) Identify operational bottlenecks; (2) Schedule production to maximize bottleneck efficiency; (3) Prevent inventory accumulation ahead of non-bottleneck stations; and (4) Elevate and eliminate the bottleneck constraint.

TOC Performance Reporting Rules: Performance reports should measure bottleneck resource utilization and total factory throughput. Reports MUST NOT encourage 100% utilization of non-bottleneck resources, as doing so generates excessive work-in-process inventory.
