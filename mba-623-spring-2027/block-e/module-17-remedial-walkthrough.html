1. Relevant and irrelevant costs

Nora Crisp has three decisions on her desk. In each one, only some of the numbers matter. Relevance sorts them.

Key ideas
  • A cost or revenue is relevant if it will be incurred in the future and differs between the alternatives.
  • An irrelevant cost is the same under every alternative, or it has already been incurred.
  • Differential analysis compares the alternatives by looking only at what differs. It uses fewer line items and keeps the decision simple.
Worked example: Cullowhee

The fleet order from Western Carolina Courier Co.:

ItemRelevance
Variable cost of the bikes in the orderRelevant: changes only if Cullowhee accepts
Racks added to this orderRelevant: extra cost of the order
One-time packaging and tooling ($6,000)Relevant: incurred only if Cullowhee accepts
Plant depreciation and supervisor salariesIrrelevant: the same either way
What Cullowhee paid for its assembly equipment in 2024Irrelevant: already paid (sunk)

2. Sunk costs, opportunity costs and disposal values

Some of the most tempting numbers are sunk. Some of the most important ones never show up in the ledger.

Key ideas
  • A sunk cost is a past cost that no decision can change. It is never relevant, including the book value of an old asset.
  • An opportunity cost is the benefit given up by choosing one alternative over another. It is always relevant, even though it is not recorded.
  • Cash from disposing of an old asset is relevant only if the replacement alternative is chosen. Managers sometimes avoid replacing an asset because the write-off shows a loss; the loss is not a reason.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Should Cullowhee replace its paint line?

ItemTreatment
Price paid three years ago: $340,000Sunk: irrelevant
Book value today: $50,000Sunk: irrelevant
Cash from selling the old line: $25,000Relevant: received only if Cullowhee replaces it
Annual operating savings from the new line: $60,000Relevant: differs between the alternatives

3. Special order with idle capacity

Western Carolina Courier Co. will pay $650 per Ridge Commuter, well under the $800 regular price. The plant has idle capacity.

Key ideas
  • With idle capacity, the order does not displace regular sales. Existing fixed costs do not change, so they are irrelevant.
  • Relevant to the order: price, the order's variable cost, and any one-time cost that exists only because of the order.
  • Accept if the order's total contribution margin exceeds its one-time costs. Full cost per bike is misleading because it contains fixed costs that do not change.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Fleet order, 600 Ridge Commuter bikes:

ItemAmount
Price per bike$650
Variable cost per bike (520 + 18 for racks)$538
Contribution margin per bike$112
Total contribution margin (600 × $112)$67,200
Less one-time packaging and tooling($6,000)
Increase in operating income$61,200

4. Special order: the price floor and full capacity

Nora wants to know how low the price can go, and what changes if the plant is busy.

Key ideas
  • The minimum price that adds nothing to profit = variable cost per unit + one-time cost ÷ units.
  • At full capacity, every special-order unit displaces a regular sale. The contribution margin of the lost sale is an opportunity cost.
  • Compare the special order's contribution margin per unit with the regular contribution margin per unit. If the special margin is lower, the order reduces profit.
Worked example: Cullowhee

The same order, two situations:

ItemAmount
Price floor with idle capacity: 538 + 6,000 ÷ 600$548 per bike
Contribution margin per bike on the order$112
Contribution margin per bike on a regular sale (800 − 520)$280
If the plant is full: change per bike (112 − 280)($168)
If the plant is full: 600 bikes × ($168)($100,800)

5. Drop or keep a segment

Trail Cub shows a negative segment margin. Does that mean Cullowhee should drop it?

Key ideas
  • Dropping a segment gives up its contribution margin and saves only its avoidable fixed costs.
  • Drop only if the avoidable fixed costs exceed the lost contribution margin.
  • Unavoidable and common costs continue whether or not the line is dropped, so they are irrelevant. They are often shifted to the remaining lines.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Trail Cub, FY2026 ($ thousand):

ItemAmount
Contribution margin1,700
Traceable fixed costs(2,100)
Segment margin(400)
Fixed costs avoided if dropped1,400
Contribution margin lost if dropped(1,700)
Change in operating income if dropped(300)

Trail Cub looks like a loser, but dropping it would reduce operating income by $300 thousand. Only $1,400 thousand of its $2,100 thousand of fixed cost goes away.

6. Make or buy, and sell or process further

Nora also asks whether to keep making battery packs, and whether finishing a product is worth the extra cost.

Key ideas
  • Make or buy: compare the supplier's price with the cost Cullowhee would avoid by buying: variable cost plus avoidable fixed cost. Weigh quality, reliability and ESG factors such as Scope 3 emissions.
  • Sell or process further: process further only if incremental revenue exceeds incremental processing cost.
  • Costs already incurred before the split-off point are sunk, however they are allocated.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Battery packs: 11,000 a year.

ItemAmount
Cost to make: 400 × 11,000 + 600,000$5,000,000
Cost to buy: 432 × 11,000$4,752,000
Savings from buying$248,000

Relevant cost per pack to make: 400 + 600,000 ÷ 11,000 = $454.55, compared with the $432 quote.

Finishing a Hemlock frame: sells for $250 unfinished or $320 powder-coated. Finishing costs $40. Change = 320 − 250 − 40 = +$30 per frame. The cost of building the frame is the same either way.

7. Constrained resources and the bottleneck

When the paint booth is the limit, the question changes from which bike earns the most to which bike earns the most per booth hour.

Key ideas
  • With one scarce resource, rank products by contribution margin per unit of the scarce resource.
  • The theory of constraints: output cannot exceed the throughput of the bottleneck. Identify it, schedule around it, avoid building inventory ahead of it, and work to elevate it.
  • Throughput = sales revenue − direct materials cost. Do not push non-bottleneck stations to 100% utilization.
Worked example: Cullowhee

The paint booth limits output:

ProductCM per bikeBooth hoursCM per booth hour
Ridge Commuter2802$140

The Balsam E1 earns more per bike, but the Ridge Commuter earns more per booth hour. When the booth is the limit, make Ridge Commuters first.
