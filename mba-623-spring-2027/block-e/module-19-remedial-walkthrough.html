1. One plantwide overhead rate

Laurel Ammons wants to know whether Hemlock Custom is really profitable. Start with how overhead is assigned today.

Key ideas
  • A plantwide rate = total manufacturing overhead ÷ total of one activity base, such as direct labor hours.
  • Overhead assigned = rate × the hours the product uses.
  • It is accurate only if every product uses overhead in proportion to that one base. Products that differ in volume, batch size or complexity are distorted.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee FY2026: $7,500 thousand of overhead; 205,000 direct labor hours.

ItemResult
Plantwide rate: 7,500,000 ÷ 205,000$36.59 per hour
Hemlock Custom: 40 hours per bike × 36.59$1,463 per bike
Trail Cub: 1.5 hours per bike × 36.59$55 per bike

2. The cost hierarchy

To see what Hemlock really consumes, sort the overhead by what causes it.

Key ideas
  • Unit-level activities occur for every unit. Batch-level occur for every batch, whatever its size.
  • Product-level activities support a product line regardless of volume. Facility-level activities keep the plant running.
  • A customer cost hierarchy has the same logic: unit, order, customer and facility levels.

3. Activity rates and assigning overhead

ABC gives each pool its own rate and assigns cost by the activity each product actually uses.

Key ideas
  • Activity rate = pool cost ÷ total driver quantity.
  • Overhead assigned to a product = rate × the driver quantity the product uses. Add across all pools.
  • Stage 1 assigns resource costs to activity pools; stage 2 assigns pool costs to products with the activity drivers.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee machine setups: $1,900 thousand over 420 setups.

ItemResult
Setup rate: 1,900,000 ÷ 420$4,523.81 per setup
Hemlock Custom: 260 setups × 4,523.81$1,176,190
Engineering change order rate: 1,600,000 ÷ 280$5,714.29 per order
Hemlock Custom: 160 orders × 5,714.29$914,286

4. What ABC changes

Total overhead is still $7,500 thousand. ABC changes who bears it.

Key ideas
  • Under ABC, products that use many batch- and product-level activities (Hemlock Custom) receive more overhead.
  • High-volume, simple products (Ridge Commuter) receive less. Under the plantwide rate they were subsidizing the complex line.
  • Total overhead and total profit do not change. The pattern of reported product profit does.

5. Limits of ABC and activity-based management

Wade is nervous that ABC will be used to kill his favorite line. The honest answer is that ABC is a tool, not a verdict.

Key ideas
  • ABC is costly to design and maintain, and the results can be resisted by managers who see their products look worse.
  • GAAP requires full absorption costing for external statements, so most firms keep two systems: one for reporting, ABC for internal decisions.
  • Activity-based management acts on the data: reprice, redesign processes, reduce non-value-added activities. Check which costs are avoidable before dropping anything.
Worked example: Cullowhee

6. Cost to serve a channel or customer

Direct online has the best gross margin. Does it still look best once the cost of serving it is counted?

Key ideas
  • Cost to serve = sum of (activity rate × activity used) across order processing, shipping, marketing, billing and returns.
  • Gross margin ignores these costs, so a customer with a high gross margin can be expensive to serve.
  • Compare cost to serve as a percent of net sales across channels.

7. Customer profitability and multi-level break-even

Laurel needs one number per channel and a way to see how many sales an account needs to pay for itself.

Key ideas
  • Profit after cost to serve = gross profit − cost to serve. Rank channels or customers by this, not by gross margin.
  • A multi-level statement separates unit-level, order-level, customer-level and facility-level costs.
  • Multi-level break-even sales = (order-level + customer-level + facility-level costs) ÷ contribution margin ratio.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Channel profit after cost to serve ($ thousand):

ChannelGross profitCost to serveProfit after cost to serve% of net sales
