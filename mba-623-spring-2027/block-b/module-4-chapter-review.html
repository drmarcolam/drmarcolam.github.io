LO 4-1 Return on Equity (ROE)

  • ROE is the most common analysis metric used by managers and investors alike.
  • It relates net income to average total stockholders’ equity and measures return from the stockholders’ perspective.
ROE = Net income ÷ Average stockholders’ equity

Pfizer, 2022: ROE = $31,372 ÷ [($95,661 + $77,201) ÷ 2] = $31,372 ÷ $86,431 = 36.3%. For comparison, ROE for S&P 500 companies ranged from 13.7% to 17.3% over 2018–2022.

LO 4-2 DuPont Disaggregation: ROA and Financial Leverage

There are two ways to measure the drivers of ROE: (1) traditional DuPont analysis, which splits ROE into profitability, productivity, and leverage; and (2) operating-focus analysis, which separates operating from nonoperating activities (LOs 4-4 to 4-8).

DuPont disaggregation
ROE = ROA × Financial leverage (FL) × NCIR
= (Net income ÷ Avg. total assets) × (Avg. total assets ÷ Avg. stockholders’ equity) × NCIR
NCIR = 1 for companies with no noncontrolling interest.
  • ROE reflects both company performance (ROA) and how the assets are financed (FL).
  • For a given level of assets, ROE is higher with more debt and less equity — but more debt means more risk.

Return on assets (ROA)

ROA = Net income ÷ Average total assets
  • ROA measures return from the perspective of the entire company (the enterprise level).
  • The numerator comes from the income statement and the denominator from the balance sheet, so a high ROA requires being profitable and managing assets — ROA pushes managers to focus on both statements.

Pfizer: ROA = $31,407 ÷ [($197,205 + $181,476) ÷ 2] = $31,407 ÷ $189,341 = 16.6%. The median S&P 500 ROA was 6.1% in 2022 (range 4.4%–6.4% over 2018–2022).

Financial leverage (FL)

FL = Average total assets ÷ Average stockholders’ equity
  • FL measures the degree to which the company finances its assets with debt versus equity. (There are many leverage measures; this one is FL. LO 4-8 introduces FLEV.)
  • Leverage matters because debt is a contractual obligation — failing to pay interest or principal has legal consequences. All else equal, higher leverage raises the probability of default and bankruptcy.

Pfizer: FL = $189,341 ÷ [($95,916 + $77,462) ÷ 2] = $189,341 ÷ $86,689 = 2.18. The median S&P 500 FL was about 2.5–2.6 over 2018–2022.

Noncontrolling interest ratio (NCIR)

Most S&P 500 companies report some noncontrolling interest. Because ROE focuses on the company’s common shareholders, a third term adjusts for the two shareholder groups:

NCIR = (NI attributable to common shareholders ÷ Net income) ÷ (Avg. equity attributable to common shareholders ÷ Avg. total equity)

Pfizer: NCIR = ($31,372 ÷ $31,407) ÷ ($86,431 ÷ $86,689) = 1.00188.

Pfizer’s DuPont check
ROE = 16.59% × 2.184 × 1.00188 = 36.3%
Because most companies have NCIR close to 1, ROE ≈ ROA × FL is usually a good approximation.

LO 4-3 Disaggregating ROA: Profitability and Productivity

ROA = Profit margin (PM) × Asset turnover (AT)
= (Net income ÷ Sales) × (Sales ÷ Average total assets)
  • Profit margin — what the company earns on each sales dollar.
  • Asset turnover — the sales generated from each dollar invested in assets.
  • Managers raise ROA by increasing PM (more profit for a given level of assets), increasing AT (fewer assets for the same profit), or both.

Pfizer: PM = $31,407 ÷ $100,330 = 31.3%; AT = $100,330 ÷ $189,341 = 0.53; ROA = 31.3% × 0.53 = 16.6%.

Adjusted ROA
Operating managers control ROA, but not the capital structure. Analysts therefore often add back the after-tax net interest expense (net of interest revenue and other nonoperating items) to the numerator:
Adjusted ROA = [Net income + Net interest expense × (1 − Statutory tax rate)] ÷ Average total assets
Pfizer: [$31,407 + ($987 − $997) × (1 − 0.22)] ÷ $189,340.5 = $31,399.2 ÷ $189,340.5 = 16.58%. The statutory rate (22%) is the assumed combined federal and state rate. The adjusted numerator approximates NOPAT (LO 4-5).

Splitting ROE into ROA and FL is the first level. The second level looks for the factors behind profitability and productivity. ROE increases with each component.

The cash conversion cycle is the average number of days to buy inventory on credit, sell it on credit, collect the receivables, and pay the suppliers. Companies generally prefer a lower CCC, because the operating cycle then generates profit and cash quickly. Analyze the CCC over time and against peers with similar business models. The S&P 500 CCC has ranged from roughly 47 to 53 days over 2013–2022.

A company’s CCC depends on its business model: the credit terms it offers customers, the kinds of inventory it carries and the depth and breadth of its product lines, and how quickly it pays suppliers. Median CCC is shortest in energy and longest in healthcare (over 100 days).

Analyzing productivity: PP&E

PP&E turnover = Sales ÷ Average PP&E. (Pfizer: $100,330 ÷ [($16,274 + $14,882) ÷ 2] = 6.4 times.) Improving PP&E turnover is not easy; it often involves:

  • Divesting unproductive assets or entire business lines
  • Joint ventures to share PP&E such as distribution networks, IT, production facilities, and warehouses
  • Selling production facilities while agreeing to buy finished goods from the new owners
  • Sale and leaseback of administrative buildings

Analyzing financial leverage

  • Used judiciously, leverage benefits stockholders — debt is a relatively inexpensive source of capital — but it adds risk, because repayment is mandatory.
  • Leverage analysis typically examines (1) the level of borrowed money relative to equity (e.g., total debt-to-equity) and (2) the level of profit or cash flow relative to required debt payments (e.g., times interest earned).
When equity is near zero or negative
Stock buybacks have grown dramatically, leaving many balance sheets with large treasury stock or very low retained earnings. A small equity balance in the denominator inflates ROE.
Apple, 2018–2022: net income totaled $366.7 billion while buybacks totaled $409.1 billion. Buybacks plus a large negative AOCI balance cut stockholders’ equity from $107.1 billion to $50.7 billion, lifting ROE from 49% to 176%.
Analysts respond by using measures less sensitive to small equity: a ROA focus or a financial leverage focus.

LO 4-4 Net Operating Assets (NOA)

Why an operating focus?

  • Companies create value mainly through their core operations, but both the balance sheet and income statement mix operating and nonoperating items.
  • Analysis improves when we separate the operating and nonoperating components and measure their returns separately:
ROE = Operating return + Nonoperating return
Operating return comes from sales, expenses, and so on; nonoperating return comes from financing and investing activities.

In DuPont analysis, FL (Total assets ÷ Equity) lumps all liabilities together. The operating approach splits them:

Classifying leases

  • Many companies lease buildings and equipment rather than buy them. Economically, leasing is no different from buying the asset with borrowed money.
  • So the right-of-use asset is an operating asset, and the related lease obligation is a nonoperating liability.
  • The challenge: some balance sheets do not report lease items separately, so analysts must dig into the notes.

Step 1 — Pfizer’s NOPBT

NOPBT = Revenues − Cost of sales − Operating expenses = $100,330 − $34,344 − $31,267 = $34,719, where operating expenses = SI&A $13,677 + R&D $11,428 + acquired IPR&D $953 + amortization $3,609 + restructuring $1,375 + other operating expense $225.

Step 2 — Tax on operating profit

Tax on operating profit = Tax expense + (Pretax net nonoperating expense × Statutory tax rate)
  • The term in parentheses is the tax shield — the taxes a company saves because nonoperating expenses (like interest) are tax-deductible.
  • Those savings do not relate to operating profit, so we add the tax shield back to total tax expense to get the tax on operating profit.

Pfizer: pretax net nonoperating expense = nonoperating expenses − nonoperating income = $987 − $997 = $(10) — a small net nonoperating income. Using a 22% statutory rate:

Typical nonoperating income statement items for nonfinancial companies: interest expense; gains and losses on debt retirement; interest and dividend revenue; investment gains and losses; the tax shield on net nonoperating expense; and income (loss) from discontinued operations, net of tax. LO 4-6 Return on Net Operating Assets (RNOA)

RNOA = NOPAT ÷ Average NOA
Average NOA = (NOA at start of year + NOA at end of year) ÷ 2

Pfizer: RNOA = $31,393 ÷ [($110,498 + $84,983) ÷ 2] = $31,393 ÷ $97,741 = 32.1%. The median S&P 500 RNOA was 13.5% in 2022 (range 10.2%–13.5% over 2018–2022).

The margin–turnover trade-off

Across industries, companies earn similar RNOAs through very different combinations. The textbook plots industries around an RNOA curve of 14.9%:

  • High turnover, low margin — consumer staples (turnover near 4.0) and consumer discretionary (about 2.5).
  • High margin, lower turnover — pharmaceuticals and biotech, oil and gas, and semiconductors (margins of roughly 20%–27%).
  • Industries such as telecommunications, with low turnover and only moderate margins, fall below the curve.

  • Apple’s operating assets earn an extraordinary 864.89% — its NOA is tiny because operating liabilities (like its large accounts payable) nearly offset its operating assets.
  • But ROE < RNOA: Apple holds so many marketable securities (nonoperating assets) that its NNO is negative (net financial assets), so FLEV is negative (about −0.80) and the nonoperating return reduces ROE.

Noncontrolling interest

  • Noncontrolling interest arises when a parent owns less than 100% of a subsidiary’s stock.
  • NCIR measures the proportion of net income and equity attributable to the parent’s stockholders (the controlling interest): (NI attributable to controlling interest ÷ NI) ÷ (Avg. equity attributable to controlling interest ÷ Avg. total equity).

LO 4-9 Vertical and Horizontal Analysis (Appendix 4C)

AnalysisWhat it does
Vertical analysisExpresses the statements in ratio form — income statement items as a percent of net sales, balance sheet items as a percent of total assets. These common-size statements allow comparisons across companies of different sizes and among accounts within one set of statements.
Pfizer: cost of sales = $34,344 ÷ $100,330 = 34.2% of revenues.
Horizontal analysisExamines financial data across time — comparing vertical analyses over time, or expressing each item as a year-over-year percent change:
(Current balance − Previous balance) ÷ Previous balance
Pfizer: revenues grew ($100,330 − $81,288) ÷ $81,288 = 23.4%; net income attributable to shareholders grew ($31,372 − $21,979) ÷ $21,979 = 42.7%.

Limitations of ratio analysis

Analyzing numbers blindly can lead to faulty conclusions and poor decisions. Keep these limits in mind:

LimitationWhy it matters
GAAP limitationsCurrent accounting rules omit many assets (e.g., internally developed intangibles).
Company changesMergers, divestitures, and strategy changes impair comparability of a company’s ratios over time.
Conglomerate effectsFew companies are pure plays; consolidated statements that combine different businesses are hard to analyze.
Fuzzy viewRatios reduce the complexity of a company’s operations to a single number; no single number captures all qualitative aspects of a business.
