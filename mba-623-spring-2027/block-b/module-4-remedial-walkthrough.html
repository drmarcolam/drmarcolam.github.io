1. Return on equity (ROE)

Walt Parris wants to know why ROE fell. First, measure it the way investors do.

Key ideas
  • ROE = net income ÷ average stockholders’ equity. It measures the return from the owners’ point of view.
  • Use the average of beginning and ending equity, because equity changes during the year.
  • Compare ROE across years and with peers. A fall in ROE is a signal to ask which driver changed.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee ($ thousand):

YearNet incomeAverage equityROE
FY20253,02718,975.516.0%
FY20262,67925,858.510.4%

Average equity for FY2026 = (19,849 + 31,868) ÷ 2 = 25,858.5. ROE fell by about 5.6 percentage points, even though net sales grew 18.7%.

2. DuPont analysis: ROE = ROA × financial leverage

ROE blends how well the company performs with how much it borrows. The DuPont analysis separates the two.

Key ideas
  • ROA = net income ÷ average total assets. Financial leverage = average total assets ÷ average equity.
  • ROE = ROA × financial leverage.
  • Higher leverage can raise ROE when assets earn more than the cost of debt, but it also raises risk, because debt payments are mandatory.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee ($ thousand):

YearROAFinancial leverageROE
FY20258.7%1.8316.0%
FY20265.7%1.8310.4%

FY2026 leverage = 47,415.5 ÷ 25,858.5 = 1.83. Leverage did not change, so the whole fall in ROE came from ROA.

3. ROA = profit margin × asset turnover

ROA itself splits into profitability and productivity: how much of each sale is profit, and how hard the assets work.

Key ideas
  • Profit margin = net income ÷ net sales. Asset turnover = net sales ÷ average total assets.
  • ROA = profit margin × asset turnover.
  • A thin margin can be offset by high turnover, and the reverse. Compare a company with a peer on both parts.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee and Ridgeline Bikes, FY2026:

CompanyProfit marginAsset turnoverROA
Cullowhee4.9%1.165.7%
Ridgeline Bikes6.2%1.328.1%

Ridgeline earns more on each sale and gets more sales from each asset dollar, so its ROA is higher on both counts.

4. Why ROE fell when sales grew

Nora’s question is the heart of the module. Trace the drop back to the income statement and the balance sheet.

Key ideas
  • Profitability: gross profit margin (gross profit ÷ sales) and operating expense margin show whether growth turned into profit.
  • Productivity: assets that grow faster than sales lower asset turnover. Here, the new plant, the acquisition and a large cash balance enlarged assets.
  • A larger equity base (the April share sale) lowers ROE when income does not grow with it.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee ($ thousand):

MeasureFY2025FY2026
Gross profit margin34.5%32.9%
Operating margin9.8%7.7%
Asset turnover1.341.16
Net sales growth22.4%18.7%

Battery and parts costs rose faster than prices, R&D and SG&A grew with sales, and assets grew faster than sales. Margin and turnover both fell.

5. Operating and nonoperating items, and net operating assets (NOA)

Cash from the share sale and bond issue is not part of what makes bikes. An operating focus separates the two.

Key ideas
  • Operating items arise from making and selling products: receivables, inventories, property and equipment, intangibles, payables, accrued liabilities, warranty and deferred revenue.
  • Nonoperating items come from financing and investing: cash and investments, and interest-bearing debt.
  • NOA = operating assets − operating liabilities. Equivalent: equity + interest-bearing debt − cash.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee, December 31, 2026 ($ thousand):

Category$ thousand
Operating assets (58,282 − cash 12,992)45,290
Operating liabilities (payables, accruals, warranty, deferred revenue)11,600
Net operating assets33,690

Check: equity 31,868 + debt (1,000 + 4,500 + 9,314 = 14,814) − cash 12,992 = 33,690. NOA at December 31, 2025 was 22,360.

6. NOPAT and return on net operating assets (RNOA)

RNOA measures what the operations earn, without the effect of cash and borrowing.

Key ideas
  • NOPAT = operating profit after tax. Interest and other nonoperating items are left out, and the tax on operating profit excludes the tax shield from interest.
  • RNOA = NOPAT ÷ average NOA = net operating profit margin × net operating asset turnover.
  • The lecture compares RNOA with the median for S&P 500 firms (13.5% in 2022).
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee ($ thousand), tax rate 25%:

YearNOPATAverage NOARNOA
FY20253,40520,52016.6%
FY20263,187.528,02511.4%

FY2026 NOPAT = operating income 4,250 × (1 − 25%). Net operating profit margin = 3,187.5 ÷ 55,200 = 5.77%; net operating asset turnover = 55,200 ÷ 28,025 = 1.97.

7. Common-size statements and horizontal analysis

Percentages let you compare a company with peers of different size and with itself over time.

Key ideas
  • Vertical (common-size) analysis: express each line as a percent of net sales (income statement) or total assets (balance sheet).
  • Horizontal analysis: growth = (this year − last year) ÷ last year.
  • Ratios have limits: accounting rules omit many assets, mergers change comparability, and one number cannot capture everything.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee FY2026: R&D of 2,300 ÷ net sales of 55,200 = 4.2% of net sales, up from 4.0% in FY2025 (1,860 ÷ 46,500). Net sales growth = (55,200 − 46,500) ÷ 46,500 = 18.7%.
