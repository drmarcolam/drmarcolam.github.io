LO 6-1 Inventory Costing Methods

Flow of costs

  • When inventory is purchased or produced, it is capitalized on the balance sheet.
  • When the inventory is sold, its cost is transferred from the balance sheet to the income statement as COGS.

Capitalization of inventory cost

  • Capitalization means that a cost is recorded on the balance sheet and is not immediately expensed in the income statement.
  • For purchased inventories, the amount capitalized is the purchase price.
  • For manufactured inventory, we capitalize all manufacturing costs, which consist of three components:
  • Cost of direct (raw) materials used in the product
  • Cost of direct labor to manufacture the product
  • Manufacturing overhead

Cost of goods sold

Beginning inventory + Inventory acquired = Cost of goods available for sale
Cost of goods available for sale = Ending inventory + Cost of goods sold
  • Beginning inventory is the ending inventory balance of the prior period.
  • Current inventory purchases (or manufacturing costs) are added to the beginning balance, yielding the cost of goods available for sale.
  • Goods available are either sold and flow to COGS, or the goods remain unsold and are in inventory at the end of the period.

Gross profit and managerial choice

Sales − Cost of goods sold = Gross profit
  • If higher cost units are transferred from the balance sheet, then COGS is higher and gross profit is lower.
  • If lower cost units are transferred to COGS, gross profit is higher.
  • Companies have a choice when it comes to determining COGS and the cost of the inventory remaining on the balance sheet.
  • Three choices for inventory costing: FIFO, LIFO, and average cost.

  • FIFO has the lowest COGS and the highest gross profit, because FIFO matches older, lower-cost inventory against current selling prices.
  • In recent years the gross profit impact from using FIFO has been minimal for many companies, because of lower rates of inflation and increased management focus on reducing inventory quantities through improved manufacturing processes and better inventory controls.

Balance sheet effects

  • In periods of rising costs, LIFO inventory can be markedly lower than FIFO.
  • LIFO balance sheets do not accurately represent the cost a company would incur to replace its current inventory.
  • LIFO and FIFO balance sheets are not comparable. GAAP therefore requires firms that choose LIFO to also report (in the footnotes) the equivalent FIFO inventory amounts.

Caterpillar's 2022 inventory note: "We state inventories at the lower of cost or net realizable value. We principally determine cost using the last-in, first-out (LIFO) method. The value of inventories on the LIFO basis represented about 65 percent and 60 percent of total inventories at December 31, 2022 and 2021, respectively. If the FIFO (first-in, first-out) method had been in use, inventories would have been $3,321 million and $2,599 million higher than reported at December 31, 2022 and 2021, respectively." This difference is the LIFO reserve.

Cash flow effects

Taxes drive the decision to use LIFO. Caterpillar has saved $968 million in total taxes over time.

LO 6-2 Inventory Disclosures

Lower of cost or market (LCM)

  • The lower of cost or market rule says: if the market value of inventory is less than its cost, "write down" the inventory to its market value.
  • For example, Lowe's uses the FIFO costing method. At the end of each accounting period, Lowe's compares ending FIFO inventory with market value (replacement cost) and writes inventory down when market value < cost.
  • This noncash expense, reflected in COGS, reduces gross profit.

LIFO reserve adjustments — the balance sheet

FIFO Inventory = LIFO Inventory + LIFO Reserve

Three adjustments convert LIFO to FIFO on the balance sheet:

  • Increase Inventory by the LIFO reserve
  • Increase tax liabilities by the tax rate applied to the LIFO reserve
  • Increase retained earnings by the difference

For CAT's 2022 balance sheet: increase Inventory by $3,321M; increase tax liabilities by $697.41M ($3,321M × 21%); increase retained earnings by the difference of $2,623.59M ($3,321M − $697.41M).

LIFO reserve adjustments — the income statement

FIFO COGS = LIFO COGS − Change in LIFO Reserve
  • During the year, the change in the LIFO reserve represents the difference in LIFO versus FIFO COGS during the current year.
  • FIFO COGS will be lower, which causes higher pretax profit, higher income tax, and higher net income.

For CAT's 2022 income statement: decrease COGS by $722M (the increase in the LIFO reserve); increase tax expense by $151.62M ($722M × 21%); increase net income by the difference of $570.38M.

Factors that negatively affect GPM

  • Changes in product mix toward lower-margin products
  • New products introduced at low prices to gain market share
  • Increases in production costs
  • Decrease in production volume
  • Increases in supply-chain costs (procurement, transportation, technology, insurance)
  • More generous sales discounts or sales returns policies
  • Inventory obsolescence and/or overstocking
  • Warranty costs
  • General decline in economic activity
  • New competitors in the market
  • Regulation that inhibits sales or adds fees or taxes to products sold

Days inventory outstanding and inventory turnover

Inventory Turnover = Cost of Goods Sold ÷ Average Inventory
Days Inventory Outstanding (DIO) = 365 ÷ Inventory Turnover
  • Inventory turnover measures the number of times during the period that the company sells its inventory.
  • DIO measures the number of days required to sell the average inventory available for sale.

Home Depot, 2022: Inventory turnover = $104,625 ÷ [($24,886 + $22,068) ÷ 2] = 4.456 times; DIO = 365 ÷ 4.456 = 81.9 days.

  • Home Depot turned over (sold) its entire inventory 4.5 times during 2022.
  • It took Home Depot about 82 days to sell its average inventory.
  • From 2013 to 2022, average DIO increased from 60.5 days to 70.6 days for the S&P 500 firms. DIO also varies widely by industry — roughly 13 days in transportation and 16 days in oil and gas, against about 112 days in semiconductors and 133 days in textiles, apparel, and luxury goods.

Interpreting DIO

Analyzing DIO matters for at least two reasons:

ReasonWhat to look for
Inventory qualityProduct mix can include more (or less) higher-margin inventories that sell more slowly; a company can change its promotion policies; a company can realize improvements in manufacturing efficiency and lower investments in direct materials and work-in-process inventories.
Asset utilizationCompanies optimize inventory through improved manufacturing processes that eliminate bottlenecks and the buildup of WIP; just-in-time (JIT) deliveries of raw materials to the production line, reducing raw materials and associated holding costs; and demand-pull production, in which raw materials are released into production when final goods are demanded by customers instead of producing for estimated demand.

Days payable outstanding (DPO)

Accounts Payable Turnover = COGS ÷ Average Accounts Payable
Days Payable Outstanding (DPO) = 365 ÷ Accounts Payable Turnover
  • Accounts payable turnover measures the number of payment cycles per period (typically per year).
  • DPO is the average length of time a company takes to pay its suppliers.
  • Use COGS in the accounts payable ratios, because payables relate to the purchase of inventories, which are reported at cost.

Home Depot, 2022: Accounts payable turnover = $104,625 ÷ [($11,443 + $13,462) ÷ 2] = 8.4 times; DPO = 365 ÷ 8.4 = 43.4 days.

  • Home Depot's DPO of 43 days is slightly longer than the typical 30-day payment term.
  • Delaying payment to suppliers allows the purchasing company to increase its available cash.
  • However, excessive delays (called "leaning on the trade") can damage supplier relationships.

Cash conversion cycle (CCC)

Days inventory outstanding + Days sales outstanding (accounts receivable) − Days payable outstanding = Cash conversion cycle

The aim is to minimize the time to complete a cycle. Each time a company completes one cash conversion cycle, it has purchased and sold inventory (realizing sales and gross profit), paid A/P, and collected A/R.

LO 6-4 Capitalization and Depreciation of Tangible Assets

PP&E: capitalization and depreciation

  • When PP&E is acquired, it is recorded at cost on the balance sheet. This is called capitalization — which is why expenditures for PP&E are called CAPEX.
  • The amount capitalized includes all costs to put the asset into service: the PP&E cost as well as transportation, duties, tax, and the necessary costs to install and test the assets.
  • Once capitalized, the PP&E cost is recognized as expense over the period of time that the PP&E produces revenues. Depreciation recognizes the using up of the asset over its useful life.
  • Instead of purchasing PP&E outright, companies often enter into long-term equipment leases. If the lease terms extend beyond a year, the equipment is capitalized just like other tangible assets.

Depreciation expense assumptions

To determine depreciation expense, a company makes three estimates:

EstimateDefinition
Useful lifeThe period of time over which the asset is expected to generate measurable benefits
Salvage valueThe amount expected for the asset when disposed of at the end of its useful life
Depreciation methodAn estimate of how the asset is used up over its useful life

Straight-line method

Assume a company acquires a machine for $100,000 with a $10,000 salvage value and a 5-year useful life.

Depreciation baseDepreciation rate
Cost − Salvage value = $100,000 − $10,000 = $90,0001 ÷ Estimated useful life = 1 ÷ 5 years = 20%

With the straight-line (SL) method, $18,000 of depreciation expense is recognized per year over the asset's estimated useful life:

Depreciation expense per year = $90,000 ÷ 5 = $18,000

Net book value

Record $18,000 straight-line depreciationNoncash assets −18,000 (Accumulated Depreciation); Earned capital −18,000 (Retained Earnings)Expenses +18,000 (Depreciation Expense); Net income −18,000

The accumulated depreciation (contra asset) account increases by $18,000, thus reducing net PP&E by the same amount. At the end of the 1st year the asset is reported on the balance sheet as:

Balance sheet presentationCategory

The $82,000 is the asset's net book value.

Other depreciation methods

  • Accelerated depreciation methods record more depreciation in the early years of an asset's useful life (hence "accelerated") and less in later years.
  • The units-of-production method records depreciation according to asset use. The depreciation base is cost less salvage value; the depreciation rate is the units produced and sold during the year compared with the total expected units to be produced and sold.
  • The Modified Accelerated Cost Recovery System (MACRS), an accelerated method, is required by the U.S. IRS to calculate taxable income.

R&D facilities and equipment

  • R&D facilities and equipment are not immediately expensed. If they are general-use in nature, costs are capitalized on the balance sheet and depreciated over their useful life like other depreciable assets.
  • R&D facilities and equipment purchased specifically for a single R&D project, with no alternative use, are expensed immediately.
  • R&D costs do create future benefits and might seem to be assets — but R&D costs are not capitalized:
  • Whether any tangible products or services will be developed is often uncertain; many R&D efforts fail to produce any benefits whatsoever.
  • Even for promising R&D programs, the timing of future products and services is uncertain.
  • Salaries for R&D personnel are no different from those of other personnel, whose salaries and wages are expensed when incurred.

LO 6-5 Asset Sales, Impairments, and Restructuring

Asset sales

Gain or Loss on Asset Sale = Proceeds from Sale − Net Book Value of Asset Sold
  • Net book value is acquisition cost less accumulated depreciation.
  • When an asset is sold, both the acquisition cost and the related accumulated depreciation are removed from the balance sheet.
  • Any gain or loss is reported in income from continuing operations.

Macy's, 2022: "Gains on Sale of Real Estate — The company recognized gains of $89 million in 2022 associated with sales of real estate, as compared to $91 million in 2021. The 2022 asset sale gains mainly consist of gains from the sale of 6 properties, versus 18 properties sold at a gain in 2021."

Asset impairments

  • Sometimes market values of PP&E assets decrease.
  • If the asset value is deemed to be permanently impaired, the company must write off the impaired cost and recognize losses.
Impairment Analysis of Tangible AssetsCategory
Sum of (undiscounted) expected cash flows is less than net book value of asset → YesAsset is impaired: write down asset to fair value
Sum of (undiscounted) expected cash flows is not less than net book value → NoNo asset impairment

CVS Health's note: "Store Impairments — During the year ended December 31, 2021, the store impairment charge relates to the write down of operating lease right-of-use (ROU) assets and property and equipment in connection with the planned closure of approximately 900 retail stores between 2022 and 2024."

As operating cash flows declined at CVS stores nationwide, management determined to close retail locations, and it was unlikely those locations would generate enough cash flow to absorb the cost of the ROU and PP&E assets. The facilities were therefore deemed impaired and the assets were written down to their fair values, resulting in an impairment expense of $1,358 million in 2021.

Restructuring costs

Corporate restructurings are designed to turn a company around and are frequently initiated in response to poor performance, mounting debt, or shareholder pressure. A restructuring can involve:

  • Eliminating business segments
  • Selling major assets
  • Downsizing the workforce
  • Reconfiguring debt

Ultimately, the goal of a restructuring is to positively impact a company's long-term financial performance.

Disclosure of restructuring costs. Because of their magnitude, restructurings require enhanced disclosure either as a separate line item in the income statement or as a footnote. Restructuring costs typically include three components:

ComponentHow it is accounted for
Employee severance or relocation costsEstimate total costs of terminating or relocating selected employees; report total estimated costs as an expense (and a liability) in the period the restructuring program is announced; subsequent payments to employees reduce the restructuring accrual (the liability).
Asset write-downsRestructuring usually involves closure or relocation of manufacturing or administrative facilities, which can require write-downs if fair value < book value — of long-term assets (PP&E), intangible assets (goodwill), and inventories. Write-downs have no cash flow effects unless the write-down has tax consequences.
Other restructuring costsThe remaining costs of the program — reported by Pfizer as exit costs (credits).

PP&E turnover rate

A crucial issue in analyzing PP&E is determining productivity (utilization): what level of PP&E is needed to generate a dollar of revenue, and how capital intensive is the company relative to its competitors?

PP&E Turnover (PPET) = Sales ÷ Average PP&E, net

Home Depot, 2022: PP&E turnover = $157,403 ÷ [($25,199 + $25,631) ÷ 2] = 6.2.

Analysis of PP&E turnover

  • Higher PP&E turnover is preferable, because it implies a lower capital investment for a given level of sales.
  • Higher turnover increases profitability because the company avoids asset carrying costs and the freed-up assets can generate operating cash flow.
  • PP&E turnover is lower for capital-intensive firms and higher for companies in service or IT. Among selected S&P industries it ranges from roughly 0.3 in utilities and 1.0 in oil, gas, and consumable fuels to about 6.5 in capital goods.

PP&E useful life

Assuming straight-line depreciation and zero salvage value, we can estimate the average useful life of depreciable assets:

Average useful life = Average depreciable asset cost ÷ Depreciation expense

Depreciable asset cost excludes land and construction-in-progress.

Home Depot, 2022: Average useful life = ($42,259 + $41,573) ÷ 2 ÷ $2,756 = 15.2 years, where depreciable asset cost is PP&E at cost less land and construction in progress ($52,275 − $8,719 − $1,297 = $42,259 for 2022).

PP&E percent used up

Percent used up measures the proportion of a company's depreciable assets that have already been "used up" — that is, transferred to the income statement. The ratio reflects the percent of depreciable assets that are no longer productive:

Percent used up = Accumulated depreciation ÷ Depreciable asset cost

Home Depot, 2022: Percent used up = $26,644 ÷ $42,259 = 63%.

Analysis of percent used up

  • Knowing the degree to which a company's assets are used up is of interest in forecasting future cash flows.
  • If depreciable assets are, say, 80% used up, we might anticipate a higher level of capital expenditures to replace aging assets in the near future.
  • We also expect that older assets are less efficient and will incur higher maintenance costs.

LO 6-7 Intangible Assets

Acquiring intangible assets

  • When one company acquires another, we must allocate the purchase price to the assets acquired and the liabilities assumed.
  • After the purchase price is allocated, any excess is allocated to the acquired intangible assets.
  • Each intangible asset must be identified, valued, and assigned a useful life, just like PP&E.
  • Intangibles can comprise a large proportion of a company's balance sheet: intangible assets as a percent of total assets for S&P 500 nonfinancial firms rose from about 23% in 2013 to about 31% in 2022.

Why intangibles are "missing" from many balance sheets

  • Although intangibles are likely to yield future benefits, it is difficult to reliably measure those benefits.
  • Many U.S. balance sheets are "missing" valuable intangible assets.
  • Even though many intangible assets are not on the balance sheet, they have tremendous economic value.
  • This can explain part of the wide disparity between a company's market value and the book value of its stockholders' equity. The median market-to-book ratio for S&P 500 companies rose from roughly 2.2 in 2009 to a peak near 4.8 in 2021, falling to about 3.8 in 2022.

Accounting for goodwill

  • Goodwill is not amortized, because it is considered to have an indefinite (or infinite) life.
  • Goodwill must be evaluated for impairment at least annually and at the same time each year, to prevent companies from gaming the impairment evaluation.
  • From a practical perspective, the goodwill impairment test is straightforward:
  • Estimate the fair value of each subsidiary that has goodwill on its balance sheet
  • Compare the estimated fair value of the subsidiary to its carrying value on the parent company's balance sheet
  • If a subsidiary's fair value < carrying value, the subsidiary's goodwill is impaired
  • A goodwill impairment test is a test of whether the value of the entire subsidiary is impaired.

Cullowhee examples

The review above uses the lecture and textbook examples. Here are the same ideas with Cullowhee Cycle Works, the company in your walkthroughs and quizzes.

Cost flow and FIFO

Cullowhee had 12,000 packs available at a total cost of $4,965,000, and 1,000 packs remain. Under FIFO, the ending packs are the newest (Q4 at $440): ending inventory = $440,000 and COGS = 4,965,000 − 440,000 = $4,525,000.

LIFO and the effect of rising costs

Under LIFO the ending packs are the oldest (beginning inventory at $380): ending inventory = $380,000 and COGS = 4,965,000 − 380,000 = $4,585,000, which is $60,000 more than FIFO. At a 25% tax rate, LIFO saves about $15,000 of taxes.

Weighted-average cost

Average cost = 4,965,000 ÷ 12,000 = $413.75 per pack. Ending inventory = 1,000 × 413.75 = $413,750, and COGS = $4,551,250, between FIFO ($4,525,000) and LIFO ($4,585,000).

Capitalize or expense?

Assembly line ($ thousand): invoice 1,800 + freight 60 + installation 100 + test runs 40 = 2,000 capitalized. Worker training and the maintenance contract are expensed.

Straight-line depreciation

Assembly line: cost 2,000, salvage 100, life 10 years. Depreciation = (2,000 − 100) ÷ 10 = 190 a year. If the line runs for 3 months of FY2026, depreciation is 190 × 3/12 = 47.5.

Goodwill and acquired technology

Highlands Cell Packs ($ thousand): price 6,000 − (PP&E 1,700 + inventory 500 + technology 1,600) = goodwill 2,200. The technology has an 8-year life: 1,600 ÷ 8 = 200 a year, or 100 for the six months after July 1.

Days payables and the cash conversion cycle

Cullowhee FY2026: average payables = (4,500 + 5,400) ÷ 2 = 4,950; DPO = 365 × 4,950 ÷ 37,030 = 48.8 days. DIO = 101.5 days (average inventory 10,300), DSO = 35.4 days. Cash conversion cycle = 101.5 + 35.4 − 48.8 = 88.1 days.

All people and companies in the Cullowhee case are fictional.
