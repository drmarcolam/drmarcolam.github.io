The Big Picture: Two Halves of Stockholders’ Equity

Contributed capital — what owners put inEarned capital — what the business generated
AccountsPreferred stock, common stock (at par), additional paid-in capital, less treasury stock (LOs 8-1, 8-2, 8-3)Retained earnings, accumulated other comprehensive income (LOs 8-4, 8-5)
What increases itIssuing shares, resale of treasury shares, stock-based compensationNet income, other comprehensive income
What decreases itRepurchasing shares (treasury stock or retirement)Dividends, comprehensive losses, the excess paid on retired shares

Three threads run through the module. First, transactions with owners never touch the income statement — issuing stock, buying it back, and paying dividends all bypass net income and settle directly in equity. Second, par value is an accounting artifact, not a measure of value; it only splits the issue proceeds between the common stock account and additional paid-in capital. Third, the share count is management’s choice, which is why EPS (LO 8-6) and book value per share tell us much less across companies than they do within one company over time.

LO 8-1 Stock as a Financing Source

Analyzing stockholders’ equity

  • Companies raise funds by selling shares of stock to investors. Unlike creditors, shareholders have no contractual return.
  • The stockholders’ equity section of the balance sheet reports the book value of the stockholders’ investment, as determined under GAAP.
  • Market value is determined by supply and demand in the marketplace as shares are actively traded among investors — it is not what the balance sheet reports.
  • There are two types of stockholders’ equity accounts: contributed capital and earned capital.

Earned capital accounts

AccountWhat it captures
Accumulated other comprehensive income (AOCI)The cumulative changes to stockholders’ equity other than from transactions with owners and transactions reflected in net income. This account can be positive or negative
Retained earningsThe cumulative net income recorded since inception less all the dividends ever paid to shareholders

Book value per share

A measure commonly used by analysts and the financial press, book value per share is the equity (net book value) of the company available to common shareholders:

Book value per share = (Stockholders’ equity − Preferred stock) ÷ Number of common shares outstanding

Worked example — J&J, 2022:

$76,804 million ÷ (3,119,843,000 − 506,246,000) shares = $29.39 per share

LO 8-2 Stock Issuances and Repurchases

Stock issuance

  • Companies issue stock to obtain cash for use in the business or to acquire another company.
  • If issued for cash, stock issuances increase cash by the issue proceeds, and equity increases by the same amount via contributed capital.
  • If the stock has a par value, the common stock account increases by shares sold × par value; the additional paid-in capital (APIC) account increases by the remainder.

Worked example. J&J issues 1,000 shares of common stock with $1.00 par value for $100 per share (the market price).

TransactionBalance sheet effectIncome statement effect
Issue 1,000 common shares with $1 par value for $100 cash per shareCash asset +100,000; Contributed capital +1,000 (Common Stock) and +99,000 (Additional Paid-In Capital)None
  • Common stock (at par) increases by $1,000 (1,000 shares × $1 par)
  • APIC increases by $99,000 ($100,000 cash proceeds − $1,000 par)

Stock repurchases — treasury stock

Companies repurchase stock to hold as treasury stock for many reasons:

  • The company believes that the market undervalues them
  • To offset the dilutive effects of share-based compensation plans
  • A stock repurchase reduces the size of the company — treasury stock is a contra-equity account with a negative balance, which reduces stockholders’ equity
JOHNSON & JOHNSON — In millions, except for share amounts20222021
Common stock held in treasury, at cost (506,246,000 shares and 490,878,000 shares)41,69439,099
  • J&J had repurchased 506,246,000 shares for $41,694 million, an average repurchase price of $82.36 per share
  • Treasury shares can be resold if the company needs to raise capital for operating or investing activities including mergers and acquisitions
  • Treasury shares can be used to compensate employees and executives

Worked example — repurchase. J&J repurchases 200 common shares at $120 cash per share.

TransactionBalance sheet effectIncome statement effect
Repurchase 200 common shares for $120 cash per shareCash asset −24,000; Contributed capital −24,000 (Treasury Stock)None

Worked example — resale. The 200 treasury shares are subsequently resold at $125 cash per share.

TransactionBalance sheet effectIncome statement effect
Resell 200 treasury (common) shares for $125 cash per shareCash asset +25,000; Contributed capital +24,000 (Treasury Stock) and +1,000 (Additional Paid-In Capital)None
  • Cash increases by $25,000
  • Treasury stock is reduced by $24,000, the original cost of the shares ($120 × 200)
  • The difference of $1,000 is added to APIC — it represents additional contributions from shareholders. No gain is reported, because this is a transaction with owners

Note: the lecture slide writes the original cost as “$120 × 2,000.” The share count is 200, consistent with the $24,000 reduction shown in the same table.

Stock repurchased and retired

Companies increasingly repurchase shares with no intention of reselling — to use excess cash or to reduce the breadth of share ownership. In that case the repurchased shares are retired (canceled) rather than held in treasury.

Worked example. J&J repurchases 200 shares originally issued for $100 per share and pays $120 cash per share with the intention of retiring them.

TransactionBalance sheet effectIncome statement effect
Repurchase and retire 200 common shares for $120 per shareCash asset −24,000; Contributed capital −200 (Common Stock) and −19,800 (APIC); Earned capital −4,000 (Retained Earnings)None
  • Cash decreases by $24,000 ($120/share)
  • Common stock at par decreases by $200 ($1/share) and APIC by $19,800 ($99/share) — reversing the original issue price
  • The difference of $4,000 ($20/share) reduces retained earnings. Note that the company pays more than it received and still reports no loss on the income statement

LO 8-3 Stock-Based Compensation

Why companies use it

  • Create incentives for employees to act like shareholders. The amount of the award is often tied to corporate performance targets including sales, income, and stock price, motivating employees to make decisions that improve company performance.
  • Encourage employee retention and longevity. Employees earn the right to own or purchase shares over time; this waiting period is the vesting period, during which employees have greater incentive to stay.

Types of plans

PlanHow it works
Restricted stockShares are issued to the employee but the employee is not free to sell them during a restriction period. During that period the employee has the rights of a shareholder other than the ability to sell
Restricted stock units (RSUs)The employee is awarded the right to receive a specified number of shares (or cash equivalent) after a vesting period. Unlike restricted stock, shares are not issued until after the restriction period, at which time the employee has all the rights of a shareholder
Employee stock optionsEmployees are given the right to purchase shares at a fixed (strike) price for a specified period. As with restricted stock, there is a vesting period before the employee can purchase
Stock appreciation rights (SARs)Employees are paid in cash or stock for the increase in share price, but do not purchase shares. Similar to an option, with no share purchase required
Employee share purchase plansEmployees may purchase shares directly from the company at a discount, typically a set percentage (such as 85%) of the prevailing market price

Companies often maintain more than one of these plans at the same time. Most plans contain forfeiture provisions — if the employee is terminated for cause or leaves before vesting, the award is forfeited.

Two analysis issues

IssueWhat it means
1. Expense recognitionWhen shares or options are awarded, companies estimate the fair value of the award and recognize that fair value as compensation expense in the period in which the employee provides service
2. Potential dilutionDilution relates to the number of common shares outstanding that have a claim against the company’s earnings or net assets. Mindful of dilution, companies often repurchase shares in advance to use for stock-based compensation plans

J&J’s note: “The Company settles employee benefit equity issuances with treasury shares. Treasury shares are replenished throughout the year for the number of shares used to settle employee benefit equity issuances.”

Accounting steps

Regardless of the type of plan, the accounting follows common steps:

  • When the award is granted, the company estimates the fair value of the award
  • The fair value is recorded as an expense in the income statement, ratably over the vesting period
  • When the shares are issued, common stock and APIC increase in the same manner as for cash-based stock issuances

Reporting and the cash flow statement

Stock-based compensation expense is included on the income statement but rarely reported as a separate line item. We can, however, determine the amount from the statement of cash flows:

J&J adds back the $1,138 million of stock-based compensation expense, which is included in computing net income — similar to the $6,970 million add-back for depreciation.

Is stock-based compensation a cash expense?

The add-back might lead some to conclude that this form of compensation is cash free. It is not. A real cash cost occurs when the company buys new treasury shares in the open market to offset the dilution created by the share award. To accurately evaluate and forecast operating cash flow, analysts either:

  • Include stock-based compensation expense — adding it back to calculate non-GAAP measures is not correct, or
  • Recognize the related treasury-stock purchase as an operating cash outflow

LO 8-4 Cash Dividends and Stock Splits

Two topics relate to the earned capital accounts: dividends, which can be paid in cash, other assets, or additional shares of stock (stock dividends); and stock splits, where additional shares are distributed to existing shareholders.

In fiscal 2022, J&J reported dividends per share of $4.45, totaling $11,682 million, and noted that it “increased its dividend in 2022 for the 60th consecutive year.”

Dividend payout and yield

Dividend payout = Common stock dividends per share ÷ Basic earnings per share (EPS)
Dividend yield = Common stock dividends per share ÷ Current share price
MetricWhat it measuresJ&J, 2022
Dividend payoutThe proportion of the company’s earnings paid out as dividends$4.45 ÷ $6.83 = 65%
Dividend yieldThe relation between dividends and the current market value of the stock$4.45 ÷ $176.65 = 2.5%

Cash dividends — financial effects

Cash dividends reduce cash and retained earnings by the same amount.

Worked example. J&J declares and pays cash dividends of $10 million.

TransactionBalance sheet effectIncome statement effect
Payment of $10 million in cash dividendsCash asset −10 mil.; Earned capital −10 mil. (Retained Earnings)None

Dividend payments do not affect net income. They directly reduce retained earnings and bypass the income statement.

Stock splits

  • A stock split happens when a company issues additional common shares to its existing stockholders.
  • Splits are usually prompted by the company’s desire to reduce its stock price to improve marketability of the shares.
  • A stock split is not a monetary transaction and, as such, there are no financial statement effects.
  • Companies must disclose the number of shares outstanding for all periods presented, must reduce the par value proportionally, and must adjust historical financial statements presented in the current 10-K likewise.

Stock dividends

One common way to perform a stock split is to declare a dividend payable in company stock.

  • To account for a stock dividend, the company reduces retained earnings (just as for cash dividends) and increases contributed capital accounts.
  • Cash and the stock’s par value per share are both unaffected by a stock dividend.

In February 2022, Alphabet Inc. announced a 20-for-one stock split in the form of a stock dividend. After the split, outstanding shares increased twentyfold, to 12,849 million shares.

Note: the lecture slide writes this as “$12,849 M.” The figure is a share count, not a dollar amount.

LO 8-6 Earnings per Share

Basic and diluted EPS

A common metric reported in the financial press is earnings per share (EPS). Companies report two EPS statistics:

Basic EPS = (Net income − Preferred dividends) ÷ Weighted average common shares
Diluted EPS = Basic EPS − EPS impact of dilutive options and warrants − EPS impact of dilutive convertible securities

EPS disclosures are followed obsessively by Wall Street analysts and investors.

Comparing EPS across companies

We cannot compare the EPS of different companies:

  • The number of shares outstanding is not proportional to income
  • Management controls the number of common shares outstanding
  • There is no relation between firm size and EPS

Book value per share(Stockholders’ equity − Preferred stock) ÷ Common shares outstanding
Contributed capitalEquity accounts recording what owners paid in: preferred stock, common stock, APIC, less treasury stock
Diluted EPSEPS adjusted for the most extreme case in which all options are exercised and all convertible securities converted
Dividend payoutDividends per share ÷ Basic EPS
Dividend yieldDividends per share ÷ Current share price
Earned capitalEquity generated by the business: retained earnings and AOCI
Employee share purchase planA plan permitting employees to buy shares directly from the company at a discount, such as 85% of market price
Intrinsic valueThe value of shares issued to employees over and above the price the employee paid
Issued sharesThe cumulative number of shares a company has sold to date
Mandatory convertible preferred stock (MCPS)Preferred stock that converts into common shares at a set ratio; on conversion, preferred stock decreases and APIC increases
Market capitalizationShares outstanding × market price per share
Other comprehensive income (OCI)The period’s aggregated change in unrealized gains and losses that bypasses net income
Par valueAn arbitrary amount set in the charter, used only to split issue proceeds between common stock and APIC
Restricted stockShares issued to an employee who may not sell them during a restriction period
Restricted stock units (RSUs)The right to receive shares (or cash) after a vesting period; shares are not issued until vesting
Shares outstandingIssued shares less treasury shares
Stock appreciation rights (SARs)Awards paying cash or stock for the increase in share price, with no share purchase
Stock dividendA dividend paid in shares; reduces retained earnings, increases contributed capital, leaves par value per share unchanged
Stock optionThe right to purchase shares at a fixed strike price for a specified period, after vesting
Stock splitA distribution of additional shares to existing shareholders; no financial statement effects, par value reduced proportionally
Treasury stockA contra-equity account carrying the cost of repurchased shares held by the company; no voting rights, no dividends
Treasury stock methodThe method for measuring the dilutive effect of options: assume exercise, then assume the proceeds repurchase shares at market
Vesting periodThe waiting time before an employee earns the right to own or purchase shares
