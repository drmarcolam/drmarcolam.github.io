Two threads run through the module. First, an accrual is recognized in the period it is incurred, not the period it is paid — which is why the balance sheet carries liabilities that never came from an external transaction. Second, the price of debt is the present value of its cash flows (LOs 7-3 and 7-6), which is why the coupon rate a company prints on a bond does not determine what that debt actually costs.

What accrued liabilities are

Also called accrued expenses, or accruals.

  • Accrued liabilities are adjustments made to the balance sheet: after all transactions have been recorded, prior to the issuance of the financial statements, so that the statements fairly present the financial condition of the company.
  • These adjustments recognize liabilities (and the related expense on the income statement) that are not the result of external transactions.
  • Accrued liabilities are incurred in the current period and therefore must be recognized in the current period.

Two broad categories of accruals

CategoryExamples
Routine contractual liabilitiesWages to employees for work performed but not yet paid; unpaid interest due in the current period; income taxes owed but not yet paid on profit earned in the period; other expense items such as rent and utilities, incurred but not yet paid
Contingent liabilitiesDepend on the occurrence of a future uncertain event to determine whether a liability exists and in what amount: litigation brought against the company (outcome and amount depend on adjudication); warranty liabilities for products sold (the amount depends on future claims for repair or replacement)

Contractual accruals — wages payable

If the liability for unpaid wages is not reflected on the balance sheet:

  • Liabilities will be understated
  • Wage expense will not be reflected in the income statement
  • Profit for the period will be overstated

To correct for this, accountants make an accounting adjustment. When wages are later paid, cash and wages payable both decrease.

Contractual accruals — deferred revenue

Also called unearned revenue.

  • Deferred revenue represents deposits or other prepayments from customers that the company has not yet earned.
  • Deferred revenue is an accrued liability until the company provides the goods or performs the service according to the contract.
  • Once the company satisfies the performance obligations, the liability is reduced and (earned) revenue is recognized.

Contingent liabilities

Some liabilities are less certain, because the ultimate settlement depends on the outcome of a future event. Examples include guarantees on the debt of another entity, lawsuits, product warranties and recalls, and environmental disasters and remediation.

A contingent liability is recorded when BOTH conditions are met:
1. It is probable that one or more future events will confirm that a liability existed at the financial statement date
2. The amount required to settle the liability in the future can be reasonably determined at the financial statement date

Warranties

  • Warranty liabilities are commitments manufacturers make to repair or replace defective products within a specified period of time.
  • If the obligation is probable and the amount estimable with reasonable certainty, GAAP requires companies to record the expected cost of warranties as a liability, and to record the related warranty expense in the same period that the sales revenue is reported.
  • When the defective product is later replaced or repaired, the liability is reduced and the company records the cost necessary to satisfy the claim.

Worked example. Sales during the period are $10,000; 2% of sales are defective and repairs cost $5.

Warranty expense = $10,000 × 2% × $5 = $1,000

Analyzing warranty disclosures

  • GAAP requires that warranty liabilities reflect the estimated cost the company expects to incur as a result of warranty claims.
  • Companies might intentionally distort this estimate:
  • Underestimate the warranty liability to report higher current income
  • Overestimate the warranty liability to depress current income and create a liability (a "cookie jar reserve") to absorb future warranty costs
  • These actions affect income in both the current and subsequent periods — both periods are incorrect.
  • We must closely examine warranty liabilities and scrutinize deviations from the historical relation to sales levels or from peer company levels.

LO 7-2 Short-Term Debt

  • When a company borrows short-term funds, it reports the cash received on the balance sheet together with an increase in liabilities (notes payable).
  • The note is reported as a current liability because the company expects to repay it within a year.
  • The borrower incurs (and the lender earns) interest on the note as time passes.
  • GAAP requires the borrower to accrue the interest liability and the related interest expense each time financial statements are issued.

Worked example. Verizon borrows $1,000 cash on January 1. The note bears 12% interest, payable quarterly.

LO 7-3 Long-Term Debt Pricing

Why bonds

  • When companies require a large amount of financing, issuing bonds in capital markets is a cost-efficient way to raise funds.
  • Bonds are structured like any other borrowing: the borrower receives cash and agrees to pay it back with interest.
  • Generally the entire face amount (principal) of the bond is repaid at maturity, with interest payments made in the interim (usually semiannually).

Issuance. Companies normally work with an underwriter (like Goldman Sachs) to set the terms of the bond issue. The underwriter sells individual bonds (usually in $1,000 denominations) to retail clients and professional portfolio managers (like The Vanguard Group), and receives a fee for underwriting the issue. These bonds are investments for individual investors, other companies, retirement plans, and insurance companies.

Interest rates and bond prices. Issued bonds trade in the secondary market just like stocks. Bond prices fluctuate even though the company's obligation to repay principal and interest remains fixed throughout the life of the bond. The bond's fixed rate of interest can be higher or lower than rates offered on other securities of similar risk, and because bonds compete with other investments, prices are set relative to those alternatives — the laws of supply and demand cause bond prices to rise and fall.

Two interest rates

RateAlso calledWhat it does
Coupon rateContract rate, stated rateStated in the bond contract; used to compute the dollar amount of interest payments paid in cash to bondholders during the life of the issue
Market rateYield, effective rateThe rate of return investors expect to earn; used to price the bond

Cash flows to bondholders. Bondholders expect two types of cash flow: periodic interest payments (usually semiannual) during the bond's life — an annuity, because the payments are equal in amount and made at regular intervals — and a single payment of the face amount at maturity, called a lump-sum because it occurs only once.

Bond price = PV of the annuity payments + PV of the lump-sum payment

Effective cost of debt

  • Bonds are priced to yield the return (market rate) demanded by investors.
  • Thus the effective rate of a bond always equals the yield demanded by investors, regardless of the bond's coupon rate.
  • This means companies cannot influence the effective cost of debt by raising or lowering the coupon rate — doing so will only result in a bond premium or discount.

Interest expense versus interest payments

The effective cost of debt determines the interest expense reported in the issuer's income statement. Because of bond discounts and premiums, interest expense is usually different from the cash interest paid.

Income statement reporting. Interest expense reported on the income statement represents the effective cost of debt, including the cash interest paid (at the coupon rate) plus a portion of the additional borrowing costs associated with the discount (or less a portion of the benefit associated with the premium). The process of recognizing this additional interest expense on a non-par bond is called amortization.

Bond repurchases

Also called bond redemption or bond extinguishment. Companies can, and sometimes do, repurchase their bonds prior to maturity.

From Verizon's note: "Early Debt Redemption Costs — During 2022, we recorded pre-tax early debt redemption costs of $1.2 billion primarily in connection with tender offers. During 2021, we recorded pre-tax early debt redemption costs of $3.5 billion primarily in connection with tender offers, the redemptions of securities issued by Verizon and open market repurchases of various Company and subsidiary notes."

Note: the lecture slide's bullet cites a $700 million loss in 2018. That figure is from an earlier edition and does not appear in the note reproduced on the same slide; use the $1.2 billion (2022) and $3.5 billion (2021) amounts above.

Gain or Loss on Bond Repurchase = Net Bonds Payable − Repurchase Payment
  • The net bonds payable, also referred to as the book value or carrying value, is the net amount reported on the balance sheet.
  • If the issuer pays more to retire the bonds than the amount carried on its balance sheet, it reports a loss on its income statement.
  • The issuer reports a gain on bond retirement if the repurchase price is less than the net bonds payable.

Fair value disclosures

  • GAAP requires companies to report current fair values of their debt in the notes — but not on the balance sheet.
  • Changes in fair values are not included in net income, because gains and losses reverse with subsequent fluctuations in interest rates, and because the bonds are repaid at par at maturity.

LO 7-5 Credit Analysis and the Quality of Debt

The fundamental questions

  • Will the company be able to pay back the amount borrowed?
  • What is the downside risk if the company fails to make payments?

Creditors focus on capital structure (solvency), coverage, and liquidity. They also forecast the company's free operating cash flow and compare it with the expected maturities of the company's debt; the time horizon of that forecast differs depending on the type of credit.

Credit ratings

A credit rating is an opinion of an entity's creditworthiness, captured in alpha-numeric scales. Each rating agency uses its own rating system, and many investment managers are precluded from purchasing noninvestment grade bonds.

The two-step approach. Each agency develops its own proprietary models, but in general the rating agencies take a two-step approach: (1) assess the likelihood of default (the nonpayment of amounts owed), and (2) assess the potential loss the lender will suffer in the event of default. Factors bearing on collectibility in default include:

  • How does the debt rank relative to other issuances by the same borrower?
  • What collateral, if any, has been pledged to secure the debt in the event of nonpayment?
  • What controls and restrictions have the lenders placed on the company to limit risk exposure?

Economics of credit ratings. Lenders require a higher interest rate as the creditworthiness of the borrower declines — this is the risk premium.

Yield Rate = Risk-Free Rate + Risk Premium

Over 2013–2023, BAA-rated corporate yields consistently ran above AAA yields, and both ran above the 10-Year Treasury — the visible price of credit risk.

S&P's general approach. S&P's Corporate Criteria Framework combines a business risk profile (country risk and industry risk, together the CICRA, plus competitive position) with a financial risk profile (cash flow/leverage) to produce an anchor. Modifiers — diversification/portfolio effect, capital structure, financial policy, liquidity, management/governance, and comparable ratings analysis — adjust the anchor to a stand-alone credit profile, which group or government influence then adjusts to the final issuer credit rating.

Capital structure (solvency) analysis

Capital structure is the specific mix of debt and equity used to finance a company's assets and operations. Review the balance sheet to determine debt owed; DuPont analysis uses Total assets ÷ Stockholders' equity. Two other common ratios:

Liabilities-to-equity ratio = Total liabilities ÷ Stockholders' equity
Total debt-to-equity = (Long-term debt and leases including current portion + Short-term debt and leases) ÷ Stockholders' equity

The liabilities-to-equity ratio shows how reliant a company is on creditor financing compared with equity financing. The total debt-to-equity ratio distinguishes between operating creditors and debt obligations.

LO 7-6 Time Value of Money

Present value concepts

Would you rather receive a dollar now, or a dollar one year from now? Most people answer a dollar now — intuition tells us that a dollar received now is more valuable than the same amount received in the future, because the dollar received now could be invested, and one year from now we would have the dollar and the interest earned on it.

Risk and interest factors yield these generalizations:

  • The right to receive an amount now (present value) is worth more than the right to receive the same amount later (future value)
  • The longer we must wait to receive an amount, the less attractive it is
  • The greater the interest rate, the greater the amount we will receive in the future
  • Putting the last two together, the difference between the present value of an amount and its future value is Principal × Interest Rate × Time
  • The more risk associated with a cash flow, the higher the interest rate

Present value computation. If we have $90.91 today and can invest it at 10% for 1 year, our investment will grow to $100:

$90.91 × 1.10 × 1 year = $100 (rounded)

So $90.91 is the present value of $100 to be received in 1 year. Equivalently, at a 10% investment rate we can calculate the present value by discounting the future value: Present Value $90.91 ← discounted for 1 year at 10% ← Future Value $100.

Future value concepts

The future value of a single sum is the amount a specific investment is worth at a future date if invested at a given rate of compound interest.

To illustrate: we invest $6,000 in a savings account paying 6% annual interest, leaving principal and interest in the account for five years, with interest credited at the end of each year. Using Table 3 in Appendix A, the factor 1.33823 sits at the intersection of the row for five periods and the column for 6%:

Principal × Factor = Future Value
$6,000 × 1.33823 = $8,029

Future value of an annuity. If instead of a single amount we invest a specified amount each period, we have an annuity. Suppose we invest $2,000 at the end of each year for five years at an 8% annual rate of return. Using Table 4 in Appendix A — the future value of a dollar invested at the end of each period — the factor 5.86660 is in the row for five periods and the column for 8%:

Periodic Payment × Factor = Future Value
$2,000 × 5.86660 = $11,733
