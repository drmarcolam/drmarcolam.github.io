1. Cost behavior patterns and fixed cost per unit

Tobias Reese wants to know how plant costs react when the second shift raises volume.

Key ideas
  • Variable: total changes in proportion to activity. Fixed: total is constant within the relevant range. Mixed: a fixed part plus a variable part. Step: constant over a range, then jumps.
  • Per unit, variable cost is constant and fixed cost per unit falls as volume rises.
  • Committed fixed costs (capacity) are hard to change. Discretionary fixed costs are set each period by management.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee plant utilities and maintenance have a fixed part of about $47,071 a month.

Monthly volumeFixed cost per unit ($)
3,000 units15.69
5,000 units9.41
8,000 units5.88

Total fixed cost is the same. Spreading it over more bikes lowers the fixed cost per bike.

2. The linear cost equation and the relevant range

A budget needs an equation. The equation works only inside the range of activity it was built from.

Key ideas
  • Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost per unit × units, or Y = a + bX.
  • The relevant range is the normal range of activity in which the fixed-cost and variable-cost assumptions hold.
  • Predicting outside the data is an extrapolation. A second shift may add supervisors, equipment or overtime.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee's estimated plant cost equation (regression, $):

Fixed cost per month (a)47,071

Predicted cost at 5,000 units92,291

The data stop at 6,797 units. The 8,000-unit prediction is outside the relevant range, so treat it as a floor.

3. The high-low method

The simplest estimate uses only the highest and lowest months.

Key ideas
  • Variable cost per unit = (high cost − low cost) ÷ (high units − low units).
  • Fixed cost = total cost at either point − variable cost per unit × units at that point.
  • Use representative high and low points, not outliers. The method ignores all the other months.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee: highest month May 2026 (6,797 units, $108,394); lowest month Jan 2025 (2,359 units, $67,935).

StepResult
Variable cost per unit: (108,394 − 67,935) ÷ (6,797 − 2,359)$9.12
Fixed cost: 108,394 − 9.12 × 6,797$46,429
Estimate at 5,000 units: 46,429 + 9.12 × 5,000$92,012

4. Scatter diagrams, regression and R-squared

Regression uses every month, not just two, and tells you how good the fit is.

Key ideas
  • A scatter diagram plots each month and shows outliers.
  • Least squares regression fits the line that minimizes squared deviations. The intercept estimates fixed cost, and the slope estimates variable cost per unit.
  • R-squared is the share of the variation in cost explained by activity. A high R-squared does not make predictions reliable outside the range of the data.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Cullowhee's 24 months of data:

Result
Intercept (fixed cost per month)$47,071
Slope (variable cost per unit)$9.044

5. Data problems in cost estimation

Even a good method fails if the data do not match the question.

Key ideas
  • Price changes: adjust data for inflation. Technology changes: use data that reflect current methods.
  • Time lags: costs may be recorded in a different period than the activity.
  • Outliers from unusual events can distort the estimate: investigate before keeping or removing them. Choose a cost driver with a clear cause-and-effect link.

6. Manufacturing cost classifications

The same cost can be classified by what it is made of, whether it is traceable, and how it behaves.

Key ideas
  • Manufacturing costs are direct materials, direct labor and manufacturing overhead.
  • Costs are also direct or indirect (traceable to a product or not) and variable or fixed (behavior).
  • Short-term decisions and break-even use the fixed/variable split. Long-run evaluation and full product cost use direct/indirect allocations.
