LO 15-1 Basic Cost Behavior Patterns

Cost behavior refers to how a cost reacts to changes in the level of business activity. As activity volume (such as sales units, production units, or service hours) rises or falls, a particular cost may rise, fall, or remain constant. Understanding cost behavior patterns is essential for manager planning, budgeting, cost control, and short-run decision analysis.

Core Cost Behavior Classifications

Variable Costs: Changes in total in direct proportion to changes in sales volume or activity level. Variable costs equal zero dollars when activity is zero. On a per-unit basis, variable cost remains constant. Total Variable Cost Equation: Y = bX, where b is the variable cost per unit and X is total activity volume.

Fixed Costs: Remain constant in total amount regardless of changes in activity volume within the relevant range. On a per-unit basis, fixed cost decreases as activity increases (spreading fixed cost over more units). Total Fixed Cost Equation: Y = a, where a is total fixed cost (slope is zero).

Mixed Costs: Contain both a fixed and a variable cost element (sometimes called semi-variable costs). Total mixed cost increases in a linear fashion as activity increases, but is positive in amount when activity is zero. Total Mixed Cost Equation: Y = a + bX.

Step Costs: Remain constant within a narrow range of activity, but shift abruptly to a higher level when activity exceeds that range. Step costs increase in a step-like fashion as activity increases (Equation: Y = a_i).

Strategic Insights: Cost Behavior Assumptions & Structural Shifts Shift in Corporate Cost Structures: Due to increased automation, robotics, and information technology, total cost functions in modern companies have shifted toward significantly HIGHER fixed costs and LOWER variable costs. Managing fixed costs and capacity utilization has become critical for organizational survival. One strategic response to reduce fixed cost exposure is outsourcing non-core operational activities.

LO 15-2 Total Cost Behavior & Linear Cost Equations

To model total organizational costs, managerial accountants represent cost behavior through a linear total cost equation. This equation models total cost (Y) as the sum of fixed capacity costs and variable activity-driven costs.

The Accountant’s Linear Total Cost Equation Total Cost Equation: Y = a + bX • Y = Total Cost (the dependent variable being estimated) • a = Total Fixed Cost (the vertical axis intercept / baseline cost when X = 0) • b = Variable Cost per Unit of Activity (the slope of the total cost line, ΔY / ΔX) • X = Total Activity Volume / Cost Driver (the independent variable, e.g., units or hours)

The Relevant Range & Economic vs. Accounting Cost Models

Relevant Range Concept: The relevant range is defined as the normal range of activity in which a company expects to operate, and where the linear cost assumptions (constant total fixed cost and constant variable cost per unit) remain valid. If demand surges outside the relevant range (e.g., Kellogg’s expanding into temporary production facilities), total fixed costs will jump to a new step level.

Economist vs. Accountant Model: Marginal cost is the extra cost incurred to produce one additional unit. While economists model total costs as a curvilinear function (where marginal cost fluctuates based on capacity constraints and efficiency), accountants use a linear approximation within the relevant range.

Types of Fixed Costs: Committed vs. Discretionary

Committed Fixed Costs (Capacity Costs): Investments in facilities, equipment, and organizational structure that cannot be significantly reduced in the short term without threatening long-run profitability (e.g., building leases, equipment depreciation, real estate taxes).

Discretionary Fixed Costs (Managed Costs): Fixed costs that arise from annual management decisions to spend on specific operating areas (e.g., advertising, employee training programs, research & development, corporate internships). Can be adjusted in the short term.

LO 15-3 Analytic Approaches to Cost Estimation

Cost estimation is the determination of the functional relationship between activity drivers and costs. Managerial accountants use three primary analytic methods to separate mixed costs into fixed and variable components:

1. The High-Low Method

A quick, two-point mathematical technique that estimates variable cost per unit and total fixed costs using only the period of highest activity and period of lowest activity.

High-Low Method Equations & Worked Example Step 1: Calculate Variable Cost per Unit (b): b = (Total Cost at Highest Activity - Total Cost at Lowest Activity) ÷ (Highest Activity Level - Lowest Activity Level) b = Δ Total Cost ÷ Δ Activity Level Step 2: Calculate Total Fixed Costs (a): a = Total Cost - [b × Activity Level] (using either the High or Low data point) Worked Cereal Production Example (Slide 29): • Low Activity (Jan): 8,600 cereal boxes produced, Total Cost = $25,000 • High Activity (Nov): 11,600 cereal boxes produced, Total Cost = $31,600 • b = ($31,600 - $25,000) ÷ (11,600 - 8,600) = $6,600 ÷ 3,000 boxes = $2.20 per box • a = $31,600 - [$2.20 × 11,600] = $31,600 - $25,520 = $6,080 Total Fixed Cost • Total Cost Equation: Y = $6,080 + $2.20X

2. Scatter Diagrams

A plot of historical activity levels (X-axis) against total costs (Y-axis), where individual observations are represented by visual dots. Scatter diagrams allow managers to visually inspect the data for linearity, detect obvious outliers, and gain intuitive insight into cost behavior before running statistical models.

3. The Least-Squares Regression Method

A statistical method that fits a cost line to data by mathematically minimizing the sum of all squared vertical deviations between individual actual cost observations and the estimated cost line. Regression is statistically superior to High-Low because it utilizes ALL data points rather than just two extremes.

Least-Squares Regression Capabilities & Limitations Statistical Measures & Goodness of Fit: • Coefficient of Determination (R²): Measures the percentage of total variation in the dependent variable (Total Cost Y) explained by changes in the independent activity variable (X). R² ranges from 0.0 to 1.0 (higher is better). • Simple Regression: Models total cost using a single cost driver (Y = a + bX). • Multiple Regression: Models total cost using two or more activity drivers (Y = a + b₁X₁ + b₂X₂). • Managerial Caution: Mathematical equations do NOT make business decisions; they are analytical tools. Estimates must make intuitive business sense and reflect normal operating conditions.

LO 15-4 Data & Operational Problems in Cost Estimation

Managerial accountants frequently encounter practical data anomalies when estimating total cost equations. Reliable cost equations require addressing the following operational problems:

Changes in Technology: Historical cost data must reflect the current machinery, automation, and technological processes being used. Old data from discarded equipment distorts future cost predictions.

Price Level Changes: Cost data collected over multi-year periods must be adjusted for general inflation and specific raw material price changes so that future cost equations reflect current price levels.

Environmental & ESG Factors: Companies investing in green technology or facing carbon pricing/taxes must incorporate environmental compliance fees. For instance, carbon tax increases variable cost per unit produced.

Time Lags & Matching Activity with Costs: Costs are often recorded in a different time period than the activity that generated them (e.g., cell phone or utility bills arriving at month-end for daily usage). Shorter time periods increase matching errors.

Identifying Causal Activity Drivers: Selecting an activity driver requires judgment, experience, and establishing a logical, causal relationship between activity and cost rather than relying on spurious statistical correlations.

LO 15-5 Cost Classifications for Decision Making

Manufacturing organizations divide product costs into three primary categories (Direct Materials, Direct Labor, Manufacturing Overhead). These costs can be simultaneously classified by behavior (Variable/Fixed) and traceability (Direct/Indirect).

The matrix below illustrates how manufacturing costs can be classified into any combination of behavior and traceability:

Managerial decision-making requires selecting different cost concepts for different decision horizons:

  • • Short-Term Issues: Focus on Fixed vs. Variable costs, Contribution Margin, and Break-Even Analysis. Fixed costs are generally sunk or committed.
  • • Long-Run Evaluation: Focus on Full Product Cost, Direct vs. Indirect allocations, and total capacity investment evaluation.

Key terms

TermDefinition
Cost BehaviorHow a cost reacts to changes in the level of business activity (sales volume or production).
Variable CostA cost that varies in total in direct proportion to activity changes; remains constant per unit.
Fixed CostA cost that remains constant in total regardless of activity changes within the relevant range.
Mixed CostA cost containing both fixed and variable components (modeled as Y = a + bX).
Step CostA cost that remains constant over a narrow activity range but jumps to a higher level when volume exceeds that range.
Relevant RangeThe normal operating activity range in which linear cost assumptions remain valid.
Linear Cost EquationMathematical representation of total cost: Y = a + bX (Fixed Cost + [Variable Rate × Activity]).
Marginal CostThe incremental cost incurred to produce one additional unit of output.
Committed Fixed CostLong-term capacity investment in plant, equipment, or structure that cannot be easily altered.
