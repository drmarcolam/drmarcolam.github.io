1. Financial versus managerial accounting

Laurel Ammons needs information to decide where to focus. That is a managerial accounting question, not a financial reporting question.

Key ideas
  • Financial accounting reports past performance to outsiders (investors, lenders, regulators) in general-purpose statements that follow GAAP.
  • Managerial accounting gives managers detailed, often forward-looking information for planning, control and decisions. It is internal and does not have to follow GAAP.
  • The IMA sets professional standards for management accountants and supports the CMA designation.
Worked example: Cullowhee

The same plant, two audiences:

QuestionType of accounting
Cullowhee lender: net sales of $55.2 million for FY2026Financial accounting (GAAP, audited, outside users)
Plant manager: unit economics by product line, and cost of a second shiftManagerial accounting (internal, forward-looking, detailed)

Both draw on the same transactions, but they answer different questions for different users.

2. Mission, goals, strategy and competitive position

Cullowhee sells four product lines in different ways. Which accounting information matters depends on the strategy behind each line.

Key ideas
  • A mission is the basic purpose. A goal is a definable, measurable target. A strategy is a course of action that helps reach goals.
  • Porter names three strategic positions: cost leadership (low cost), differentiation (unique, premium price) and market niche (a specialized segment).
  • Managerial accounting supports the plan-organize-lead-control cycle with budgets, cost analysis and performance reports.

3. The value chain

Laurel Ammons wants to know which activities create value and which support them. The value chain organizes them.

Key ideas
  • The value chain is the set of activities from raw materials to the final customer. Customers' view of value and cost drives every link.
  • Primary processes: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. Support processes: accounting, design, finance, human resources and maintenance.
  • A value chain perspective looks for ways to cut cost and share information with suppliers and customers, not just inside the company.

4. Classifying costs by behavior

Before management can decide where to focus, it needs to know which costs move with volume and which do not.

Key ideas
  • Variable costs change in total in proportion to volume. Fixed costs stay the same in total within the relevant range. Mixed costs have both parts.
  • Variable cost per unit is constant. Fixed cost per unit falls as volume rises.
  • The classification depends on the activity measure and the time horizon.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Examples from the Cullowhee plant:

5. Contribution margin per unit and contribution margin ratio

A line with a high price is not necessarily a high earner. Contribution margin shows what each unit leaves after its own variable costs.

Key ideas
  • Contribution margin per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit.
  • Contribution margin ratio = contribution margin ÷ price (or total contribution margin ÷ net sales).
  • Total contribution margin = units × contribution margin per unit.

6. Segment margin and each line's share of the total

A line can earn a positive contribution margin and still lose money once its own fixed costs are counted.

Key ideas
  • Segment margin = contribution margin − traceable fixed costs.
  • A line's share of total contribution margin or segment margin shows where the profit comes from.
  • A line with a negative segment margin is a candidate for a closer look, not an automatic cut: ask what is avoidable.

7. Ethics, ESG and corporate governance

Managers are under pressure to make the numbers look good. The IMA standards give accountants a guide.

Key ideas
  • The IMA's Statement of Ethical Professional Practice has four standards: competence, confidentiality, integrity and credibility.
  • Ethical problems often grow from small compromises. Recognize the early warning signs and raise the concern.
  • ESG asks companies to balance profit with environmental and social responsibility. Corporate governance is the system that directs and controls the company; Sarbanes-Oxley was enacted in 2002 to strengthen it.
Worked example: Cullowhee

Scenario: a manager asks the accountant to shift $400 thousand of costs from a profitable line to a losing line so that the profitable line looks better.

IMA standardWhat it requires here
