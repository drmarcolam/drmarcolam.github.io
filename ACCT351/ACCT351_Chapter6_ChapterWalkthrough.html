The five-step model and timing of revenue

Revenue recognition answers two questions: how much revenue to record and when to record it. Start with the core principle and the five steps, then decide whether each performance obligation is satisfied at a point in time or over time.

Core principle and the five steps

Companies recognize revenue when goods or services are transferred to customers, in the amount the company expects to be entitled to receive in exchange.

  1. Identify the contract with a customer.
  2. Identify the performance obligation(s) in the contract.
  3. Determine the transaction price.
  4. Allocate the transaction price to each performance obligation.
  5. Recognize revenue when (or as) each performance obligation is satisfied.
  • Performance obligation: a promise to transfer a good or service to a customer. It is satisfied when the customer obtains control.
  • Control indicators (point in time): the customer has an obligation to pay, legal title, physical possession, the risks and rewards of ownership, and has accepted the asset.
  • Over time if any one of three criteria is met: (1) the customer consumes the benefit as the seller performs, (2) the customer controls the asset as it is created, or (3) the asset has no alternative use to the seller and the seller has a right to payment for progress to date.
  • Deferred revenue (a contract liability) is recorded when cash is received before the obligation is satisfied; revenue is recognized as the obligation is satisfied.
  • A contract exists only if it has commercial substance, is approved, identifies rights and payment terms, and collection is probable. Under IFRS, “probable” means more than 50%, a lower bar than U.S. GAAP.

Example 1 — Point in time or over time?

For each situation, decide whether the seller recognizes revenue at a point in time or over time.
  1. Blue Ridge Outfitters in Asheville sells a tent from its shelf, and the customer takes it home today.
  2. Cullowhee Fitness sells a 12-month membership. Members use the gym throughout the year.
  3. Haywood Builders constructs a medical office on the customer’s own land. The customer owns the work in process as it is built.
  4. Smoky Mountain Furniture ships a standard sofa F.O.B. destination. Title and possession pass when the customer receives it.
Need a hint?
Ask: when does the customer obtain control? Over time requires one of the three criteria (benefit consumed as performed, customer controls the asset as created, or no alternative use plus right to payment).

Solution

  • a. Point in time. Control passes at the sale: possession, title, and acceptance all occur today.
  • b. Over time. Criterion 1: the member consumes the benefit as access is provided.
  • c. Over time. Criterion 2: the customer controls the asset as it is created.
  • d. Point in time. With F.O.B. destination terms, control passes on receipt, so revenue waits until delivery.

Contracts with multiple performance obligations

Many contracts bundle goods and services that are satisfied at different times. Separate the distinct pieces, allocate the price using relative stand-alone selling prices, and recognize each piece on its own timeline.

  • Distinct goods or services become separate performance obligations. A good or service is distinct if it is both capable of being distinct (the customer could use it on its own) and separately identifiable (not highly interdependent or customized, and not just an input to a combined output).
  • Transaction price: the amount the seller expects to be entitled to receive from the customer.
  • Allocation: allocate the transaction price in proportion to each obligation’s relative stand-alone selling price (SSP). A bundle discount is spread across the obligations unless evidence ties it to only one.
  • Estimating SSP: adjusted market assessment, expected cost plus margin, or the residual approach (only when the SSP is highly uncertain).
  • Not performance obligations: up-front prepayments and quality-assurance (assurance-type) warranties. Are performance obligations: extended (service-type) warranties and options that give the customer a material right.
Allocation = Transaction price × (SSP of obligation ÷ Sum of all SSPs)

Example 1 — Identifying performance obligations

For each contract, decide whether the promised items are separate performance obligations or one combined performance obligation.
  1. Cullowhee Cable & Internet sells a router and 12 months of internet service. Each is also sold separately, and the router works with other providers.
  2. Haywood Builders agrees to deliver a finished office building, which includes lumber, wiring, fixtures, and labor.
  3. An online service sells access to a clip-art library. The only way to use the clip art is through the service’s website.
Need a hint?
Test both parts: capable of being distinct and separately identifiable in the context of the contract.

Solution

  • a. Separate. Each item is sold on its own and can be used with other providers, so each is capable of being distinct and separately identifiable.
  • b. One combined. The materials are capable of being distinct, but the builder’s promise is to combine them into one finished building, so they are not separately identifiable.
  • c. One combined. The two items are so interdependent that the customer can only use one through the other.

Example 3 — A customer option (material right)

On January 1, , Nantahala Paddle Supply sells 20 kayaks for $300 each in cash. Each kayak includes a coupon for 50% off a $150 paddle, redeemable within one year. Customers do not normally receive this discount, and Nantahala expects 80% of coupons to be redeemed.
Need a hint?
The coupon is a material right, so it is a separate performance obligation. Its stand-alone selling price = discount value × probability of redemption.
$
$
$

Solution

Discount value: $150 × 50% = $75. Expected SSP of the coupon: $75 × 80% = $60.

Total SSP per kayak sale = $300 + $60 = $360. Kayak share = $300 ÷ $360 = 5/6; coupon share = $60 ÷ $360 = 1/6.

Kayaks: 20 × $300 × 5/6 = $5,000 sales revenue. Coupons: 20 × $300 × 1/6 = $1,000 deferred revenue.

Entry: Debit Cash $6,000; credit Sales revenue $5,000; credit Deferred revenue $1,000. Nantahala recognizes the $1,000 when coupons are redeemed or expire.

Special issues in applying the five steps

Real contracts add complications at each step: uncertain prices, returns, who is really the seller, financing, and special arrangements such as licenses and consignment.

  • Variable consideration (bonuses, penalties, rebates, returns) is estimated using the expected value (many possible outcomes) or the most likely amount (two outcomes). The constraint limits revenue to the amount for which a significant reversal is probable not to occur. Estimates are updated each period.
  • Right of return: not a performance obligation. It is variable consideration, so revenue is reported net of estimated returns.
  • Principal vs. agent: a principal controls the good or service before transfer (primary responsibility, pricing discretion, inventory risk) and reports gross revenue. An agent reports only its commission.
  • Time value of money: if financing is significant, split the price into a delivery component (present value) and interest. As a practical matter, ignore financing when payment and delivery are within one year; the examples below use one year to practice the mechanics.
  • Licenses: functional IP (software, music, film) is recognized at a point in time; symbolic IP (brand names, logos, franchise rights) is recognized over the license period.
  • Bill-and-hold and consignment: usually no transfer of control until delivery (bill-and-hold) or sale to the end customer (consignment). Gift cards are deferred revenue until redeemed or redemption becomes remote (breakage).

Example 1 — Variable consideration

Tuckasegee Software signs a six-month contract with Brevard Games on January 1, . Brevard pays a fixed fee of $200,000 plus a $100,000 bonus if players log at least 20,000 hours. Tuckasegee estimates a 60% chance of earning the bonus and has good evidence for that estimate (no constraint applies).
Need a hint?
Expected value = sum of (each possible price × its probability). Most likely amount = the outcome with the higher probability.

Solution

Expected value: ($300,000 × 60%) + ($200,000 × 40%) = $180,000 + $80,000 = $260,000.

Most likely amount: the bonus has a 60% chance, so the more likely price is $200,000 + $100,000 = $300,000.

With only two outcomes, the most likely amount is usually the better predictor. Monthly revenue: $300,000 ÷ 6 = $50,000.

Each period Tuckasegee reassesses the estimate. If the bonus becomes unlikely, it reduces the receivable and records the adjustment in that period’s revenue.

Example 4 — Significant financing component

Brevard Kiln Works agrees to sell a kiln with a cash price of $50,000. The implicit interest rate is 10% and the financing is significant.
Case 1 (receivable): The kiln is delivered on January 1, , and the customer will pay $55,000 one year later.
Case 2 (prepayment): The customer pays $50,000 on January 1, , and the kiln is delivered one year later.
Need a hint?
In Case 1 the seller is lending to the customer; in Case 2 the customer is lending to the seller. Interest accrues at 10% of the balance during the year.

Solution

Case 1. $55,000 ÷ 1.10 = $50,000, so revenue at delivery is $50,000. During the year, interest revenue is $50,000 × 10% = $5,000 and the note grows to $55,000 when collected.

Case 2. Deferred revenue grows from $50,000 to $55,000 as interest expense of $50,000 × 10% = $5,000 accrues, so revenue at delivery is $55,000.

The customer pays less when paying early and more when paying late, which is the compensation for the time value of money.

Example 5 — Licenses, bill-and-hold, consignment, and gift cards

Choose when the seller recognizes revenue in each arrangement.
Need a hint?
Functional IP works on its own. Symbolic IP depends on the seller’s ongoing activities. For goods, ask who controls them and where they physically are.

Solution

  • a. Point in time. Functional IP: the customer can use it as soon as it is available.
  • b. Over the license period. Symbolic IP: the seller’s ongoing activities (games, promotion) affect the value of the license.
  • c. On delivery. The customer lacks physical possession and the SEC conditions for earlier recognition are not met.
  • d. When the consignee sells to an end customer. The consignor keeps title and the risks and rewards of ownership.
  • e. When redeemed (or when redemption is remote). The sale of the card is initially deferred revenue.

Long-term contracts

Construction and other long-term projects usually meet the criteria for revenue over time. The contractor tracks progress with the cost-to-cost ratio, accumulates costs and profit in CIP, and reports a contract asset or liability.

  • Construction in progress (CIP): the contractor’s work-in-process asset. It holds costs incurred plus gross profit (or minus losses) recognized to date.
  • Billings on construction contract: a contra account to CIP that increases when the customer is billed. This prevents double counting the asset and the receivable.
  • Balance sheet: if CIP > billings, report a contract asset (CIP in excess of billings). If billings > CIP, report a contract liability (billings in excess of CIP). Amounts billed but not collected are accounts receivable.
  • Over time (most contracts): use the cost-to-cost measure of progress.
  • Upon completion: if the contract does not qualify for over-time recognition, all revenue and gross profit are deferred until completion. Total profit over the contract is the same under both methods; only timing differs.
  • Losses: a periodic loss on a profitable project is recognized using the same formulas. If an overall loss is projected, the entire loss is recognized immediately, under either method.
Cumulative revenue = Contract price × (Costs to date ÷ Total estimated costs)
Revenue this period = Cumulative revenue − Revenue recognized in prior periods

Tuckasegee Valley Paving has a $2,000,000 contract and recognizes revenue over time.

Total estimated cost at the end of Year 2: $1,150,000 + $1,150,000 = $2,300,000. Expected loss: $2,000,000 − $2,300,000 = ($300,000).

Percentage complete: $1,150,000 ÷ $2,300,000 = 50%. Cumulative revenue: $2,000,000 × 50% = $1,000,000. Year 2 revenue: $1,000,000 − $500,000 (Year 1) = $500,000.

Year 1 recognized a $100,000 profit, and the cumulative result must be a $300,000 loss, so Year 2 recognizes a loss of $300,000 + $100,000 = $400,000. This is treated as a change in estimate; Year 1 is not restated.

Cost of construction = revenue + loss = $500,000 + $400,000 = $900,000.

Under the completion method, no profit was recognized in Year 1, so the whole expected loss of $300,000 is recognized in Year 2 (debit Loss on long-term contracts, credit CIP).

Wrap-up: Chapter 6 at a glance

  • Five steps: identify the contract, identify performance obligations, determine the transaction price, allocate it by relative stand-alone selling prices, and recognize revenue when (or as) each obligation is satisfied.
  • Timing: point in time when control transfers; over time if the customer consumes the benefit, controls the asset as created, or the asset has no alternative use and the seller has a right to payment.
  • Deferred revenue (contract liability) arises when cash is received first. Contract assets arise when work is done but payment depends on more than the passage of time.
  • Special issues: variable consideration and the constraint, returns (net sales), principal vs. agent (gross vs. net), significant financing, licenses, bill-and-hold, consignment, and gift cards.
  • Long-term contracts: cost-to-cost progress, CIP and billings accounts, the same total profit under both methods, and immediate recognition of an overall expected loss.
  • IFRS differences: a lower “probable” threshold for a contract to exist, “highly probable” in the constraint, and a different approach to licenses of symbolic IP.
