The accounting equation, journal entries, and the trial balance

The accounting process captures the effects of transactions with double-entry bookkeeping: journalize, post, and prove the ledger with a trial balance.

  • Accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ equity. Every transaction has a dual effect, so the equation always balances.
  • Debits and credits: assets, expenses, and dividends increase with debits (normal debit balance). Liabilities, equity (common stock, retained earnings), and revenues increase with credits.
  • Processing cycle (10 steps): (1) source documents, (2) analyze, (3) journalize, (4) post to the ledger, (5) unadjusted trial balance, (6) adjusting entries, (7) adjusted trial balance, (8) financial statements, (9) close temporary accounts (year-end), (10) post-closing trial balance (year-end).
  • A trial balance proves that total debits equal total credits. It does not catch errors such as a debit and credit posted to the wrong accounts for the right amount.

Example 3 — The trial balance

Western Peaks Supply prepares an unadjusted trial balance. All accounts have normal balances. The debit accounts are: Cash $40,000, Accounts receivable $15,000, Inventory $30,000, and Equipment $50,000. The credit accounts are: Accounts payable $22,000, Notes payable $40,000, and Common stock $60,000. Retained earnings has an unknown balance.
Need a hint?
Total debits must equal total credits. Add each side, then solve for the missing figure.
$
$

Solution

Total debits: $40,000 + $15,000 + $30,000 + $50,000 = $135,000.

Known credits: $22,000 + $40,000 + $60,000 = $122,000. Retained earnings = $135,000 − $122,000 = $13,000.

A trial balance only proves that debits equal credits. Posting the right amount to the wrong account of the same side leaves it in balance, so the error goes undetected. The other two errors create an imbalance.

Adjusting entries

At the end of each period, adjusting entries bring prepayments, accruals, and estimates up to date so that the financial statements reflect accrual accounting.

  • Adjusting entries make the accounts follow accrual accounting at the end of the period. They are needed for prepayments (deferrals), accruals, and estimates. They never involve the cash account.
  • Prepaid expense: cash paid first, expense later. Adjust: debit expense, credit asset.
  • Deferred revenue: cash received first, revenue later. Adjust: debit liability, credit revenue.
  • Accrued expense: expense incurred, cash paid later. Adjust: debit expense, credit liability. Accrued revenue: revenue earned, cash received later. Adjust: debit receivable, credit revenue.
  • Estimates: depreciation (debit depreciation expense, credit accumulated depreciation) and bad debts.
  • Interest = principal × annual rate × fraction of the year.

Financial statements, closing, and cash-to-accrual conversion

After adjusting, the accounts feed the financial statements. At year-end, temporary accounts are closed, and cash-basis records can be converted to accrual.

  • The adjusted trial balance is the source for the financial statements: income statement, statement of shareholders’ equity, balance sheet, and statement of cash flows.
  • Ending retained earnings = beginning retained earnings + net income − dividends.
  • Closing entries (year-end): (1) close revenues to retained earnings (debit each revenue); (2) close expenses (credit each expense); (3) close dividends. Temporary accounts (revenues, expenses, gains, losses, dividends) go to zero; permanent accounts (assets, liabilities, equity) carry forward.
  • A post-closing trial balance contains only permanent accounts.
  • Converting cash-basis to accrual: Revenue = cash collected + ending receivables − beginning receivables. Expense = cash paid + ending payables − beginning payables (or + beginning prepaid − ending prepaid).

Appendices: reversing entries and special journals

Two optional topics round out the process: reversing entries that simplify the next period, and special journals that streamline high-volume transactions.

  • Reversing entries (optional, first day of the new period) reverse certain accruals so routine entries can be recorded as usual. After reversing and then recording the normal cash payment, the expense for the new period is just the portion that belongs to it.
  • Special journals (sales, cash receipts, purchases, cash disbursements) handle high-volume repetitive transactions; the general journal records everything else, including adjusting and closing entries. Subsidiary ledgers hold detail (for example, each customer) and tie to a control account in the general ledger.

Example 12 — Reversing entries

At December 31, Haywood Supply accrued $6,000 of salaries (see Example 7). On January 3, it pays the full week’s payroll of $10,000 in cash. Assume the company uses reversing entries.
Need a hint?
The reversing entry on January 1 undoes the accrual. Then the payroll is recorded as a normal debit to Salaries expense.

Solution

   Cash10,000

The expense account is credited $6,000 and then debited $10,000, leaving $4,000, which is the two days worked in the new year ($2,000 × 2). The result is the same as without reversing entries, but the January entry is routine.

Wrap-up: Chapter 2 at a glance

  • Equation and entries: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Debits increase assets, expenses, and dividends; credits increase liabilities, equity, and revenues. A trial balance proves debits equal credits but cannot detect every error.
  • Adjusting entries cover prepayments (debit expense / credit asset), deferred revenue (debit liability / credit revenue), accruals, and estimates. They bring the books to accrual accounting.
  • Year-end: prepare statements from the adjusted trial balance, close revenues, expenses, and dividends to retained earnings, and prepare a post-closing trial balance with only permanent accounts.
  • Cash to accrual: adjust cash flows for changes in receivables, payables, and prepaid balances.
  • Appendices: reversing entries simplify routine entries next period; special journals and subsidiary ledgers handle high-volume transactions.
