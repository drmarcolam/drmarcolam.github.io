Depreciation methods

Each method allocates cost over the useful life; they differ in how much expense is recognized in each period.

  • Depreciation allocates the cost of PP&E (less residual value) to the periods that benefit. Depletion applies to natural resources; amortization applies to intangible assets. None of these is a valuation process; each is cost allocation.
  • Depreciable base = cost − residual (salvage) value.
  • Straight-line: (cost − residual) ÷ service life. Sum-of-the-years’-digits (SYD): depreciable base × (remaining life ÷ sum of years’ digits). Double-declining-balance (DDB): 2 ÷ life × beginning book value (ignore residual value, but never depreciate below residual). Units of production (activity-based): (cost − residual) ÷ total estimated units × units this period.
  • Accelerated methods (SYD, DDB) record more depreciation in the early years.

Partial years and dispositions

When assets are acquired or sold mid-year, depreciation must be recorded for the part of the year the asset was held.

  • Partial-year depreciation: multiply annual depreciation by the fraction of the year the asset was held. For accelerated methods, compute full-year amounts for each year of the asset’s life, then allocate to calendar years.
  • Disposition: (1) record depreciation up to the date of sale; (2) remove the cost and accumulated depreciation; (3) record cash received and a gain or loss = proceeds − book value.
  • Gain if proceeds exceed book value; loss if proceeds are less than book value.

Depletion, amortization, and changes

Natural resources and intangibles use depletion and amortization; estimates change over time.

  • Depletion = (cost + restoration cost − residual value) ÷ estimated recoverable units × units extracted. Depletion of units sold goes to cost of goods sold; units unsold remain in inventory.
  • Amortization of a finite-life intangible: cost ÷ useful life (usually straight-line), over the shorter of legal and useful life. Indefinite-life intangibles (and goodwill) are not amortized but tested for impairment.
  • Change in estimate (useful life or residual value): prospective. New depreciation = (book value − new residual) ÷ remaining life. Change in method: a change in estimate effected by a change in principle; also prospective. Error (for example, using the wrong amount): restate prior periods.

Impairment, subsequent expenditures, and IFRS

Long-lived assets must be written down when their carrying amounts are not recoverable.

  • Impairment of PP&E and finite-life intangibles (U.S. GAAP): Step 1: if events suggest the carrying amount may not be recoverable, compare the carrying amount with the undiscounted expected future cash flows. If the carrying amount is higher, the asset is impaired. Step 2: loss = carrying amount − fair value. The new carrying amount becomes the cost basis; no reversal.
  • Indefinite-life intangibles and goodwill are tested at least annually. Loss on indefinite-life intangibles = carrying amount − fair value. Goodwill impairment = carrying amount of the reporting unit − fair value of the unit, limited to the goodwill balance.
  • Assets held for sale: report at the lower of carrying amount and fair value less cost to sell; stop depreciating.
  • Subsequent expenditures: ordinary repairs and maintenance are expensed; additions, improvements (increasing capacity or efficiency), and extraordinary repairs that extend useful life are capitalized; rearrangements are capitalized if they provide future benefit.
  • IFRS: one-step impairment (carrying amount vs. recoverable amount = higher of fair value less cost to sell and value in use); reversals are allowed (not for goodwill); component depreciation required; revaluation model permitted.

Example 10 — Impairment of equipment (U.S. GAAP)

Mount Pisgah Plastics tests a production line after a sharp fall in demand. Carrying amount: $800,000. Expected undiscounted future cash flows: $700,000. Fair value: $520,000.
Need a hint?
Step 1: compare the carrying amount with undiscounted cash flows. Step 2: measure the loss against fair value.
$

Solution

$800,000 > $700,000, so the asset is impaired. Loss = $800,000 − $520,000 = $280,000. The adjusted carrying amount ($520,000) is the new cost basis, and reversals are not allowed.

Part 3: A building held for sale has a carrying amount of $500,000, fair value of $450,000, and costs to sell of $20,000.
Need a hint?
Goodwill loss is limited to the goodwill balance. Held-for-sale assets are reported at the lower of carrying amount and fair value less cost to sell.

Solution

Wrap-up: Chapter 11 at a glance

  • Depreciation allocates (cost − residual) over the useful life using straight-line, SYD, DDB, or units of production. Depletion and amortization follow the same idea for natural resources and intangibles.
  • Partial years and sales: record depreciation to the sale date, remove cost and accumulated depreciation, and recognize gain or loss.
  • Changes in estimate and method are prospective; errors require restatement.
  • Impairment (U.S. GAAP): compare carrying amount with undiscounted cash flows, then measure the loss against fair value. No reversal. Goodwill and indefinite-life intangibles are tested annually.
  • IFRS permits impairment reversals (not goodwill), requires component depreciation, and permits revaluation.
