Internal control and cash

Cash is the most liquid and most easily misappropriated asset, so controls and reconciliations are essential.

  • Internal control is a process designed to provide reasonable assurance about reliable financial reporting, effective and efficient operations, and compliance. Key elements: control environment, risk assessment, control activities, information and communication, and monitoring.
  • Cash controls: separate custody of cash from record keeping and authorization; deposit receipts intact and promptly; make payments by check or electronic transfer with proper approval; prepare a bank reconciliation monthly by someone independent.
  • Cash and cash equivalents are reported together. Restricted cash is classified as current or noncurrent depending on the timing of the restriction. A compensating balance is disclosed (and classified based on the related debt).
  • Bank reconciliation: adjust the bank balance for deposits in transit (+), outstanding checks (−), and bank errors; adjust the book balance for items the bank recorded that the company has not (service charges, NSF checks, interest earned, notes collected) and for book errors. Only book-side items require journal entries.

Trade receivables: discounts, returns, and uncollectible accounts

Receivables are reported at net realizable value, the amount the company expects to collect.

  • Trade discounts reduce list price (sales are recorded net of them). Cash (sales) discounts encourage prompt payment, for example 2/10, n/30.
  • Gross method: record the sale at the invoice amount; record a sales discount only if taken. Net method: record the sale net of the discount; if the customer pays late, the discount not taken is recorded as revenue (sales discounts forfeited).
  • Returns: estimate expected returns at the time of sale. Record revenue only for the amount expected to be kept, a refund liability for the expected refund, and an asset for the right to the returned goods (at cost, less expected costs to recover).
  • Uncollectible accounts (CECL): estimate expected credit losses over the life of the receivable when recorded. Use the allowance method: debit bad debt expense, credit allowance for uncollectible accounts. Write-off: debit allowance, credit accounts receivable (no expense).
  • Estimation: percentage of credit sales (income statement approach: the amount is the expense) or aging / percentage of receivables (balance sheet approach: the amount is the required ending allowance; adjust for the existing balance).

Notes receivable and financing with receivables

Notes receivable earn interest; receivables can also be sold or pledged to obtain cash sooner.

  • Interest-bearing note: record at face value. Interest = face × annual rate × time. Accrue interest at year-end (debit interest receivable, credit interest revenue).
  • Noninterest-bearing note: the face amount includes interest. Record the note at present value using the market rate; the discount is amortized into interest revenue.
  • Financing with receivables: a secured borrowing (pledging or assigning) keeps the receivables on the books and records a liability. A sale (factoring) removes the receivables when control is transferred; record any fee or loss. A holdback becomes a receivable from the factor.
  • Discounting a note at a bank generates proceeds equal to maturity value less a discount (maturity value × bank rate × time remaining).

Analysis and IFRS

Ratios reveal how effectively a company collects its receivables.

  • Receivables turnover = net credit sales ÷ average net accounts receivable. Average collection period = 365 ÷ receivables turnover. Faster turnover indicates quicker collection.
  • Credit policies, collection effectiveness, and the allowance affect the investment in receivables.
  • IFRS: impairment uses an expected credit loss model (12-month or lifetime, depending on credit deterioration). Other differences exist for cash equivalents and compensating balances.

Wrap-up: Chapter 7 at a glance

  • Internal control and cash: segregate duties, reconcile bank accounts, and classify restricted cash by the timing of the restriction.
  • Discounts and returns: the gross method records discounts when taken; the net method records forfeited discounts as revenue. Returns create a refund liability and an asset for the goods expected back.
  • Uncollectibles: percentage of sales gives the expense; aging gives the required ending allowance. Write-offs do not change net receivables.
  • Notes and financing: interest = face × rate × time; noninterest-bearing notes are recorded at present value; factoring without recourse is a sale.
  • Analysis: receivables turnover and the average collection period monitor the investment in receivables.
