Single amounts: future value and present value

Compound interest drives the time value of money. Future and present value of a single amount are the building blocks for everything else.

  • Time value of money: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future because it can earn interest.
  • Simple interest = principal × rate × time. Compound interest earns interest on the principal and on previously earned interest.
  • Future value of a single amount: FV = PV × (1 + i)n. Present value of a single amount: PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)n.
  • With more frequent compounding, use i = annual rate ÷ periods per year and n = years × periods per year.
  • To solve for an unknown i or n, compute the factor (FV ÷ PV, or PV ÷ FV) and locate it in the table or use a calculator.

Annuities, deferred annuities, and perpetuities

Many financial arrangements involve a series of equal payments. Annuity factors save us from valuing each payment separately.

  • An annuity is a series of equal payments at equal intervals. In an ordinary annuity, payments are at the end of each period. In an annuity due, payments are at the beginning.
  • FV of an ordinary annuity = PMT × [((1 + i)n − 1) ÷ i]. PV of an ordinary annuity = PMT × [(1 − (1 + i)−n) ÷ i].
  • Annuity due = ordinary annuity × (1 + i), for both FV and PV.
  • Deferred annuity: the first payment starts more than one period after today. PV = PV of the annuity at the start of payments, discounted back for the deferral period.
  • Perpetuity: PV = payment ÷ i.

Applications: valuing notes and expected cash flows

Present value techniques measure notes, leases, bonds, and other long-term obligations at their economic value.

  • Valuing a note: a long-term note is recorded at the present value of its cash flows, discounted at the effective (market) rate.
  • If the stated rate equals the market rate, the note sells at face value. If the stated rate is lower than the market rate, the note sells at a discount; if higher, a premium. Interest expense = carrying amount × market rate.
  • Noninterest-bearing note: record the receivable/payable at present value; the difference from the face amount is a discount amortized to interest.
  • Expected cash flow approach: weight each possible cash flow by its probability, then discount at a risk-free rate.
  • For leases and pensions, the same present value techniques apply.

Choosing a technique

Most time value problems come down to choosing the right technique and the right number of periods.

  • Identify whether the cash flows are a lump sum or an annuity, the timing (beginning or end), and the valuation date.
  • Match the periods and rate to the compounding frequency.

Wrap-up: Chapter 5 at a glance

  • Compound interest earns interest on interest. FV = PV(1 + i)n; PV = FV ÷ (1 + i)n.
  • Annuities are equal payments at equal intervals. An annuity due equals the ordinary annuity × (1 + i). A deferred annuity is discounted back for the deferral period; a perpetuity is PMT ÷ i.
  • Notes are recorded at the present value of their cash flows at the market rate. Interest expense equals carrying amount × market rate.
  • Expected cash flows weight outcomes by probability before discounting.
