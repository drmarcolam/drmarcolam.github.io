Cost classifications

Direct vs. Indirect, and Product vs. Period Costs

Nantahala Kayak Works in Bryson City, North Carolina, builds fiberglass kayaks. Every cost is either a product cost (direct materials, direct labor, manufacturing overhead) or a period cost (selling and administrative). Product costs sit in inventory until the product is sold; period costs go straight to the income statement.

A cost is direct if it can be easily and conveniently traced to the cost object (here, a kayak). It is indirect if it cannot. Manufacturing overhead = all manufacturing costs other than direct materials and direct labor.
Example 1 — Classify the Cost

Classify each Nantahala cost into its cost category.

Need a hint?
Ask whether the cost is incurred in the factory or outside it, and whether it can be traced to a single kayak.
Solution
  • Fiberglass cloth: direct materials, because it is an integral part of the kayak and easy to trace.
  • Layup and finishing wages: direct labor, or "touch labor" that is easily traced.
  • Production supervisor: manufacturing overhead, an indirect factory cost.
  • Sales commissions: selling cost, a period cost.
  • Controller: administrative cost, a period cost.
Example 2 — Product Cost or Period Cost?

Decide whether each cost is a product cost or a period cost.

Solution

Factory costs (depreciation on factory equipment, factory utilities) are manufacturing overhead and therefore product costs. Selling and administrative costs (vehicles for sales staff, advertising) are period costs expensed when incurred.

Cost behavior

Cost Behavior: Variable, Fixed and Mixed

Nantahala's unit costs are: direct materials $18, direct labor $12, variable manufacturing overhead $5. Fixed manufacturing overhead is $90,000 per year, and the company expects to produce 6,000 kayaks.

  • Variable cost: total changes in proportion to activity; cost per unit is constant.
  • Fixed cost: total is constant within the relevant range; cost per unit falls as activity rises.
  • Mixed cost: has both elements: Y = a + bX (a = fixed part, b = variable rate, X = activity).
Example 1 — Variable Manufacturing Cost per Unit

What is the variable manufacturing cost per kayak?

Need a hint?
Add direct materials, direct labor, and variable manufacturing overhead.
$
Solution

$18 + $12 + $5 = $35 per kayak.

Variable manufacturing cost = $35 per kayak

Example 2 — Total Manufacturing Cost

What is the total manufacturing (product) cost of producing 6,000 kayaks?

Need a hint?
Total variable manufacturing cost plus total fixed manufacturing overhead.
$
Solution

Variable: 6,000 × $35 = $210,000.
Fixed: $90,000.
Total: $210,000 + $90,000 = $300,000.

Total manufacturing cost = $300,000

Example 3 — Fixed Cost per Unit

At 6,000 kayaks, fixed overhead is $15 per kayak. If production rises to 7,500 kayaks (still within the relevant range), what is the fixed manufacturing overhead per kayak?

$
Solution

Total fixed overhead stays at $90,000. $90,000 ÷ 7,500 = $12 per kayak.

Fixed overhead per kayak = $12

Per-unit fixed cost falls as volume rises. That is why unit costs built from fixed costs are valid for only one level of activity.
Example 4 — Total Variable Cost

What is the total variable manufacturing cost at 7,500 kayaks?

$
Solution

7,500 × $35 = $262,500.

Total variable manufacturing cost = $262,500

Example 5 — A Mixed Cost

The factory's electric bill has a fixed monthly service charge of $2,000 plus $0.60 per machine-hour. What will the bill be at 5,000 machine-hours?

Need a hint?
Use Y = a + bX.
$
Solution

Y = $2,000 + ($0.60 × 5,000) = $2,000 + $3,000 = $5,000.

Electric bill = $5,000

Income statements

Traditional vs. Contribution Format Income Statements

Nantahala produced and sold 8,000 kayaks at $60 each. Variable selling and administrative cost is $4 per kayak, and fixed selling and administrative cost is $50,000. Use the cost data from the previous section.

  • Traditional (absorption) format: Sales − Cost of goods sold = Gross margin; − Selling and administrative expenses = Net operating income.
  • Contribution format: Sales − All variable expenses = Contribution margin; − All fixed expenses = Net operating income.
Example 1 — Gross Margin (Traditional Format)

Cost of goods sold includes all manufacturing costs: variable ($35 per unit) and fixed ($90,000). What is Nantahala's gross margin?

Need a hint?
Sales = 8,000 × $60. COGS = 8,000 × $35 + $90,000.
$
Solution
Traditional format
Amount
Sales$480,000
Cost of goods sold$370,000
Gross margin$110,000
Selling and administrative expenses$82,000
Net operating income$28,000

Gross margin = $110,000

Example 2 — Contribution Margin

Using the contribution format, what is the contribution margin? (Remember that variable selling and administrative cost is deducted too.)

Need a hint?
Variable expenses per kayak = $35 + $4.
$
Solution
Contribution format
Amount
Sales$480,000
Variable expenses$312,000
Contribution margin$168,000
Fixed expenses$140,000
Net operating income$28,000

Contribution margin = $168,000

Example 3 — Net Operating Income

What is net operating income? It should come out the same under either format.

$
Solution

Traditional: $110,000 − $82,000 = $28,000.
Contribution: $168,000 − $140,000 = $28,000.

Net operating income = $28,000

The two formats organize costs differently (by function vs. by behavior) but always produce the same net operating income. The contribution format is more useful for decisions because it separates variable from fixed costs.
Decision concepts

Differential, Sunk and Opportunity Costs

  • Differential cost: a cost that differs between alternatives (it can be fixed or variable).
  • Sunk cost: already incurred and cannot be changed by any future decision, so it is never differential.
  • Opportunity cost: the benefit given up when one alternative is chosen over another.
Example 1 — Name That Cost

Nantahala is deciding whether to replace its old gel-coat press. Classify each item.

Solution

The book value is a past outlay that cannot be recovered or changed by today's decision (sunk). Operating costs that differ between the alternatives are differential. Rent given up by keeping the press in that space is an opportunity cost.

Example 2 — Annual Savings from Replacing the Press

The old press costs $50,000 a year to operate. The new press would cost $38,000 a year to operate plus $9,000 a year in lease payments. By how much would annual cost decrease if Nantahala replaces the press? Ignore the old press's book value.

Need a hint?
Compare the total annual cost of each alternative and subtract. Book value is sunk.
$
Solution

New press: $38,000 + $9,000 = $47,000. Old press: $50,000.
Differential cost: $50,000 − $47,000 = $3,000 saved per year.

Annual savings = $3,000

The $20,000 book value is sunk and plays no role in the comparison.
Wrap-up

Chapter 1 at a Glance

  • Product costs: direct materials, direct labor and manufacturing overhead. Period costs: selling and administrative.
  • Behavior: total variable cost rises with activity while unit variable cost stays constant; total fixed cost stays constant while unit fixed cost falls.
  • Income statements: traditional format is organized by function, contribution format by behavior. Both give the same net operating income.
  • Decisions: only differential costs and benefits matter. Sunk costs never do.
