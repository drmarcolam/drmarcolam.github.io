Setting Standards and the Standard Cost Card

Nantahala Rain Gear in Franklin, North Carolina, makes rain jackets. A standard has two parts: a quantity standard (how much input should be used) and a price standard (what the input should cost).

Standard cost card for one jacket
InputQuantity standardPrice standardStandard cost
Fabric2.5 yards$8.00 per yard$20.00
Direct labor1.2 hours$15.00 per hour$18.00
Variable overhead1.2 hours$4.00 per hour$4.80
Standard cost per jacket$42.80

Last month Nantahala produced 5,000 jackets. Actual results: 13,200 yards of fabric were purchased and used at $7.70 per yard; 6,300 direct labor-hours were worked at $15.40 per hour; actual variable overhead was $27,090.

Example 1 — Standard Quantity Allowed

How many yards of fabric are allowed for 5,000 jackets?

Need a hint?
Standard quantity per unit × actual output.
yards
Solution

5,000 × 2.5 = 12,500 yards.

Standard quantity allowed = 12,500 yards

Standard quantity allowed is based on actual output, not planned output.
Example 2 — Standard Hours Allowed

How many direct labor-hours are allowed for 5,000 jackets?

hours
Solution

5,000 × 1.2 = 6,000 hours.

Standard hours allowed = 6,000 hours

Materials

Materials Price and Quantity Variances

  • Materials price variance = AQ × (AP − SP)
  • Materials quantity variance = SP × (AQ − SQ)
A positive result is unfavorable (U); a negative result is favorable (F).
Example 1 — Materials Price Variance

What is the materials price variance? Enter the amount (without F or U).

Need a hint?
Actual price is $7.70 versus the $8.00 standard.
$
Solution

13,200 × ($7.70 − $8.00) = 13,200 × (−$0.30) = −$3,960, which is favorable.

Materials price variance = $3,960 F

Example 2 — Materials Quantity Variance

What is the materials quantity variance? Enter the amount.

Need a hint?
Compare yards actually used (13,200) with yards allowed (12,500), at the standard price.
$
Solution

$8.00 × (13,200 − 12,500) = $8.00 × 700 = $5,600, unfavorable.

Materials quantity variance = $5,600 U

Example 3 — Favorable or Unfavorable?

Label each variance.

Solution

Paying less per yard than the standard is favorable. Using more yards than the standard allows for the output is unfavorable. Together these may signal that lower-priced fabric was purchased but wasted more in cutting.

Labor

Labor Rate and Efficiency Variances

  • Labor rate variance = AH × (AR − SR)
  • Labor efficiency variance = SR × (AH − SH)
Example 1 — Labor Rate Variance

What is the labor rate variance? Enter the amount.

$
Solution

6,300 × ($15.40 − $15.00) = 6,300 × $0.40 = $2,520, unfavorable.

Labor rate variance = $2,520 U

Example 2 — Labor Efficiency Variance

What is the labor efficiency variance? Enter the amount.

Need a hint?
Compare actual hours with standard hours allowed, at the standard rate.
$
Solution

$15.00 × (6,300 − 6,000) = $15.00 × 300 = $4,500, unfavorable.

Labor efficiency variance = $4,500 U

Variable overhead

Variable Overhead Variances

Variable overhead is applied on direct labor-hours, so the standard rate is $4.00 per hour. Actual variable overhead was $27,090 for 6,300 hours.

  • Variable overhead rate variance = AH × (AR − SR)
  • Variable overhead efficiency variance = SR × (AH − SH)
Example 1 — Actual Variable Overhead Rate

What was the actual variable overhead rate per direct labor-hour?

$ per hour
Solution

$27,090 ÷ 6,300 hours = $4.30 per hour.

Actual rate = $4.30 per hour

Example 2 — Variable Overhead Rate Variance

What is the variable overhead rate variance? Enter the amount.

$
Solution

6,300 × ($4.30 − $4.00) = $1,890, unfavorable.

Variable overhead rate variance = $1,890 U

Example 3 — Variable Overhead Efficiency Variance

What is the variable overhead efficiency variance? Enter the amount.

$
Solution

$4.00 × (6,300 − 6,000) = $1,200, unfavorable.

Variable overhead efficiency variance = $1,200 U

Because overhead is applied on direct labor-hours, the overhead efficiency and labor efficiency variances always move together: both are unfavorable here because more hours were worked than standard.

Example 2 — Who Is Responsible?

Match each variance with the manager who usually has the most control over it.

Solution

The purchasing manager controls what is paid for materials. Production managers control how much material and labor are used. Standards should be used to find causes and fix problems, not to assign blame.

Wrap-up

Chapter 10 at a Glance

  • Price variances: (actual price − standard price) × actual quantity. Quantity/efficiency variances: (actual − standard allowed) × standard price.
  • Standard quantity/hours allowed are based on actual output.
  • Positive results (actual higher) are unfavorable for costs; negative are favorable.
  • The individual variances add up to the total variance.
