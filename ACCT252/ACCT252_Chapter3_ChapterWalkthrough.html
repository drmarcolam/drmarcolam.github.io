Predetermined rate

The Predetermined Overhead Rate

Brasstown Custom Furniture in Brasstown, North Carolina, builds one-of-a-kind furniture for clients, so it uses job-order costing: each job gets its own cost sheet with direct materials, direct labor and applied manufacturing overhead.

Overhead can't be known until the year ends, so jobs are charged overhead with a predetermined overhead rate set before the year starts:

Predetermined overhead rate = Estimated total manufacturing overhead ÷ Estimated total amount of the allocation base
Overhead applied to a job = Predetermined rate × Actual amount of the allocation base used by the job

Brasstown's plantwide allocation base is direct labor-hours (DLH). It estimates fixed manufacturing overhead of $120,000 plus variable overhead of $2.50 per DLH, and expects 20,000 direct labor-hours for the year.

Example 1 — Estimated Total Overhead

Use Y = a + bX to estimate total manufacturing overhead for the year.

Need a hint?
Fixed overhead plus variable rate × estimated DLH.
$
Solution

Y = $120,000 + ($2.50 × 20,000) = $120,000 + $50,000 = $170,000.

Estimated overhead = $170,000

Example 2 — The Rate

What is the plantwide predetermined overhead rate per direct labor-hour?

$ per DLH
Solution

$170,000 ÷ 20,000 DLH = $8.50 per DLH.

Rate = $8.50 per DLH

Example 3 — Why Predetermined?

Why not just wait and use actual overhead and actual hours?

Solution

A predetermined rate lets a company cost jobs and quote prices during the year. It does not guarantee profit, and because it is based on estimates, applied overhead will usually differ from actual overhead.

Job cost

Costing a Job

Job 301 is a set of 10 dining chairs. It used $9,000 of direct materials (lumber and hardware), and 500 direct labor-hours at $18 per hour.

Example 1 — Applied Overhead

How much manufacturing overhead is applied to Job 301?

Need a hint?
Predetermined rate × actual DLH on the job.
$
Solution

500 DLH × $8.50 = $4,250.

Applied overhead = $4,250

Over / underapplied

Underapplied and Overapplied Overhead

At year-end Brasstown's records show actual manufacturing overhead of $175,000 and 20,500 actual direct labor-hours.

Overhead applied = Predetermined rate × Actual hours. If actual overhead > applied, overhead is underapplied. If applied > actual, it is overapplied.
Example 1 — Overhead Applied for the Year

How much overhead was applied to jobs during the year?

$
Solution

20,500 DLH × $8.50 = $174,250.

Applied overhead = $174,250

Example 2 — The Difference

Is overhead underapplied or overapplied, and by how much? Enter the amount of the difference; the solution explains the direction.

Need a hint?
Compare actual overhead with applied overhead.
$
Solution

Actual $175,000 − applied $174,250 = $750. Applied was less than actual, so overhead is underapplied by $750.

Underapplied by $750

Underapplied overhead means jobs were under-costed. It is usually closed to cost of goods sold, which reduces income.

Solution

Example 2 — Job 301 Under Two Rates

Job 301 used 150 machine-hours in Cutting and 300 direct labor-hours in Finishing. How much overhead is applied to Job 301 using departmental rates?

Need a hint?
Apply each department's rate to the job's activity in that department, then add.
$
Solution

Cutting: 150 × $16.00 = $2,400.
Finishing: 300 × $9.00 = $2,700.
Total: $5,100.

Overhead applied = $5,100

Example 3 — New Unit Cost

Using that overhead and the same direct materials and direct labor as before, what is Job 301's new unit product cost per chair?

$
Solution

Total: $9,000 + $9,000 + $5,100 = $23,100. ÷ 10 = $2,310 per chair (up from $2,225).

Unit product cost = $2,310

Departmental rates better reflect jobs that use departments differently. Job 301 is machine-intensive, so it picks up more overhead than under the single plantwide rate.
Wrap-up

Chapter 3 at a Glance

  • Rate = estimated overhead ÷ estimated base. Applied = rate × actual base used.
  • Job cost = direct materials + direct labor + applied overhead; unit cost = job cost ÷ units.
  • Underapplied = actual > applied; overapplied = applied > actual.
  • Multiple rates can improve accuracy when departments use overhead differently.
