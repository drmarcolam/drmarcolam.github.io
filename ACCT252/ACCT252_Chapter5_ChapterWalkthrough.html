The five steps of ABC. This walkthrough follows them in order:
  1. Define activities, activity cost pools, and activity measures.
  2. Assign overhead costs to the activity cost pools (first-stage allocation).
  3. Calculate activity rates.
  4. Assign overhead costs to cost objects (second-stage allocation).
  5. Prepare management reports (product and customer margins).
LO 5-1 · Step 1

How ABC Differs from Traditional Costing

Traditional absorption costing is designed for external reports. ABC is designed for internal decisions, and it differs in three ways:

  1. It can assign nonmanufacturing costs to products, but only when the product causes the cost.
  2. It leaves some manufacturing costs out of product costs: organization-sustaining costs and the cost of unused (idle) capacity.
  3. It uses many overhead cost pools, each with its own activity measure, instead of one plantwide rate or a few departmental rates.

ABC sorts activities into five levels. Only the first level moves with the number of units produced.

The five levels of activity
LevelWhat it meansExamples
Unit-levelPerformed every time a unit is producedMachine time to run each unit
Batch-levelPerformed every time a batch is handled, regardless of batch sizeSetting up a machine; processing a customer order
Product-levelSupports a specific product, however many units or batchesDesigning a product; product manager's salary
Customer-levelSupports a specific customer, not tied to a productSales calls; customer technical support
Organization-sustainingCarried out no matter what is produced or soldHeating the plant; the plant manager's salary
Example 1 — Classify the Activity

Cataloochee's ABC team is deciding which pool each activity belongs in. Choose the activity level for each item.

Need a hint?
Ask: does this work happen once per unit, once per batch or order, for a product, for a customer, or no matter what the company does?
Solution
  • Setting up the router for one customer's cabinet order: Batch-level. A setup happens once per order or batch, whether the batch is 1 cabinet or 100.
  • Engineering a new cabinet door profile: Product-level. Design work supports a specific product, regardless of how many units or batches follow.
  • Sanding each cabinet as it comes off the line: Unit-level. The work is repeated for every unit, so the cost rises in proportion to units produced.
  • Paying the plant manager's salary: Organization-sustaining. The salary is the same no matter which products, batches or customers the plant serves.
  • A sales representative's quarterly visit to a builder: Customer-level. The visit supports a customer relationship and is not tied to any one product.
Example 2 — What ABC Leaves Out

Under ABC, how should Cataloochee treat the plant manager's salary, an organization-sustaining cost?

Solution

The salary is unaffected by which products are made, so assigning it to products would be arbitrary. ABC puts organization-sustaining costs and the cost of unused capacity in an Other pool and treats them as period expenses.

Not assigned to products (period expense)

LO 5-2 · Step 2

First-Stage Allocation to Activity Cost Pools

The general ledger lists costs by department or type, not by activity. In the first-stage allocation, each overhead cost is spread across the activity cost pools using the percentages from employee interviews:

Cost allocated to a pool = Total cost of the resource × Percentage consumed by that pool

LO 5-3 · Step 3

Computing Activity Rates

An activity rate is the cost of performing one unit of an activity. Divide each pool's total cost (from the first-stage allocation) by its total expected activity:

Activity rate = Total cost in the pool ÷ Total activity for that pool

No rate is computed for the Other pool, because those costs are not assigned to cost objects. Round only at the end if a rate does not divide evenly.

Example 1 — Customer Orders Rate

The Customer orders pool has $145,000 of cost, and Cataloochee expects to process 500 customer orders. What is the activity rate per order?

Need a hint?
Total cost ÷ total orders.
$ per order
Solution

$145,000 ÷ 500 orders = $290 per order.

Rate = $290 per order

LO 5-4 · Step 4

Second-Stage Allocation to Cost Objects

In the second-stage allocation, activity rates are multiplied by the amount of each activity a product or customer actually used:

Overhead assigned = Σ (Activity rate × Activity used by the cost object)

LO 5-5 · Step 5

Product and Customer Margins

The ABC allocations only cover indirect costs, so a margin report must also include sales and the direct costs that can be traced to the cost object:

Margin = Sales − Direct materials − Direct labor − ABC overhead assigned to that cost object

LO 5-1 · Comparison

Traditional Costing vs. ABC

Cataloochee's traditional system assigns only manufacturing overhead (indirect factory wages and factory depreciation), using one plantwide rate based on machine-hours. Administrative salaries are never assigned to products.

Plantwide rate = Total manufacturing overhead ÷ Total machine-hours
Example 1 — The Plantwide Rate

Manufacturing overhead is $400,000 + $200,000. Total machine-hours are 12,000. What is the plantwide overhead rate per machine-hour?

$ per MH
Solution

($400,000 + $200,000) ÷ 12,000 MHs = $600,000 ÷ 12,000 = $50 per MH.

Plantwide rate = $50 per MH

Example 2 — Traditional Product Margin

Custom cabinets used 3,000 machine-hours. Under the traditional system, what is the product margin for Custom? (Sales $600,000, direct materials $150,000, direct labor $90,000.)

Need a hint?
Overhead applied = rate × Custom's machine-hours. Then subtract it with the direct costs from sales.
$
Solution

Overhead applied: 3,000 MHs × $50 = $150,000.
Margin: $600,000 − $150,000 − $90,000 − $150,000 = $210,000.

Example 3 — How Much Does ABC Shift?

How much more overhead does ABC assign to Custom cabinets than the traditional system does?

Need a hint?
Compare the ABC overhead for Custom with the traditional overhead for Custom.
$
Solution

ABC: $254,000. Traditional: $150,000. Difference: $254,000 − $150,000 = $104,000.

ABC assigns $104,000 more overhead to Custom cabinets

The pattern: The traditional system spreads overhead by machine-hours, a volume measure, so high-volume Standard cabinets absorb most of it. Custom cabinets use few machine-hours but trigger product design and extra order handling, so ABC moves cost onto them. The Custom margin falls from $210,000 to $106,000, while Standard rises from $400,000 to $509,000. Net operating income is the same under both systems ($310,000); only the distribution among products changes.

Caution. ABC costs are only potentially relevant. Before acting (for example, repricing or dropping Custom), managers must decide which of these costs would actually go away, since unavoidable costs should be ignored. ABC is also costly to build and maintain, which is why it supplements, rather than replaces, the system used for external reports.

LO 5-6, 5-7 · Appendix 5A

Time-Driven ABC and Capacity Analysis

Time-driven ABC (TDABC) skips the employee interviews. Instead it prices a minute of capacity and multiplies by the minutes each activity takes. Ask your instructor whether Appendix 5A is part of your assignment; the page is here either way.

Three steps:
  1. Cost per minute = Total cost of resources supplied ÷ Practical capacity in minutes
  2. Time-driven activity rate = Minutes per unit of the activity × Cost per minute
  3. Cost assigned = Time-driven rate × Number of activities

Highlands Outfitters has a customer service department with 12 employees, each paid $46,800 a year. Each employee has 2,400 minutes available per week for 52 weeks, and about 75% of that time is practical capacity (the rest goes to breaks, training, etc.). Activities: order processing (8 min), customer inquiry (20 min), return handling (35 min).

Practical capacity per employee = 52 × 2,400 × 75% = 93,600 minutes. Practical capacity of the department = 93,600 × 12 = 1,123,200 minutes.

Need a hint?

$ per minute
Solution

$561,600 ÷ 1,123,200 minutes = $0.50 per minute.

Wrap-up

Chapter 5 at a Glance

  • ABC vs. traditional: ABC includes nonmanufacturing costs caused by products, excludes organization-sustaining and idle-capacity costs, and uses many pools with unique activity measures.
  • Five levels: unit, batch, product, customer, organization-sustaining. Only unit-level costs move with production volume.
  • First stage: cost × interview percentage → pools. Rates: pool cost ÷ pool activity. Second stage: rate × activity used.
  • Reports: product margins exclude customer-level and organization-sustaining costs; customer margins include customer-level costs.
  • Limits: ABC is costly, produces numbers that differ from the external system, and only shows potentially relevant costs.
