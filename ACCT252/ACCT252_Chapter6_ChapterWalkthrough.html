Relevant costs

Relevant Costs and Benefits

Cherokee Canoe & Paddle in Cherokee, North Carolina, faces several decisions. In every one, only relevant costs and benefits matter.

A cost or benefit is relevant if it (1) is a future cost or benefit and (2) differs between the alternatives. Sunk costs and costs/benefits that are the same under every alternative are irrelevant.
Example 1 — Relevant or Irrelevant?

Cherokee is deciding whether to keep or drop its paddle line. Classify each item.

Need a hint?
Ask: will this cost change depending on which alternative I choose?
Solution

Sunk costs can't be changed. Allocated costs that continue regardless of the decision don't differ between alternatives. Costs that go away if paddles are dropped (variable costs, avoidable fixed costs) differ between alternatives and are relevant.

Make or buy

Make or Buy (Outsourcing)

Cherokee makes 10,000 grab-handle assemblies a year. Making each costs direct materials $6, direct labor $4 and variable overhead $2. In addition, a supervisor salary of $30,000 would be eliminated if production stopped, and $50,000 of allocated general factory overhead ($5 per unit) would continue regardless. An outside supplier offers the part at $14 each.

Example 1 — Relevant Cost to Make

What is the total relevant cost of making 10,000 units?

Need a hint?
Include variable costs and the avoidable supervisor. Exclude the allocated overhead that continues.
$
Solution

Variable: 10,000 × ($6 + $4 + $2) = $120,000.
Avoidable supervisor: $30,000.
Relevant cost to make: $150,000.

Relevant cost to make = $150,000

Example 2 — Advantage of Buying

What is the cost advantage of buying ($14 × 10,000 units) compared with making?

$
Solution

Buy: 10,000 × $14 = $140,000. Make: $150,000.
Buying saves $150,000 − $140,000 = $10,000.

Buying saves $10,000

The allocated overhead is not relevant: it continues whether Cherokee makes or buys.
Example 3 — Opportunity Cost

If Cherokee buys the part, the freed factory space could be rented to another firm for $25,000 a year. What is the total annual advantage of buying, including the rental income?

$
Solution

$10,000 + $25,000 = $35,000.

Total advantage of buying = $35,000

The rent is the opportunity cost of continuing to make the part in that space.
Special order

Special Orders

A national outfitter offers to buy 2,000 canoes at $38 each (the regular price is much higher). Cherokee has idle capacity. Variable manufacturing costs per canoe are direct materials $12, direct labor $10, and variable overhead $5. Normal variable selling cost of $3 per unit would not be incurred on this order.

Example 1 — Incremental Profit

Ignoring any extra costs, what is the incremental profit from accepting the order?

Need a hint?
Relevant variable cost per canoe = $12 + $10 + $5. Compare it with the offered price.
$
Solution

Variable cost = $27 per canoe. Incremental profit per canoe = $38 − $27 = $11. × 2,000 = $22,000.

Incremental profit = $22,000

Example 2 — With Special Tooling

The order would also require special tooling costing $8,000. What is the incremental profit now?

$
Solution

$22,000 − $8,000 = $14,000. Still profitable, so accept.

Incremental profit = $14,000

Example 3 — Minimum Price

What is the lowest price per canoe at which Cherokee would break even on this order (including the tooling)?

Need a hint?
Variable cost per unit plus tooling spread over the units.
$
Solution

$27 + ($8,000 ÷ 2,000) = $27 + $4 = $31.

Minimum price = $31

With idle capacity, any price above the incremental cost adds to profit. Without idle capacity, the opportunity cost of displaced regular sales would also matter.
Keep or drop

Keep or Drop a Product Line

Cherokee's paddle line has sales of $240,000, variable expenses of $150,000, avoidable fixed expenses of $60,000, and $50,000 of allocated common fixed expenses that would continue if paddles were dropped.

Example 1 — Effect of Dropping

By how much would company net operating income decrease if the paddle line were dropped?

Need a hint?
Lost contribution margin minus the avoidable fixed costs saved. The allocated costs continue.
$
Solution
Differential analysis: drop paddles
Amount
Lost contribution margin$90,000
Avoidable fixed expenses saved$60,000
Net decrease in operating income$30,000

Dropping paddles would reduce income by $30,000

The paddle line looks unprofitable on a fully allocated basis (CM $90,000 − $60,000 − $50,000 = $(20,000)), but the allocated $50,000 continues anyway. Keep the line.
Constrained resource

Using a Constrained Resource

Cherokee's molding machine is a bottleneck with 2,400 machine-hours available. Canoes earn $120 contribution margin per unit and use 3 machine-hours; demand is 600 canoes. Kayaks earn $150 per unit and use 5 machine-hours.

When a resource is constrained, rank products by contribution margin per unit of the constraint, not by CM per unit. Produce in that order up to demand.
Example 1 — CM per Machine-Hour

What is the contribution margin per machine-hour for a canoe?

$
Solution

$120 ÷ 3 MH = $40 per MH. Kayak: $150 ÷ 5 MH = $30 per MH.

Canoe CM per MH = $40

Example 2 — Which Product First?

Which product should be produced first with the scarce machine time?

Solution

Canoes earn $40 of contribution margin per machine-hour; kayaks earn only $30. Even though a kayak has the higher CM per unit, the canoe makes better use of the bottleneck.

Example 3 — Maximum Total CM

Produce all 600 canoes demanded, then use the remaining machine-hours for kayaks. What total contribution margin is earned?

Need a hint?
Canoes use 1,800 MH. The remaining MH go to kayaks at 5 MH each.
$
Solution

Canoes: 600 × $120 = $72,000 using 1,800 MH.
Remaining: 600 MH ÷ 5 = 120 kayaks × $150 = $18,000.
Total CM = $90,000.

Total contribution margin = $90,000

Sell or process further

Sell or Process Further

A joint process at Cherokee costs $50,000 and yields a hull blank that can be sold at the split-off point for $80,000, or processed further at an added cost of $30,000 and sold as a finished hull for $120,000.

Example 1 — Gain from Processing Further

What is the incremental gain (or loss) from processing further?

Need a hint?
Compare the added revenue with the added cost. The joint cost is incurred either way.
$
Solution

Incremental revenue: $120,000 − $80,000 = $40,000.
Incremental cost: $30,000.
Net gain from processing further: $10,000.

Gain = $10,000 in favor of processing further

Example 2 — The Joint Cost

Is the $50,000 joint cost relevant to this decision?

Solution

The joint cost was incurred before the split-off point and is the same whether the blank is sold or processed further. It is a sunk cost and not relevant.

Wrap-up

Chapter 6 at a Glance

  • Relevant = future and different between alternatives. Sunk and common costs are not.
  • Make or buy: compare avoidable costs of making with the purchase price, plus any opportunity cost.
  • Special order: accept if incremental revenue exceeds incremental cost (with idle capacity).
  • Keep or drop: compare lost contribution margin to avoidable fixed costs.
  • Constraint: maximize CM per unit of the constrained resource.
  • Sell or process further: ignore joint costs; compare added revenue to added cost.
