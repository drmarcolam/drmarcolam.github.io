Traditional costing

Plantwide and Departmental Overhead Rates

Monster Energy Drinks makes two drinks (Energy and Mule) in 60 batches using total overhead of $156,000 and 2,400 machine hours. Energy needs 18 machine hours per batch and Mule needs 22. Direct materials and labor per batch: Energy $260 and $80; Mule $380 and $100.

Plantwide rate = Total overhead ÷ Total allocation base. Departmental rates assign overhead to each department first, then to products by their use of that department's base.

Example 1 — Plantwide Rate

Compute the plantwide overhead rate per machine hour.

$
Solution

$156,000 ÷ 2,400 = $65 per machine hour.

Rate = $65 per machine hour

Example 2 — Energy Overhead per Batch

Using the plantwide rate, what overhead is assigned to one batch of Energy?

$
Solution

18 hours × $65 = $1,170.

Overhead per batch = $1,170

Example 3 — Mule Total Batch Cost

What is the total cost of a batch of Mule using the plantwide rate?

$
Solution

Overhead 22 × $65 = $1,430. Total = $380 + $100 + $1,430 = $1,910. (Energy: $260 + $80 + $1,170 = $1,510.)

Mule batch cost = $1,910

Example 4 — Mixing Department Rate

The mixing department has $40,000 of overhead and uses 400 direct labor hours (Energy 180, Mule 220). Compute its rate per direct labor hour.

$
Solution

$40,000 ÷ 400 = $100 per direct labor hour.

Mixing rate = $100 per DLH

Example 5 — Energy Overhead with Departmental Rates

Bottling has $116,000 of overhead and 1,400 machine hours. An Energy batch uses 3.00 mixing labor hours and 11.33 bottling machine hours. What overhead per batch results from departmental rates?

$
Solution

Mixing: 3.00 × $100 = $300. Bottling rate = $116,000 ÷ 1,400 = $82.86; 11.33 × $82.86 = $939. Total = $1,239 (total batch cost $1,579).

Overhead per batch = $1,239

Activity-based costing

Activities, Cost Drivers and the Cost Hierarchy

ABC traces the cost of activities to cost objects based on the cost object's use of each activity. Premises: activities drive costs, and costs should be assigned in proportion to the activities consumed.

  • Unit-level: performed for every unit.
  • Batch-level: performed for every batch.
  • Product-level: supports each different product.
  • Facility-level: sustains the whole facility.
Example 1 — Cost Hierarchy

Classify each activity cost.

Solution

Costs that rise with each unit are unit-level; those triggered by each batch are batch-level; costs that support a specific product line are product-level; and costs that sustain the plant, whatever is produced, are facility-level.

Example 2 — Cost Drivers

Choose the best cost driver for each activity.

Solution

A good cost driver has a logical, causal relationship with the activity's cost.

ABC product costing

Computing Activity Rates and Product Costs

A window manufacturer has these activity cost pools: setup $55,000 (550 setups); materials handling $10,050 (670 moves); machine operating $242,400 (20,200 machine hours); packing $67,200 (1,400 packing orders).

Activity rate = Activity cost ÷ Activity driver quantity. Product overhead = Σ (Rate × Driver units used).

Example 1 — Machine Operating Rate

Compute the machine operating cost per machine hour.

$
Solution

$242,400 ÷ 20,200 = $12 per machine hour. (Setup $100 per setup, handling $15 per move, packing $48 per order.)

Rate = $12 per machine hour

Example 3 — Energy Batch Cost under ABC

Under ABC, overhead assigned to Energy is $79,137 for 60 batches. With materials $260 and labor $80 per batch, what is the total cost per batch?

$
Solution

Example 4 — Comparing the Methods

Batch cost for Energy is $1,510 (plantwide), $1,579 (departmental) and $1,659 (ABC). For Mule: $1,910, $1,841 and $1,761.

Solution

Plantwide costing spreads overhead on machine hours only, so it undercosts Energy and overcosts Mule compared with ABC. ABC provides more accurate cost data for decisions on pricing and product mix.

Implementing ABC

Limitations and Time-Driven ABC

ABC is more accurate but also more expensive to maintain. Time-driven ABC estimates a cost per unit of time for each resource.

Example 1 — Capacity Cost Rate

Kellogg's accumulates $27,525 of machine cost over 1,101 hours. Compute the capacity cost rate per hour.

$
Solution

$27,525 ÷ 1,101 = $25 per hour.

Rate = $25 per hour

Example 2 — True or False?

Decide whether each statement is true or false.

Solution

ABC can be costly, so benefits must exceed costs. Many firms run both systems. ABC also applies to nonmanufacturing costs such as selling, service and customer activities.

Customer profitability

ABC and Customer Profitability

Elite Refinishing serves three customers, each with $150,000 of gross profit. Activity costs: refinishes $150 each, touch-ups $100 each, communications $30 each. Brookside: 120 refinishes, 260 touch-ups, 900 communications. Edgewater: 70 refinishes, 205 touch-ups, 530 communications.

Customer profit = Gross profit − Cost of activities used to serve the customer.

Example 1 — Brookside's Profit

Compute Brookside's customer profit.

$
Solution

Activity costs: $18,000 + $26,000 + $27,000 = $71,000. Profit = $150,000 − $71,000 = $79,000.

Brookside profit = $79,000

Example 2 — Edgewater's Profit

Compute Edgewater's customer profit.

$
Solution

Activity costs: $10,500 + $20,500 + $15,900 = $46,900. Profit = $150,000 − $46,900 = $103,100.

Edgewater profit = $103,100

Example 3 — Communications per Procedure

Brookside has 900 communications for 380 procedures (refinishes plus touch-ups). How many communications per procedure (one decimal)?

Solution

900 ÷ 380 = 2.4. Edgewater's customers average about 1.9 communications per procedure.

Communications = 2.4 per procedure

Complete the statement.

Wrap-up

Module 19 at a Glance

  • Plantwide and departmental rates use one or a few volume-based drivers; both can distort product costs.
  • ABC: activity rate = activity cost ÷ driver; assign cost by driver use; hierarchy = unit, batch, product, facility.
  • ABC gives more accurate costs but is more expensive; time-driven ABC uses a capacity cost rate per hour.
  • Use ABC to measure customer profitability and multi-level contribution margins.
