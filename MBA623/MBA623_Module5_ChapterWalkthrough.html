Revenue recognition

The Five-Step Revenue Recognition Model

A company recognizes revenue when it satisfies a performance obligation by transferring a good or service to the customer.

Example 1 — Order the Steps

Place each activity in its step of the revenue recognition model.

Solution

The model proceeds from the contract (1), to its separate promises (2), to the price (3), to allocating that price across the promises (4), to recognizing revenue as each promise is satisfied (5).

Example 2 — When Is Revenue Recognized?

Choose the timing for each situation.

Solution

Cash from a gift card or season ticket creates deferred revenue until the obligation is met. Long-term contracts use cost-to-cost to recognize revenue as work progresses.

Long-term contracts

The Cost-to-Cost Method

A contractor signs a contract with a price of $900,000. Total expected costs are $675,000: $202,500 in Year 1, $337,500 in Year 2 and $135,000 in Year 3.

Revenue each year = Contract price × (Costs incurred to date for the year ÷ Total expected costs). Income = Revenue − Costs.

Example 1 — Year 1 Revenue

How much revenue is recognized in Year 1?

Need a hint?
Percent complete = costs incurred ÷ total expected costs.
$
Solution

$202,500 ÷ $675,000 = 30% complete. 30% × $900,000 = $270,000.

Year 1 revenue = $270,000

Example 2 — Year 2 Income

How much income (revenue − costs) is recognized in Year 2?

$
Solution

$337,500 ÷ $675,000 = 50%. Revenue = 50% × $900,000 = $450,000. Income = $450,000 − $337,500 = $112,500.

Year 2 income = $112,500

Sales allowances

Returns and Net Sales

Companies estimate returns and deduct them from gross sales to report net sales.

Example 1 — Net Sales After Returns

Levi Strauss sells jeans to a customer for $130 on account and expects returns of 3% of sales. What net sales does it recognize (two decimals)?

Need a hint?
Deduct the expected returns from the sales price.
$
Solution

$130 × (1 − 0.03) = $126.10. The expected $3.90 of returns reduces revenue.

Net sales = $126.10

Example 2 — ModCloth

ModCloth sells $10 million of merchandise and expects 2% to be returned. How much net sales does it recognize, in $ millions?

$
Solution

98% × $10 million = $9.8 million.

Net sales = $9.8 million

Deferred revenue

Unearned Revenue

Cash received before the company performs creates a liability. Hamilton Theatre collects $630,000 for season tickets covering six productions.

Example 1 — Revenue per Production

How much revenue is recognized after each production?

$
Solution

$630,000 ÷ 6 = $105,000.

Revenue per production = $105,000

Example 2 — Remaining Liability

After two productions, what unearned revenue remains on the balance sheet?

$
Solution

$630,000 − 2 × $105,000 = $420,000.

Unearned revenue = $420,000

Example 3 — Reading Deferred Revenue Trends

Padgett's deferred revenue rose from $51,002 to $74,502 million over the year; Williams's fell from $94,300 to $88,065 million.

Solution

Growing deferred revenue means new prepayments exceed revenue recognized, a sign of future revenue. A decline means current revenue came from earlier prepayments and new prepayments slowed.

Accounts receivable

Allowance for Uncollectible Accounts

GAAP requires reporting receivables at the amount expected to be collected: Net A/R = Gross A/R − Allowance.

Example 1 — Bad Debt Expense

Gross receivables are $86,000, estimated uncollectible accounts are $2,100, and the allowance currently has a credit balance of $700. What bad debt expense is recorded?

$
Solution

The allowance must reach $2,100, so expense = $2,100 − $700 = $1,400.

Bad debt expense = $1,400

Example 2 — Net Realizable Value

After the adjustment, what is net accounts receivable?

$
Solution

$86,000 − $2,100 = $83,900.

Net A/R = $83,900

Example 4 — Write-Off of a $500 Account

Effect of writing off a customer's $500 balance after bankruptcy:

Solution

Writing off a $500 account reduces gross A/R and the allowance equally, so net A/R is unchanged. The expense was recorded earlier when the allowance was set up.

Operating expenses

R&D, Discontinued Operations and Non-GAAP Measures

Analysts look beyond reported net income at recurring and transitory items.

Example 1 — True or False?

Decide whether each statement is true or false.

Solution

Discontinued operations are transitory, so they are separated from continuing income. EBITDA and adjusted EPS are non-GAAP measures that managers choose to report, and the SEC requires a reconciliation to GAAP so readers can compare.

Wrap-up

Module 5 at a Glance

  • Recognize revenue when performance obligations are satisfied; use cost-to-cost for long-term contracts and deduct expected returns.
  • Cash received before performance is unearned (deferred) revenue; rising deferred revenue signals future revenue.
  • Net A/R = Gross A/R − Allowance; bad debt expense adjusts the allowance; write-offs do not change net A/R. A/R turnover = Sales ÷ Average A/R and DSO = 365 ÷ turnover.
  • Separate transitory items (discontinued operations) and read non-GAAP measures with the required GAAP reconciliation.
