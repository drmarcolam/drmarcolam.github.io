Profit equation

Predicting Profit

A hair salon charges $60 per haircut. Variable cost is $15.30 per haircut and fixed costs are $240,000 per year.

Profit = (Price × Units) − (Fixed costs + Variable cost per unit × Units)

Example 1 — Profit at 9,000 Haircuts

Compute annual profit for 9,000 haircuts.

$
Solution

Sales = $60 × 9,000 = $540,000. Variable costs = $15.30 × 9,000 = $137,700. Profit = $540,000 − ($240,000 + $137,700) = $162,300.

Profit = $162,300

Example 2 — Profit After Cost Increases

Fixed costs rise 10% and variable cost rises to $17.64 per haircut. Compute profit at 9,000 haircuts.

$
Solution

Fixed costs = $240,000 × 1.10 = $264,000. Variable costs = $17.64 × 9,000 = $158,760. Profit = $540,000 − ($264,000 + $158,760) = $117,240.

Profit = $117,240

Contribution margin

The Contribution Income Statement

Chillin' Time sells ice cream bars for $1.50. Variable cost is $1.10 per bar and fixed costs are $1,780 per month. Monthly volume is 6,950 bars.

  • Contribution margin = Sales − Variable costs (what is left to cover fixed costs and profit).
  • CM ratio = Contribution margin ÷ Sales.
Example 1 — Total Contribution Margin

Break-even and target profit

Break-Even Point and Profit Planning

  • Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ Unit contribution margin
  • Break-even sales dollars = Fixed costs ÷ CM ratio
  • Target-profit units = (Fixed costs + Target profit) ÷ Unit CM
  • Margin of safety = Actual (or expected) sales − Break-even sales
Example 1 — Break-Even Units

How many bars must Chillin' Time sell to break even?

Solution

$1,780 ÷ $0.40 = 4,450 bars.

Break-even = 4,450 bars

Example 2 — Break-Even Sales Dollars

What is break-even in sales dollars?

$
Solution

$1,780 ÷ 0.26667 = $6,675 (or 4,450 × $1.50).

Break-even sales = $6,675

Example 3 — Units for a Target Profit

How many bars must be sold to earn a $800 monthly profit?

Solution

($1,780 + $800) ÷ $0.40 = 6,450 bars.

Units = 6,450 bars

Example 4 — Margin of Safety

At 6,950 bars, what is the margin of safety in units?

Solution

6,950 − 4,450 = 2,500 bars. Sales could fall 2,500 bars before the company incurs a loss.

Margin of safety = 2,500 bars

Example 5 — Sales for a Target Profit

A company has fixed costs of $225,000 and variable costs equal to 45% of sales. What sales dollars are needed for a $200,000 profit?

$
Solution

CM ratio = 1 − 0.45 = 0.55. ($225,000 + $200,000) ÷ 0.55 = $772,727 (about $772,728 rounded up).

Sales needed = $772,728

Multiple products

Break-Even for a Multi-Product Firm

A hockey-stick company sells Bauer (CM $60), Warrior (CM $45) and CCM (CM $40) in a constant sales mix of 6 : 3 : 1. Fixed costs are $150,000.

Compute the weighted-average unit contribution margin using the sales mix, then divide fixed costs by it. Allocate break-even units to products in the mix.

Example 1 — Average Unit CM

Compute the weighted-average unit contribution margin.

$
Solution

$60 × 6/10 + $45 × 3/10 + $40 × 1/10 = $36 + $13.50 + $4 = $53.50.

Average CM = $53.50

Example 2 — Break-Even Units

Compute total break-even units (round up).

Solution

$150,000 ÷ $53.50 = 2,803.7, so 2,804 units.

Break-even = 2,804 units

Example 3 — Warrior Sticks at Break-Even

How many Warrior sticks are sold at break-even (round up)?

Solution

2,804 × 3/10 = 841.2, so 842.

Warrior = 842 sticks

Operating leverage

Operating Leverage: Risk and Opportunity

Operating leverage ratio = Contribution margin ÷ Pre-tax profit. It estimates the percent change in profit for each 1% change in sales.

Example 1 — Operating Leverage Ratio

A company has contribution margin of $10,200 and pre-tax profit of $2,200. Compute the operating leverage ratio (two decimals).

Solution

$10,200 ÷ $2,200 = 4.64.

Operating leverage = 4.64

Example 2 — Profit Change from a 10% Sales Increase

By what percent will profit increase if sales rise 10% (one decimal)?

%
Solution

4.64 × 10% = 46.4%.

Profit increase = 46.4%

Example 3 — Risk and Opportunity

Compare a company with high operating leverage (high fixed costs) with one with low leverage.

Solution

High fixed costs magnify the effect of volume changes on profit in both directions: greater opportunity and greater risk.

Income taxes

Planning for an After-Tax Profit

To earn a target after-tax profit, first convert it to before-tax profit: Before-tax profit = After-tax profit ÷ (1 − tax rate).

Example 1 — Before-Tax Profit

A company wants an after-tax profit of $4,500 with a 40% tax rate. What before-tax profit is required?

$
Solution

$4,500 ÷ (1 − 0.40) = $7,500.

Before-tax profit = $7,500

Example 2 — Units for the After-Tax Target

Fixed costs are $25,000 and the unit contribution margin is $10 ($40 price, $30 variable). How many units are needed for that $7,500 before-tax profit?

Solution

($25,000 + $7,500) ÷ $10 = 3,250 units.

Units = 3,250

Example 3 — Break-Even Sensitivity

How does each change affect the break-even point?

Solution

Break-even = fixed costs ÷ unit CM. Higher fixed costs or lower unit CM raise it; a higher price raises unit CM and lowers it.

Wrap-up

Module 16 at a Glance

  • Profit = (Price × Units) − (Fixed + Variable per unit × Units); contribution margin = sales − variable costs.
  • Break-even = Fixed costs ÷ Unit CM (units) or ÷ CM ratio (dollars); target profit adds the profit to fixed costs.
  • Multi-product firms use the weighted-average CM for a constant sales mix.
  • Operating leverage = CM ÷ Profit measures risk and opportunity; target after-tax profit ÷ (1 − t).
