Debits and credits

T-Accounts and Normal Balances

Journal entries record transactions using debits (left side of a T-account) and credits (right side). The FSET shows the same transaction on all four statements.

  • Assets, expenses and dividends have normal debit balances.
  • Liabilities, contributed capital, retained earnings and revenues have normal credit balances.
Example 1 — Normal Balance

Choose the side on which each account normally has its balance.

Solution

An account increases on its normal-balance side. Assets, expenses and dividends increase with debits; liabilities, equity and revenues increase with credits.

Financial statement effects

Analyzing Transactions with the FSET

For each transaction ask: Which accounts are affected? In what direction? By how much? A new cleaning business, Pisgah Clean, begins operations in April:

  • Apr 1: Owner invests $9,000 cash for common stock.
  • Apr 2: Pays $2,850 cash for a six-month van lease (prepaid).
  • Apr 3: Borrows $10,000 from a bank (note payable).
  • Apr 4: Buys $5,500 of equipment; pays $2,500 cash, the rest due in 30 days.
  • Apr 5: Pays $4,300 cash for cleaning supplies.

Example 2 — April 4 Effects

Equipment purchase: $2,500 paid in cash, $3,000 owed within 30 days. Indicate the effect on each item.

Solution

Equipment (a noncash asset) rises $5,500; cash falls $2,500; accounts payable (a liability) rises $3,000. Buying an asset is not an expense, so net income is unaffected.

Adjustments

The Four Types of Accounting Adjustments

Adjustments at period-end make the statements accurate and complete under accrual accounting.

  • Prepaid (deferred) expenses: cash paid first, expense later.
  • Unearned (deferred) revenues: cash received first, revenue later.
  • Accrued expenses: expense incurred, cash paid later.
  • Accrued revenues: revenue earned, cash received later.
Example 1 — Classify the Adjustment

Choose the type of adjustment each situation requires.

Solution

Cash first and expense later is a prepaid expense; cash first and revenue later is unearned revenue; an incurred but unpaid cost is an accrued expense; an earned but uncollected amount is an accrued revenue.

Adjustment amounts

Computing the Adjusting Entries

Each adjustment moves the right amount between the balance sheet and the income statement.

Example 1 — Supplies Used

The Supplies account shows $3,870 before adjustment, and a count at year-end shows $1,100 on hand. What is supplies expense?

$
Solution

Supplies used = $3,870 − $1,100 = $2,770.

Supplies expense = $2,770

Entry: Debit Supplies expense $2,770; Credit Supplies $2,770.
Example 2 — Prepaid Rent

The company prepaid $3,200 of rent that covers four months. One month has now passed. What is rent expense to recognize?

Need a hint?
Divide the prepayment by the months it covers.
$
Solution

$3,200 ÷ 4 months = $800 per month.

Rent expense = $800

Example 3 — Unearned Revenue Earned

A customer prepaid $1,872 for a 12-month service contract. Nine months of service have been provided. How much revenue is recognized?

$
Solution

$1,872 × 9/12 = $1,404.

Revenue recognized = $1,404

Entry: Debit Unearned revenue $1,404; Credit Revenue $1,404.
Example 4 — Accrued Salaries

Salaries of $15,000 are paid every two weeks. At December 31, $4,700 of salaries is accrued. On January 9 the company pays $15,000. How much salaries expense belongs to January?

$
Solution

The $15,000 payment settles the $4,700 liability recorded at December 31. The remainder is January expense: $15,000 − $4,700 = $10,300.

January salaries expense = $10,300

Example 5 — Accrue Utilities of $430

Choose the account to debit and the account to credit.

Solution

Accruing $430 of utilities: Debit Utilities expense (an expense, which reduces retained earnings) and Credit Utilities payable (a liability). No cash moves yet.

Preparing statements

Constructing the Financial Statements

After all entries are recorded, prepare the statements in order: income statement, statement of stockholders' equity (reconcile retained earnings), balance sheet, and statement of cash flows.

Example 1 — Ending Retained Earnings

Leuz Architect Services began the year with retained earnings of $18,000, earned net income of $27,900 and paid dividends of $9,700. Compute ending retained earnings.

$
Solution

$18,000 + $27,900 − $9,700 = $36,200.

Ending retained earnings = $36,200

Closing process

Closing the Temporary Accounts

Closing transfers the balances of temporary accounts to retained earnings so that revenues, expenses and dividends start the next period at zero.

Example 1 — Temporary or Permanent?

Classify each account.

Solution

Revenues, expenses and dividends are closed to retained earnings each period. Balance sheet accounts such as cash and retained earnings carry forward.

Example 2 — Closing Entries

Hanlon Consulting has service fees earned of $80,300, rent expense $20,800, salaries expense $48,700, supplies expense $5,600 and depreciation expense $10,200. Retained earnings before closing are $67,000. What are retained earnings after closing revenues and expenses?

Need a hint?
Net loss = revenue − total expenses.
$
Solution

Close revenue: Retained earnings +$80,300. Close expenses totaling $20,800 + $48,700 + $5,600 + $10,200 = $85,300: Retained earnings −$85,300.
$67,000 + $80,300 − $85,300 = $62,000.

Retained earnings after closing = $62,000

Accrual vs. cash

Income vs. Cash Flow

Penno Services performed $200,000 of services during the year and had collected $50,000 in cash by year-end. It paid $25,000 of wages in cash and owes another $15,000 of wages at year-end.

Example 1 — Net Income

Compute net income under accrual accounting.

$
Solution

Revenue is $200,000 when earned. Wages expense = $25,000 + $15,000 = $40,000. $200,000 − $40,000 = $160,000.

Net income = $160,000

Example 2 — Net Cash Inflow

Compute the net cash inflow for the year.

$
Solution

Cash collected $50,000 − wages paid $25,000 = $25,000.

Net cash inflow = $25,000

Net income exceeds net cash flow by $135,000 because $150,000 of revenue has not been collected and $15,000 of wages is unpaid.

Costco ($ millions): merchandise inventories were $9,834 at the beginning of the year and $11,040 at the end. Cost of goods sold was $123,152.

Beginning inventory + Purchases − Cost of goods sold = Ending inventory.

Compute Costco's inventory purchases for the year, in $ millions.

Rearrange the inventory equation to solve for purchases.
$
Solution

Purchases = Ending inventory + COGS − Beginning inventory = $11,040 + $123,152 − $9,834 = $124,358.

Purchases = $124,358 million

Wrap-up

Module 3 at a Glance

  • Record transactions with debits and credits or the FSET; assets, expenses and dividends increase with debits.
  • Four adjustments: prepaid expenses, unearned revenues, accrued expenses, accrued revenues.
  • Prepare statements in order: income statement, equity statement, balance sheet, cash flows.
  • Closing zeroes out temporary accounts (revenues, expenses, dividends) into retained earnings.
