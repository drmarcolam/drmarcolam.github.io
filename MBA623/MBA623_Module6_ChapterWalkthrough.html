Inventory costing

FIFO, LIFO and Average Cost

Madden Company began the year with 1,300 units at $150 and purchased 1,700 units at $180 during the year (3,000 units available; cost of goods available $501,000). It sold 2,000 units.

  • FIFO: the oldest costs go to COGS; ending inventory holds the newest costs.
  • LIFO: the newest costs go to COGS; ending inventory holds the oldest costs.
  • Average cost: $501,000 ÷ 3,000 units = $167 per unit.
Example 1 — FIFO COGS

Compute cost of goods sold under FIFO.

Need a hint?
The 2,000 units sold come from the oldest layers first.
$
Solution

1,300 × $150 = $195,000 plus 700 × $180 = $126,000: $321,000. Ending inventory = 1,000 × $180 = $180,000.

FIFO COGS = $321,000

Example 2 — LIFO COGS

Compute cost of goods sold under LIFO.

$
Solution

1,700 × $180 = $306,000 plus 300 × $150 = $45,000: $351,000. Ending inventory = 1,000 × $150 = $150,000.

LIFO COGS = $351,000

Example 3 — Average Cost COGS

Compute cost of goods sold under average cost.

$
Solution

$167 × 2,000 = $334,000. Ending inventory = $167 × 1,000 = $167,000.

Average-cost COGS = $334,000

Example 4 — Rising-Cost Effects

Which method produces each result when costs are rising?

Solution

With rising costs, FIFO has the lowest COGS (highest gross profit and ending inventory), and LIFO the highest COGS (lowest income and taxes). This tax saving is why many U.S. companies use LIFO.

LIFO reserve

Converting LIFO to FIFO

LyondellBasell ($ millions): LIFO inventory $4,588; LIFO reserve $670; total assets $30,435; cost of goods sold $29,301; the LIFO reserve decreased by $128 during the year.

  • FIFO inventory = LIFO inventory + LIFO reserve
  • FIFO COGS = LIFO COGS − Change in LIFO reserve
Example 1 — FIFO Inventory

Compute inventory on a FIFO basis, in $ millions.

$
Solution

$4,588 + $670 = $5,258.

FIFO inventory = $5,258 million

Example 2 — FIFO Total Assets

Compute total assets if LyondellBasell had used FIFO (before any tax effects), in $ millions.

$
Solution

$30,435 + $670 = $31,105.

FIFO total assets = $31,105 million

Example 3 — FIFO COGS

Compute cost of goods sold on a FIFO basis, in $ millions.

Need a hint?
The reserve decreased, so the change is negative.
$
Solution

FIFO COGS = $29,301 − (−$128) = $29,429.

FIFO COGS = $29,429 million

Analyzing inventory

Inventory Turnover, DIO and the Cash Conversion Cycle

PriceSmart ($ thousands): COGS $2,610,111; inventory $321,025 (current year) and $310,946 (prior year). Nordstrom ($ millions): COGS $10,155; inventory $1,978 and $2,027.

Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ Average inventory. DIO = 365 ÷ turnover. CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO.

Example 1 — PriceSmart Turnover

Compute PriceSmart's inventory turnover (two decimals).

Solution

Average inventory = ($321,025 + $310,946) ÷ 2 = $315,986. $2,610,111 ÷ $315,986 = 8.26.

Turnover = 8.26

Example 2 — PriceSmart DIO

Compute PriceSmart's days inventory outstanding (one decimal).

days
Solution

365 ÷ 8.26 = 44.2 days.

DIO = 44.2 days

Example 3 — Nordstrom DIO

Nordstrom's turnover is 5.07. Compute its DIO (one decimal).

days
Solution

365 ÷ 5.07 = 72.0 days.

DIO = 72.0 days

PriceSmart sells its inventory about four weeks faster than Nordstrom, consistent with a warehouse-club model.
Example 4 — Winnebago's CCC

Winnebago: DSO 26.2 days, DIO 35.9 days, DPO 17.0 days. Compute the cash conversion cycle.

days
Solution

35.9 + 26.2 − 17.0 = 45.1 days.

CCC = 45.1 days

Example 5 — Cash Released by Lower DIO

A company has COGS of $73,000 thousand a year. If it cuts DIO by 5 days, how much cash does it release, in $ thousands?

$
Solution

Cash = ΔDays × COGS ÷ 365 = 5 × $73,000 ÷ 365 = $1,000.

Cash released = $1,000 thousand

Depreciation

Capitalizing and Depreciating PP&E

PP&E is recorded at cost (all costs to acquire and ready it for use) and depreciated over its useful life. Straight-line depreciation = (Cost − Salvage) ÷ Useful life.

Example 1 — Delivery Van

A delivery van costs $37,000, has salvage value of $2,900 and a five-year life. Compute annual straight-line depreciation.

$
Solution

($37,000 − $2,900) ÷ 5 = $6,820.

Depreciation = $6,820 per year

Example 2 — Machine

A machine costs $129,000, has salvage value of $6,000 and a life of 60 months. Compute monthly depreciation.

$
Solution

($129,000 − $6,000) ÷ 60 = $2,050 per month.

Depreciation = $2,050 per month

Example 3 — Net Book Value

Arcola Company equipment cost $330,000, salvage $40,000, 10-year life. What is its net book value after four years of straight-line depreciation?

$
Solution

Annual depreciation = $290,000 ÷ 10 = $29,000. Accumulated depreciation after four years = $116,000. NBV = $330,000 − $116,000 = $214,000.

Net book value = $214,000

Example 4 — Loss on Sale

Arcola sells the equipment for $200,000 at that time. What is the loss on the sale?

Need a hint?
Compare proceeds with net book value.
$
Solution

Gain (loss) = Proceeds − Net book value = $200,000 − $214,000 = $(14,000), a loss.

Loss on sale = $14,000

Example 5 — Depreciation Methods

Complete each statement.

Solution

Accelerated methods record more expense early and less later; straight-line spreads cost evenly; units-of-production follows actual use.

Analyzing PP&E

PP&E Turnover and Percent Used Up

($ millions) Intel: sales $70,848; net PP&E $48,976 and $41,109. Texas Instruments: sales $15,784; net PP&E $3,183 and $2,664.

PP&E turnover = Sales ÷ Average net PP&E. Higher turnover means less capital investment for a given level of sales.

Example 1 — Intel's PP&E Turnover

Compute Intel's PP&E turnover (two decimals).

Solution

$70,848 ÷ (($48,976 + $41,109) ÷ 2) = $70,848 ÷ $45,042.5 = 1.57.

Turnover = 1.57

Example 2 — TI's PP&E Turnover

Compute Texas Instruments' PP&E turnover (two decimals).

Solution

$15,784 ÷ $2,923.5 = 5.40.

Turnover = 5.40

TI generates about $5.40 of sales per PP&E dollar versus $1.57 for Intel, which owns its own fabrication plants.
Example 3 — Percent Used Up

Microsoft's intangibles have a gross carrying amount of $23,577 million and accumulated amortization of $12,279 million. What percent has been used up (one decimal)?

%
Solution

$12,279 ÷ $23,577 = 52.1%.

Percent used up = 52.1%

A high percent used up suggests higher future capital spending to replace the assets.
Impairments and intangibles

Impairments, Restructuring and Goodwill

Assets whose value has permanently declined are written down (impaired); acquisitions create goodwill as a residual.

Example 1 — True or False?

Decide whether each statement is true or false.

Solution

Goodwill is a residual, is not amortized, and is tested for impairment. Restructuring costs including severance are accrued when estimable. Internally generated intangibles are generally not recorded, so balance sheets omit many valuable assets.

Example 2 — Goodwill Impairment

A company reports $1,900 million of goodwill after recognizing a $2,100 million impairment. What was goodwill before the impairment, in $ millions?

$
Solution

Before = After + Impairment = $1,900 + $2,100 = $4,000 million.

Goodwill before = $4,000 million

Wrap-up

Module 6 at a Glance

  • FIFO, LIFO, average cost allocate cost between COGS and inventory; with rising costs FIFO gives higher profit, LIFO lower taxes. Convert with the LIFO reserve.
  • Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ Average inventory; DIO = 365 ÷ turnover; CCC = DIO + DSO − DPO.
  • Depreciation (cost − salvage) ÷ life; gain or loss = proceeds − net book value; PP&E turnover = Sales ÷ Average PP&E.
  • Goodwill is a residual, not amortized, and tested annually for impairment; restructuring and impairments are largely transitory.
