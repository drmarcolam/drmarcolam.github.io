Accrued liabilities

Accruals: Warranties, Interest and Contingencies

Accrued liabilities are recorded after all transactions are posted so that liabilities and expenses are complete.

  • Routine contractual accruals: wages, interest, taxes, deferred revenue.
  • Contingent liabilities (such as warranties and lawsuits) are accrued when the loss is probable and can be reasonably estimated.
Example 1 — Type of Accrual

Classify each liability.

Solution

Wages and interest are fixed by contract. Warranties and lawsuits depend on future events, so they are estimated and accrued only if probable and estimable.

Example 2 — Warranty Expense

A manufacturer sells 10,000 units. It expects 2% to be defective and each repair costs $5. What warranty expense does it record at the time of sale?

$
Solution

10,000 × 2% × $5 = $1,000. The expense and the warranty liability are both recorded when the products are sold.

Warranty expense = $1,000

Example 3 — Accrued Interest: Short-Term Note

Verizon borrows $1,000 on January 1 at 12% interest payable quarterly. How much interest is accrued at March 31?

$
Solution

$1,000 × 12% × 3/12 = $30.

Interest = $30

Example 4 — Accrued Interest at Year-End

A company borrowed $30,000 at 10% on November 21 for 120 days. How much interest has accrued by December 31 (40 days, 365-day year, one decimal)?

$
Solution

$30,000 × 10% × 40/365 = $328.8.

Accrued interest = $328.8

Short-term debt

Current vs. Long-Term Debt

Debt due within 12 months is a current liability. Principal on long-term debt due in the next 12 months is reported as current maturities of long-term debt.

Example 1 — Classify the Debt

Choose how each obligation is reported.

Solution

Only principal due within a year is current. The five-year bond remains long-term until the last year before maturity.

Bond pricing

Premium, Discount and Par

A $100,000, 8% bond matures in four years and pays interest semiannually ($4,000 per period; 8 periods). Its price is the present value of the cash flows at the market rate.

  • Coupon rate > market rate: sold at a premium.
  • Coupon rate < market rate: sold at a discount.
  • Coupon rate = market rate: sold at par.
  • The effective rate always equals the market rate.
Example 1 — Premium, Discount or Par?

How is each bond priced?

Solution

Investors pay less than face when the coupon is below the market rate and more than face when it is above. A quote above 100 means a price above face (a premium), so the yield is below the coupon rate.

Example 2 — Price at a 10% Market Rate

What is the price when the market rate is 10% (5% per half-year)? Use present value of 8 payments of $4,000 plus $100,000 at 5%.

Need a hint?
You can use PV(5%, 8, −4000, −100000) in Excel.
$
Solution

Interest: $4,000 × 6.4632 = $25,853. Principal: $100,000 × 0.67684 = $67,684. Price = $93,537.

Price = $93,537

Example 3 — Price at a 6% Market Rate

What is the price when the market rate is 6% (3% per half-year)?

$
Solution

Interest: $4,000 × 7.0197 = $28,079. Principal: $100,000 × 0.78941 = $78,941. Price = $107,020.

Price = $107,020

Example 4 — First-Period Interest Expense

For the discount bond (price $93,537; market rate 5% per half-year), what interest expense is reported for the first six months?

$
Solution

Interest expense uses the effective rate: $93,537 × 5% = $4,677. Cash paid is only $4,000; the extra $677 accretes the discount.

Interest expense = $4,677

Interest expense on a discount bond is higher than the cash interest paid; on a premium bond it is lower.
Bond retirements

Gains and Losses on Early Retirement

Gain or loss = Net bonds payable (carrying value) − Amount paid to retire.

Example 1 — Loss on Retirement

A company retires bonds with a carrying value of $196,600 by paying $202,000. What is the loss?

$
Solution

$196,600 − $202,000 = $(5,400): a loss of $5,400.

Loss = $5,400

Example 2 — Gain on Retirement

A company retires $600,000 face value bonds (carrying value $626,000) at 103% of face. What is the gain?

Need a hint?
First compute the cash paid as a percent of face.
$
Solution

Payment = 103% × $600,000 = $618,000. Gain = $626,000 − $618,000 = $8,000.

Gain = $8,000

Credit analysis

Debt Maturities, Coverage and Ratings

Creditors ask: can the company repay what it owes? They focus on capital structure, coverage and liquidity.

  • Times interest earned = Operating income ÷ Interest expense
  • Yield = Risk-free rate + Risk premium
Example 1 — Marriott's Near-Term Maturities

Marriott's debt maturing in the next three years is $833, $912 and $3,108 million; total debt is $9,347 million. What percent of total debt matures within three years (one decimal)?

Need a hint?
Add the three maturities first.
%
Solution

$833 + $912 + $3,108 = $4,853. $4,853 ÷ $9,347 = 51.9%.

Near-term maturities = 51.9%

Example 2 — Times Interest Earned

A company reports operating income of $750 million and interest expense of $60 million. Compute times interest earned (one decimal).

Solution

$750 ÷ $60 = 12.5 times.

TIE = 12.5

Example 3 — Required Yield

The risk-free rate is 4.0% and the market requires a 2.5% risk premium for a lower-rated borrower. What yield will investors require?

%
Solution

4.0% + 2.5% = 6.5%.

Yield = 6.5%

Example 4 — Reading Ratios

International Traders: liabilities-to-equity rose from 2.079 to 2.421; times interest earned rose from 9.6 to 12.5.

Solution

More liabilities per dollar of equity means more reliance on debt. A higher times interest earned ratio means operating profit covers interest more times, so coverage improved.

Time value of money

Present and Future Value

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. Use PV tables, a financial calculator or Excel's PV and FV functions.

Example 1 — Present Value of a Single Amount

What is the present value of $5,000 to be received in three years if the annual rate is 8%?

Need a hint?
Divide by (1 + rate) raised to the number of years.
$
Solution

$5,000 ÷ 1.083 = $3,969.16.

PV = $3,969.16

Example 2 — Present Value of an Annuity

What is the present value of $100 received at the end of each of the next three years at 5%?

$
Solution

$100 × 2.7232 (Table 2, 3 periods, 5%) = $272.32.

PV = $272.32

Example 3 — Future Value of an Annuity

You invest $2,000 at the end of each year for five years at 8%. What is the balance after five years?

$
Solution

$2,000 × 5.8666 = $11,733.

FV = $11,733

Wrap-up

Module 7 at a Glance

  • Accrue routine liabilities (wages, interest, deferred revenue) and contingencies that are probable and estimable (warranties).
  • Bond price = PV of interest + PV of principal at the market rate: coupon > market → premium; coupon < market → discount.
  • Interest expense = carrying value × effective rate; retirement gain/loss = carrying value − cash paid.
  • Assess credit with solvency, coverage (times interest earned) and liquidity; yield = risk-free + risk premium; apply time value of money.
