Constructing and Assessing Income Statements Using the Cost-to-Cost Method

Spreading the revenue and profit of a multi-year construction contract across the years the work is done — and asking whether costs incurred are a fair measure of the work.

Rounding convention: do not round intermediate calculations. Enter dollar amounts in millions to two decimal places (e.g., 44.00) and percentage of completion as a percentage to two decimal places (e.g., 20.00). The percentage of completion is cumulative — costs incurred to date over total expected costs — while revenue, expense and net income are the amounts for that year alone.
Problem

Watauga Construction Company

Watauga Construction Company of Boone contracted to build a shopping center at a contract price of $220 million. The schedule of expected (which equals actual) cash collections and contract costs follows.

YearCash collectionsCost incurred
Required: (a) Calculate the amount of revenue, expense and net income for each of the three years, and for all three years combined, using the cost-to-cost revenue recognition method. (b) Discuss whether or not the cost-to-cost method provides a good measure of this construction company’s performance under the contract.

Percentage complete (cumulative) = costs incurred to date ÷ total expected costs

Revenue to date = percentage complete × contract price

Revenue for the year = revenue to date − revenue recognized in prior years

Expense for the year = the cost actually incurred that year

Cash collections play no part in the calculation. They follow the billing schedule in the contract, which is negotiated separately from the work; the whole point of the method is to report income when the work is done rather than when the customer pays.

Two checks on the combined row: total revenue must equal the contract price, and total net income must equal the contract price less total costs. The method decides when the profit is reported, never how much of it there is.

(b) Does the method measure performance well?

Choose the statement that best assesses the cost-to-cost method for this contract.

  1. Yes. Revenue is recognized as the work is performed, with costs incurred serving as the measure of progress, so income follows the effort expended rather than the timing of cash collections. Its weakness is its dependence on the estimate of total cost: if that estimate is wrong, the pattern of reported income is wrong with it.
  2. No. Revenue should be recognized only as cash is collected, since the collections schedule is the one objectively verifiable measure of what the customer has accepted.
  3. No. The method recognizes the entire profit on the contract as soon as the contract is signed, which overstates income in the earliest year.
  4. Yes. The method assigns an equal share of the contract profit to each year of the contract regardless of the activity in that year, which smooths income and makes the years comparable.
