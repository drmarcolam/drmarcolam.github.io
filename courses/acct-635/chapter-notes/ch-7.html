1. Learning Objectives & Chapter Map

Proprietary funds report a government's business-type activities: they provide goods and services to paying customers on an exchange basis. After studying this chapter you should be able to:

  • LO 7-1: Explain the similarities and differences between Internal Service Funds (ISFs) and Enterprise Funds (EFs).
  • LO 7-2: Explain how ISFs are used and how their operating costs are charged to other departments, and prepare their journal entries and statements.
  • LO 7-3: Account for common Enterprise Fund transactions (revenue bonds, special assessments) and prepare the required statements.
  • LO 7-4: Identify the differences between GASB and FASB GAAP for the same types of transactions.

2. Measurement Focus and Basis of Accounting

  • Economic resources measurement focus: Proprietary funds record all assets and liabilities, including capital assets with their related depreciation, and long-term debt that will be repaid exclusively from proprietary fund revenues.
  • Full accrual basis: Recognize revenue when earned and expenses when incurred, as a business does.
  • No other financing sources or uses: Because capital assets and long-term debt are on the books, buying equipment is not an expenditure and borrowing is not an other financing source. Proceeds of debt are a liability.

3.1 When an Enterprise Fund Is Required

An Enterprise Fund is required for a significant activity if any of the three criteria is met:

  1. The activity is financed with debt secured only by the activity's net revenues (typically revenue bonds).
  2. Laws or regulations require the activity to recover its costs of providing services through fees or charges.
  3. The government's pricing policies are intended to recover costs through fees or charges.

3.2 Control of Proprietary Fund Activities

  • There is a cause-and-effect relationship between the level of operations and the revenues earned and expenses incurred, so flexible budgets are used.
  • Budgets seldom have the force of law. GAAP does not require budgetary comparison schedules for proprietary funds, and budgets are usually not integrated into the accounts.
  • ISFs are controlled indirectly, through the operating budgets of the departments that use their services. They typically have no budget entries and no encumbrances.
  • EFs: customers exercise some control because they choose whether to buy. A monopoly (such as a public utility) may have its rates set by a legislative or governing board. Generally no budget entries and no encumbrance accounting.

4. Proprietary Fund Financial Statements

Three statements are required: the statement of fund net position (or balance sheet), the statement of revenues, expenses, and changes in fund net position, and the statement of cash flows.

4.1 Statement of Fund Net Position

  • Classified format: Current assets are reasonably expected to be realized in cash or consumed within a year. Current liabilities are expected to be liquidated with current assets or by creating other current liabilities. Everything else is noncurrent.
  • Deferred outflows of resources: A use of net assets that applies to a future period.
  • Deferred inflows of resources: An acquisition of net assets that applies to a future period.
  • Deferred outflows and inflows are limited to items specifically identified by the GASB and are reported separately from assets and liabilities.

4.3 Statement of Cash Flows

  • Operating cash flows use the direct method, with a reconciliation of operating income to net cash from operating activities.
  • Four categories: operating, noncapital financing, capital and related financing, and investing.
  • Noncash investing, capital, and financing activities are disclosed separately.

5.5 Reporting ISFs in the Combined Statements

  • A government may have several ISFs. Their totals are shown in a single separate column to the right of the Total Enterprise Funds column in the proprietary fund statements.
  • In the government-wide statements, ISFs are normally combined with governmental activities, because their customers are mostly the government's own departments.

6. Enterprise Funds

Enterprise Funds are used when a government is in the business of providing goods or services to customers who, to a significant extent, are not part of the government. Public benefit corporations and authorities, which are separate legal entities created for business-type activities, also use Enterprise Fund accounting.

Entries are generally like those of an ISF, with these differences: sales are to outsiders, so Accounts Receivable (not Due from General Fund) is reported, and the fund may issue revenue bonds. The statements required are the same as for an ISF.

A city water utility issues $5,000,000 of 6% revenue bonds at par on October 1. Interest is paid each April 1 and October 1. The utility's own revenues, not taxes, repay the bonds.

Why the contrast with a Debt Service Fund matters
An Enterprise Fund accrues interest at year-end because it uses full accrual. A governmental Debt Service Fund records interest only when it is due and payable. The liability is also reported directly in the Enterprise Fund, but for general obligation debt it appears only in the government-wide statements.

6.2 Special Assessments

Special assessments finance services or capital improvements for one group of citizens rather than the general public, such as an alley, road maintenance, street lighting, or special police protection. Property owners who benefit are assessed for their share of the cost. In an Enterprise Fund, assessments to taxpayers result in receivables and revenues.

7.1 Leases in Proprietary Funds (Rubyville Illustration)

Proprietary funds use full accrual, so a long-term lease puts the intangible right-to-use asset and the lease payable directly on the fund's books. Assume Rubyville's utility signs a 5-year lease with $200,000 payments due at the beginning of each year, at 8%. The present value is $200,000 × 4.31213 = $862,426. Short-term leases (12 months or less) are simply Dr Lease Expense, Cr Cash when the payment is made.

Accrued interest at year-end
A payment made on the first day of the year pays for the year ahead, so interest builds up during the year. Proprietary funds use full accrual, so the interest is recorded as an expense in the year it is incurred. A governmental fund, by contrast, shows the interest as an expenditure when it is paid (see Chapter 6).

8. Check Yourself

Why is a city motor pool an Internal Service Fund rather than an Enterprise Fund?

Show answer

Its customers are the city's own departments and the city is the predominant participant. An Enterprise Fund is for activities that charge significantly outside users.

A city operates a water utility financed by revenue bonds backed only by the utility's net revenues. Which fund type is required?

Show answer

An Enterprise Fund, because debt secured only by the activity's net revenues meets the first criterion.

Is a $500,000 General Fund transfer to create a motor pool fund revenue?

Show answer

No. It is a nonoperating transfer in, reported below operating income. It increases net position and is closed to Fund Net Position.

Where does the Motor Pool Fund report the $12,000 uncollected from the General Fund?

Show answer

As Due from General Fund, a current asset. It is a reconciling item in the cash flow statement: an increase in a receivable reduces operating cash.

Why does the statement of cash flows reconcile operating income, and what does depreciation do in that reconciliation?

Show answer

Operating income is on the accrual basis and cash flows are on a cash basis. Depreciation is a noncash expense, so it is added back.

Does a proprietary fund have a fund balance?

Show answer

No. It reports fund net position, split into net investment in capital assets, restricted, and unrestricted.

How is a governmental fund's lease accounting different from a proprietary fund's?

Show answer

A governmental fund records capital outlay (expenditure) and an other financing source, then principal and interest as expenditures when paid. A proprietary fund records the intangible asset and lease payable, then amortization and interest expense.

9. Quick Review Summary & Exam Traps

  • Proprietary funds: Economic resources focus, full accrual, capital assets and long-term debt on the books. Three statements; cash flow statement is required.
  • ISF vs. EF: Internal customers vs. external customers. ISFs are shown with governmental activities in the government-wide statements; EFs with business-type activities.
  • EF required: Revenue-bond debt secured only by net revenues, legal cost-recovery requirement, or a pricing policy to recover costs.
  • Budgets: Flexible budgets, usually no budget entries and no encumbrances.
  • Fund net position: Net investment in capital assets, restricted, unrestricted. Restricted may not be negative.
  • Cash flows: Direct method, four categories, plus noncash disclosures. Reconcile operating income to operating cash.
  • Transfers in: Nonoperating, below operating income. Charges for services are operating revenue.
  • ISF presentation: Combined into one column to the right of Total Enterprise Funds.
  • Special assessments: Receivable and revenue in an Enterprise Fund.
  • GASB vs. FASB: Modified approach, short/long-term leases, impairment, deferral of pension differences, fiduciary reporting, deferred inflows/outflows.
