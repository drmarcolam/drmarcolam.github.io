CORE LEARNING OBJECTIVES:
1. Define the nature, legal necessity, and operating rationale of fund accounting.
2. Identify the three core fund categories and differentiate their measurement focus and basis of accounting.
3. Master the specific objectives, allowable activities, and financial statements for each of the 11 fund types.
4. Apply revenue and expenditure recognition rules under modified accrual vs. full accrual accounting.

1. Fundamentals & Operational Purpose of Fund Accounting

Fund accounting is the foundation of state and local government financial management. Unlike commercial businesses, which operate as single consolidated economic entities focused on net income, governmental organizations manage diverse activities subject to legal restrictions, dedicated tax mandates, and budgetary limits.

Definition of a Fund: A fiscal and accounting entity with a self-balancing set of accounts recording cash and other financial resources, together with all related liabilities and residual equities or balances, and changes therein, which are segregated for the purpose of carrying on specific activities or attaining certain objectives in accordance with special regulations, restrictions, or limitations.

Governments establish separate funds for four primary administrative and legal reasons:

  • Legal & Budgetary Compliance: Facilitates strict adherence to statutory requirements, voter mandates, and legally adopted budgets that limit spending authority.
  • Resource Restriction Stewardship: Ensures that revenue dedicated by the legislature or donors for specific purposes (e.g., highway maintenance, education) is not diverted to general operations.
  • Administrative Control for Business Activities: Provides isolated accounting structures to evaluate cost recovery for commercial-type services such as municipal utilities and public transit.
  • Independent General Ledgers: Each fund maintains its own separate general ledger, assets, liabilities, net position/fund balance, and resource inflows and outflows.

2. Measurement Focus & Basis of Accounting Framework

To evaluate public accountability correctly, accounting measurements are tailored to the economic nature of each fund category. The measurement focus determines what resources are being measured, while the basis of accounting dictates when inflows and outflows are recognized.

Key Methodological Distinctions:

• Current Financial Resources vs. Economic Resources: Governmental funds focus strictly on expendable financial assets (cash, receivables, investments) and short-term / matured liabilities. Capital assets (buildings, equipment) and non-matured long-term debt (bonds payable) are EXCLUDED from governmental fund balance sheets. In contrast, proprietary and fiduciary funds record all capital assets and long-term liabilities.

• Modified Accrual Revenue Recognition: Revenues are recognized only when they become both Measurable (the amount can be determined) and Available (collectible within the current fiscal period or soon enough thereafter to pay current liabilities, typically within 60 days for property taxes).

• Expenditure vs. Expense Recognition: Governmental funds record 'expenditures' (decreases in financial resources) when liabilities are incurred. Capital asset purchases are fully expensed as capital outlay expenditures when acquired. Proprietary funds record 'expenses' (consumption of economic assets) and capitalize/depreciate capital assets over their estimated useful lives.

3. Analysis of the 11 Specific Governmental Fund Types

State and local government activities are divided among 11 distinct fund types across the three major categories. Below is a detailed structural breakdown of each fund's statutory purpose, funding sources, and reporting requirements.

A. The Five Governmental Funds (Current Financial Resources / Modified Accrual)

1. General Fund: Accounts for all financial resources not required to be accounted for in another fund. It is the primary operating fund for day-to-day general public services (police, fire, sanitation, parks). Every general-purpose government has exactly ONE General Fund. Primary revenues include property taxes, sales taxes, intergovernmental grants, licenses, and fees.

2. Special Revenue Funds: Accounts for proceeds of specific revenue sources that are restricted or committed to spending for specified purposes other than debt service or capital projects. Examples include dedicated motor fuel taxes for highway maintenance, hotel occupancy taxes for tourism, and specific state/federal operating grants.

3. Capital Projects Funds: Accounts for financial resources restricted, committed, or assigned for capital outlays, including the acquisition or construction of major capital facilities (roads, municipal buildings, bridges). Funding comes primarily from general obligation bond proceeds (reported as Other Financing Sources), federal construction grants, and transfers in.

4. Debt Service Funds: Accounts for financial resources accumulated to pay principal and interest on general long-term debt. Required when legally mandated or when resources are being accumulated for payments due in future years. Funding sources include dedicated property taxes and operating transfers from the General Fund.

5. Permanent Funds: Accounts for resources that are legally restricted so that ONLY earnings—and NOT principal—may be used to support programs that benefit the government or its citizens (e.g., public cemetery perpetual care endowment, public library trust).

B. The Two Proprietary Funds (Economic Resources / Full Accrual)

1. Enterprise Funds: Accounts for activities that supply goods or services for a fee to users external to the government. Mandatory if debt is backed solely by fee revenue, or if pricing policies/laws require cost recovery. Examples include municipal water/sewer utilities, mass transit, electric utilities, toll roads, parking garages, and airports.

2. Internal Service Funds: Accounts for goods or services provided on a cost-reimbursement basis by one department/agency to other departments within the government (or to other governments). Established for cost efficiency and bulk purchasing. Examples include central motor pools, data processing, printing services, and central supplies distribution.

C. The Four Fiduciary Funds (Economic Resources / Full Accrual)

1. Pension (and Other Employee Benefit) Trust Funds: Accounts for resources held in qualifying irrevocable trusts for public employee retirement plans, retiree health benefit plans, and other employee post-employment benefits.

2. Investment Trust Funds: Accounts for the external portion of government-sponsored investment pools managed by a sponsoring government on behalf of outside entities.

3. Private-Purpose Trust Funds: Accounts for all other trust arrangements under which principal and/or income benefit specific individuals, private organizations, or other governments (e.g., state-managed unclaimed/escheat property held pending owner claims).

4. Custodial Funds: Accounts for fiduciary activities not required to be reported in trust funds where the government holds assets temporarily (e.g., property taxes collected by a county on behalf of local school districts).

4. Transaction Accounting Equations & Recognition Rules

Because governmental funds measure current financial resources, their fundamental accounting equation differs significantly from commercial and proprietary accrual accounting:

Governmental Fund Accounting Equation:
Financial Assets = Short-Term & Matured Liabilities + Fund Balance

2. A city paid salaries of $450,000 and owes $18,000 more for work already performed at year-end. How much is recorded in (a) the General Fund and (b) an Enterprise Fund?

Show answer

Both record $468,000: an expenditure in the General Fund (the liability was incurred) and an expense in the Enterprise Fund. Salaries are an ordinary operating cost that is normally paid soon, so both bases accrue it.

3. On the first day of the year, a fund buys equipment for $90,000 with a 6-year life, financed with bond proceeds. What does each fund record for the year (straight-line, no salvage)?

Show answer
  • Governmental fund: $90,000 capital outlay expenditure. The equipment and the bonds are not reported in the fund.
  • Enterprise Fund: The equipment is capitalized and $15,000 of depreciation expense is recorded ($90,000 ÷ 6).

4. A fund pays $24,000 on debt: $20,000 principal and $4,000 interest. What is recorded as an expenditure or expense?

Show answer
  • Debt Service Fund: $24,000 debt service expenditure (principal and interest).
  • Enterprise Fund: $4,000 interest expense. The $20,000 principal payment reduces the liability.

5. A General Fund levies $500,000 of property taxes and expects $20,000 to be uncollectible. Of the $480,000 expected to be collected, $460,000 arrives during the year, $15,000 within 60 days after year-end, and $5,000 after 100 days. How much revenue does the General Fund report, and how much would full accrual report?

Show answer
  • Modified accrual: $475,000 ($460,000 + $15,000). Only amounts collected within the year or 60 days after are available. The $5,000 expected later is a deferred inflow.
  • Full accrual: $480,000. There is no availability test.

6. Why does the General Fund report no building or bonds payable, while an Enterprise Fund does?

Show answer

Governmental funds use the current financial resources focus: only spendable financial assets and short-term or matured liabilities. Proprietary funds use the economic resources focus, so all assets (including capital assets) and all liabilities (including long-term debt) are reported.

7. How many General Funds can a government have? Special Revenue Funds?

Show answer

Exactly one General Fund. Zero, one, or many Special Revenue Funds, depending on how many restricted or committed revenue sources it has.

8. Write the accounting equation for a governmental fund.

Show answer

Financial assets (+ deferred outflows) = short-term and matured liabilities (+ deferred inflows) + fund balance.
