1. Core Learning Objectives & Chapter Blueprint

Chapter 6 addresses the accounting treatment for major infrastructure construction, long-term debt servicing, governmental leasing, and permanent endowments. Upon completing this study guide, students will be able to:

  • LO 6-1: Measurement Focus & Asset/Liability Exclusion: Explain why capital assets and general long-term liabilities are excluded from governmental fund balance sheets under the modified accrual basis.
  • LO 6-2: Capital Projects Fund (CPF) Mechanics: Account for multi-year capital budgets, general obligation bond sales, intergovernmental grants, retainage payable, progress billings, and encumbrance reversals.
  • LO 6-3: Complex Bond Issuances & Arbitrage: Journalize bond sales at par, premium, and discount, bond issuance costs, interest accrued between payment dates, and federal arbitrage rebate liabilities.
  • LO 6-4: Debt Service Fund (DSF) Operations: Apply the 'due and payable' expenditure rule for debt service, the 1-month fiscal year-end accrual exception, and advance refunding escrow mechanics.
  • LO 6-5: GASB 87 Lease Accounting: Distinguish short-term and long-term lease accounting in governmental funds, calculating intangible right-to-use capital outlays and lease liability amortization.
  • LO 6-6: Permanent Funds vs. Fiduciary Trusts: Differentiate Permanent Funds (nonexpendable principal benefiting government programs) from Fiduciary Private-Purpose Trust Funds.

2. Measurement Focus and Accounting Basis for Long-Term Funds

Governmental funds operate strictly under the current financial resources measurement focus and modified accrual basis. Long-lived physical infrastructure and general long-term debt are excluded from fund balance sheets.

Why capital assets and bonds never appear in these funds
A bridge is not a spendable financial resource, and bonds that are not yet due do not claim current financial resources. Both are reported only in the government-wide statements (see Chapter 10). The fund records the cash spent on the bridge as an expenditure and the cash received from the bonds as an other financing source.

3. Capital Projects Funds (CPF)

3.1 What a Capital Projects Fund Does

  • Purpose: Accounts for financial resources restricted or otherwise limited to spending for capital outlays, including the acquisition or construction of major capital facilities and other capital assets (other than those financed by proprietary and trust funds).
  • When required: A CPF must be used when capital outlays are financed from general obligation bond proceeds, or when it is otherwise legally required.
  • Typical projects: Buildings, roads, bridges, and other major capital assets. Smaller items such as buses, fire trucks, vehicles, and furniture are often acquired through the General Fund or a Special Revenue Fund instead.
  • Common financing: General obligation bonds, federal or state grants, transfers from other funds, and investment earnings.
  • End of project: Resources remaining in the CPF are transferred to another fund (often a Debt Service Fund) or, in the case of grants, possibly returned to the grantor.

3.2 Capital Budgets and Control

A capital budget controls a capital project and may cover multiple years (often 5 to 10). The sample capital budget below shows how requests are matched to funding sources:

CPF activity is controlled through three layers: the provisions of bond indentures and grant agreements, the budget recorded in the accounts, and encumbrance accounting. Encumbrances let a project manager see, from the subsidiary ledgers, what has been approved, what has been spent, and what has only been committed, so the remaining spendable resources are always known.

Closing entries in a CPF
A CPF's life follows the project, not the fiscal year. Closing entries are not strictly needed to know where the project stands, but for multi-year projects they are used at each year-end to prepare annual statements.

3.5 Investments Held by a Fund

Investments are securities or other assets held primarily for income or profit that generate cash or can be sold for cash. They are generally reported at fair value, adjusted at year-end, and the change in fair value is reported in the operating statement along with dividends and interest.

Bond proceeds are an other financing source, never a revenue. The face amount is recorded separately from any premium or discount. The examples use a $2,000,000 general obligation bond issue.

  • Premium: More resources than planned. The bond covenant typically says how the premium is used: it may be spent on the project or transferred to the Debt Service Fund to help repay the debt (CPF: Dr Transfer Out to Debt Service Fund, Cr Cash).
  • Discount: Fewer resources than planned. The government must scale down the project or seek additional resources.
  • Accrued interest: The government that sells bonds between interest dates receives the interest accrued to the sale date from the buyer.
Arbitrage
Arbitrage means borrowing at a lower tax-exempt interest rate and investing the proceeds at a higher taxable rate. Federal rules strictly control the practice for tax-exempt bonds. A government that earns excess interest must pay it to the federal government, may owe an excise tax on the excess earnings, or risks losing the debt's tax-exempt status. It matters in Capital Projects Funds because bond proceeds sit invested until they are spent. Professional guidance is recommended.

6. Debt Service Funds (DSF)

6.1 Purpose and Funding

  • Purpose: Accounts for financial resources that are restricted or otherwise limited to spending for principal and interest on general long-term debt.
  • Required when: Legally mandated, or when resources are being accumulated for principal and interest payments that come due in future years.
  • Common funding sources: Property taxes assessed specifically to service debt, transfers in from the General Fund or other funds (an other financing source), and investment earnings.
  • Control: Mainly bond indentures and budgetary authorizations. Some governments record a budget in the accounts and some do not, because the fund's activity is limited. Managers must check the covenants so none is violated.

6.2 When Debt Service Is Recognized

  • Interest and principal are expenditures when due and payable. Long-term debt is not recorded as a fund liability or expenditure before it legally matures.
  • Why not accrue early? Most appropriations include only the debt service that comes due during the year, and the resources to pay may not yet be in the fund. Accruing a liability before it is due could produce a deficit fund balance.
  • Option for the first month of the next year: At year-end a government may record a liability and expenditure for debt service payments made within the first month of the next fiscal year, if resources are available in the DSF for the payment. This applies to principal and interest.
  • Matured Interest Payable: The textbook records this account to emphasize that interest is not accrued until due. In practice, if cash is paid the same day, a government may debit Expenditures—Interest and credit Cash directly.

6.5 Refunding Bonds

Refunding means paying off old bonds with the proceeds of new bonds. Governments refund to lower the interest rate, extend the maturity date, change payment schedules, or remove covenants tied to the existing bonds.

Swipe the table sideways to see all columns.

TypeHow It Works
Current refundingProceeds from the refunding bonds are used to pay off the existing bonds immediately.
Advance refundingProceeds are placed with an escrow agent and invested until the old debt is retired at a later date. This happens when the old bonds are not callable until a later date.

Governmental funds vs. government-wide statements
Governmental funds do not report the intangible right-to-use asset, its amortization, or the lease liability. They report the PV as capital outlay with an offsetting other financing source, and then the principal and interest paid as expenditures. The asset and liability appear only in the government-wide statements (Chapter 10). Proprietary funds record the intangible asset and lease payable directly (Chapter 7).

8. Permanent Funds

Permanent Funds account for resources that are legally restricted so that (1) only earnings, not principal, can be spent, and (2) the earnings must support the government's own programs, benefiting the government or its citizens (for example, a cemetery perpetual-care endowment or a library trust).

Swipe the table sideways to see all columns.

FeaturePermanent Fund (governmental)Private-Purpose Trust Fund (fiduciary)
Who benefitsThe government's own programs and its citizens.Individuals, private organizations, or other governments outside the government.
What may be spentOnly the earnings; principal is preserved.Principal and earnings, if the trust agreement allows.
Measurement focusCurrent financial resources, modified accrual.Economic resources, full accrual.

Permanent Fund entries look like those of other governmental funds. Earnings may be spent directly or transferred out to another fund, such as a Special Revenue Fund that supports a specific program. That fund then budgets the money as part of its own budget process. The fund is controlled through state law and the individual trust agreement, so the accounting must support a review of the government's stewardship. A formal budget is not required unless a legal provision demands one.

10. Quick Review Summary & Exam Traps

  • Capital assets and unmatured bonds: Never recorded in governmental funds. Capital outlay is an expenditure; bond proceeds are an other financing source.
  • Premium and discount: Other financing source (premium) and other financing use (discount), not revenue or expense. Record the face amount separately.
  • Issuance costs: Expenditures—Bond Issue Costs.
  • Encumbrances: Recorded at contract signing; reversed for the exact amount billed.
  • Retainage Payable: Credited when the contractor bills the government; paid after final approval.
  • Grants received early: Advance on Construction Grant (liability) until eligibility requirements are met, then revenue.
  • Debt service: Interest and principal are expenditures only when due and payable (with an option for payments made in the first month of the next year if resources are on hand).
  • Debt Service Fund funding: Dedicated property taxes, transfers in (an other financing source), and investment earnings.
  • Refunding: Current refunding pays off the old bonds immediately; advance refunding places proceeds with an escrow agent.
  • Leases: Short-term (12 months or less) → expenditure when paid. Long-term → capital outlay equal to the present value, an other financing source, then principal and interest expenditures.
  • Permanent Fund vs. Private-Purpose Trust Fund: The difference is who benefits (own programs vs. outsiders) and whether principal can be spent.
