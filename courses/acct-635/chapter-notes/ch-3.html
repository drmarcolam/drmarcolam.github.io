2. Budget Enactment Laws, Budget Types & Process

Balanced Budget Laws & Financial Management

A balanced budget is defined as a financial state where estimated revenues and amounts available from previously accumulated fund balances equal total appropriations. Most state and municipal governments are legally required to enact balanced budgets prior to the start of each fiscal period.

Types of Budgets & Approaches

Governments utilize two primary types of budgets to plan operations and capital acquisition:

  • Operating Budget: Covers day-to-day operating activities for a fiscal period (typically annual or biennial). Applies at minimum to the General Fund and significant Special Revenue Funds. Incorporates prior year actuals and current year estimated figures.
  • Capital Budget: Formulates long-term plans for acquiring or constructing long-lived assets (land, buildings, equipment). Critical for municipal debt planning and capital project fund financing.

Budgetary Approaches: Governments implement various budgeting methodologies, including Object-of-Expenditure (traditional line-item approach showing expenditure categories), Performance and Planning-Programming-Budgeting (PPB), and Zero-Based Budgeting.

The 10-Step Budget Process Cycle

The budget process is a continuous, multi-month cycle where current-year evaluation directly shapes the subsequent year's plan:

3. Revenue Generation & Property Tax Millage Calculations

While taxes like income tax, sales tax, and license fees result from prior ongoing legislation, property taxes require specific annual legal action by the legislative body to set the required levy and millage rate.

Millage Rate Calculation Formula

Property Tax Millage Rate Core Formulas
1. Required Tax Levy = Budgeted Property Tax Revenue Needed / Expected Collection Percentage
2. Net Assessed Value = Total Assessed Value - Exemptions (Government/Religious Property, Veterans, Senior, Housing, etc.)
3. Millage Rate = Required Tax Levy / Net Assessed Value of Property
(Expressed as a decimal or as a rate per $1,000 of net assessed value).

4. Cash Forecasting & Short-Term Financing (TANs)

Municipal cash receipts (e.g., semi-annual property tax collections) are typically highly uneven, whereas operating expenditures (salaries, equipment, utilities) occur continuously throughout the fiscal year. Cash forecasts allow financial managers to anticipate cash deficits, arrange Tax Anticipation Notes (TANs), and invest temporary surpluses.

Key Cash Management Insight
In April, total disbursements ($230,500) exceeded cash available ($141,000), leaving a deficit of $99,500 relative to the required $10,000 minimum balance. When heavy property tax revenues ($230,500) arrived in June, the city fully repaid the $100,000 TANs plus $950 total interest.

5. Revenue & Expenditure Classifications

To ensure legal compliance and public accountability, governmental accounting requires strict multi-level classification for revenues and expenditures.

Revenue Classification Structure

Revenues are classified primarily by Fund, and secondarily by Source within each fund:

  • Taxes (Property, Sales, Income)
  • Charges for Services
  • Fines and Forfeitures
  • Fees for Licenses and Permits
  • Interest on Investments
  • Intergovernmental Revenues (Grants, Shared Revenues)

The 5 Levels of Expenditure Subclassification

Expenditures are categorized across five hierarchical levels to track spending authority from broad government purpose down to specific item type:

6. Budgetary Accounting & Journal Entry Mechanics

State and local governments incorporate budgetary accounts directly into their General Ledger. There are five primary types of budgetary journal entries:
1. Recording the adopted budget
2. Recording budget revisions and interchanges
3. Recording encumbrances (issuing purchase orders/contracts)
4. Reversing encumbrances upon receipt of goods/services
5. Closing budgetary entries at year-end.

A. Recording the Adopted Budget

Estimated Revenues are DEBITED, Appropriations are CREDITED, and the balancing difference is debited or credited to Budgetary Fund Balance:

B. Budgetary Interchanges vs. Revisions

Budgetary Interchange: Reallocates spending authority between departments without changing total budget size (e.g., reduce Other-Than-Personnel by $8,000 to increase Personnel Services by $8,000):
Dr. Appropriations—Police Other-Than-Personnel $8,000
Cr. Appropriations—Police Personnel Services $8,000

Budgetary Revision: Net modification to total budget appropriations (e.g., increasing police personnel appropriation by $8,000 without reducing other appropriations):
Dr. Budgetary Fund Balance $8,000
Cr. Appropriations—Police Personnel Services $8,000

7. Subsidiary Ledgers & Budgetary Controls

Governments maintain detailed subsidiary ledgers to ensure individual revenue sources and departmental appropriations stay within legal limits.

Allotment Systems

An allotment system subdivides annual appropriations into time-based periods (e.g., quarterly allotments equal to 1/4 of total appropriation) to prevent departments from exhausting funds early in the fiscal year.

8. Quick Review Summary & Exam Key Takeaways

  • Balanced Budget Rule: Revenues + Available Fund Balance = Appropriations.
  • Property Tax Millage Formula: Required Levy = Needed Revenue / Collection Rate %. Millage Rate = Required Levy / Net Assessed Value.
  • TANs Short-Term Financing: Issued to bridge temporary cash flow deficits caused by uneven tax collections.
  • 5 Expenditure Subclassifications: Fund -> Function/Program -> Organizational Unit -> Activity -> Object.
  • Budgetary Control Entry: Debit Estimated Revenues, Credit Appropriations, Difference to Budgetary Fund Balance.
  • Encumbrance Rule: Always reverse encumbrance for the EXACT originally encumbered PO amount, then record expenditure for actual invoice amount.
  • Available Appropriation: Appropriation Credit - Current Encumbrances Debit - Current Expenditures Debit.

1. A city needs $8,400,000 of property tax revenue and expects to collect 98% of the levy. Total assessed value is $1,500,000,000, less $150,000,000 of exemptions. Compute (a) the required levy, (b) the millage rate, and (c) the tax on a $250,000 property.

Show answer
  1. Required levy = $8,400,000 ÷ 0.98 = $8,571,429.
  2. Net assessed value = $1,500,000,000 − $150,000,000 = $1,350,000,000. Rate = $8,571,429 ÷ $1,350,000,000 = 0.006349, or 6.349 mills ($6.349 per $1,000).
  3. Tax = 250 × $6.349 = $1,587.30.

2. A General Fund starts a quarter with $20,000 cash, expects $70,000 of receipts and $130,000 of disbursements, and must keep $15,000 minimum cash. How much should it borrow on a tax anticipation note, and what is the repayment if the note runs 3 months at 6%?

Show answer
  1. Cash available = $20,000 + $70,000 = $90,000.
  2. Need = $130,000 + $15,000 minimum − $90,000 = $55,000 to borrow.
  3. Interest = $55,000 × 6% × 3/12 = $825. Repay $55,825.
