Capital projects

Building a Project: Retainage and Encumbrances

Laurel Gap signs a $2,500,000 construction contract for a community center. The contractor's first progress billing is $600,000, with 10% retainage.

  • Record an encumbrance when the contract is signed. Reverse it for the amount billed.
  • Retainage Payable is credited when the contractor bills, and is paid after final approval.
  • Grant cash received before eligibility is met is a liability.
Example 1 — Record the Billing

Choose Debit or Credit for each account in the entry for the first progress billing.

Solution

Contractor's first billing:

EventAccountDebit ($)Credit ($)
1
Contractor's first billing		Expenditures—Construction Costs600,000
    Retainage Payable60,000
    Construction Contracts Payable540,000

The full billing is an expenditure; 10% is held back as retainage.

Example 2 — Retainage

How much retainage is held back?

$
Solution

$600,000 × 10% = $60,000.

Retainage = $60,000

Example 3 — Payable Now

How much is currently payable to the contractor?

$
Solution

$600,000 − $60,000 = $540,000.

Currently payable = $540,000

Example 4 — Remaining Encumbrance

After reversing the encumbrance for the billing, how much remains encumbered?

$
Solution

$2,500,000 − $600,000 = $1,900,000.

Open encumbrance = $1,900,000

Example 5 — Advance Grant Cash

The town receives a $500,000 state grant before qualifying expenditures are made. How is it reported until eligibility requirements are met?

Solution

Eligibility requirements are not yet met, so the cash is an advance (liability). It becomes revenue as qualifying expenditures are made.

Bond issuance

Selling Bonds at Par, Premium and Discount

The town sells $3,000,000 of general obligation bonds.

Bond proceeds are an other financing source, never revenue. A premium is an other financing source, a discount is an other financing use, and issuance costs are expenditures.

Example 1 — Sold at 102

The bonds are sold at 102. How much cash does the fund receive?

$
Solution

$3,000,000 × 1.02 = $3,060,000.

Cash = $3,060,000

Example 2 — Record the Premium Sale

Choose Debit or Credit for each account.

Solution

Sell at 102:

EventAccountDebit ($)Credit ($)
2
Sell at 102		Cash3,060,000
    Other Financing Source—Long-Term Debt Issued3,000,000
    Other Financing Source—Bond Issue Premium60,000

The $60,000 premium is reported separately from the face amount.

Example 3 — Sold at 98

If the bonds had instead been sold at 98, how much cash would the fund receive?

$
Solution

$3,000,000 × 0.98 = $2,940,000. The $60,000 discount is an other financing use.

Cash = $2,940,000

Example 4 — Classify Each Item

How is each item reported in the Capital Projects Fund?

Solution

Governmental funds do not record revenue for borrowing. Issuance costs are expenditures.

Debt service

Paying the Debt

A Debt Service Fund records debt service when it is due and payable, not before.

Long-term debt is not a fund liability until it matures. An option allows recording payments due in the first month of the next year if resources are on hand at year-end.

Example 1 — Interest Comes Due

Interest of $380,000 becomes due and payable. Choose Debit or Credit.

Solution

Interest due:

EventAccountDebit ($)Credit ($)
3
Interest due		Expenditures—Interest380,000
    Matured Interest Payable380,000

The liability is recorded because the interest is now due.

Example 2 — Debt Service Expenditures

In addition to the $380,000 of interest, $250,000 of serial bond principal comes due. What are total debt service expenditures?

$
Solution

$380,000 + $250,000 = $630,000.

Total = $630,000

Example 3 — Net Change in Fund Balance

The fund has revenues of $940,000 and a transfer in of $150,000. What is the net change in fund balance?

$
Solution

$940,000 − $630,000 + $150,000 = $460,000.

Net change = $460,000

Leases

GASB 87 Long-Term Leases

The town signs a 5-year lease for equipment. Payments of $20,000 are due at the start of each year (first at signing). The town's rate is 7%.

A lease over 12 months is a financing. The governmental fund records capital outlay equal to the present value and an other financing source, then principal and interest expenditures as paid.

Example 1 — Present Value

The present value of the payments is $20,000 × (1 + the 4-period ordinary annuity factor at 7%). What is the lease present value? (Round to the nearest dollar.)

Need a hint?
The first payment is made immediately, so it is not discounted.
$
Solution

Annuity due: $20,000 × (1 + 3.38721) = $87,744.

Present value = $87,744

Example 2 — Record the Lease

Choose Debit or Credit for each account at lease inception.

Solution

Lease begins:

EventAccountDebit ($)Credit ($)
4
Lease begins		Expenditures—Capital Outlay87,744
    Other Financing Source—Long-Term Leases87,744

The fund does not record the intangible asset or the liability.

Example 3 — Liability After the First Payment

The first $20,000 payment is all principal. What is the lease liability afterward?

$
Solution

$87,744 − $20,000 = $67,744.

Liability = $67,744

Example 4 — Year-2 Interest

Year-2 interest is 7% of the remaining liability. What is it? (Round to the nearest dollar.)

$
Solution

$67,744 × 7% = $4,742.

Interest = $4,742

Example 5 — Year-2 Principal

How much of the year-2 payment is principal?

$
Solution

$20,000 − $4,742 = $15,258.

Principal = $15,258

Permanent funds

Endowments and Refunding

A donor gives $150,000 to a library endowment. Investment income is $9,000 this year, and $7,500 is transferred to the Library Special Revenue Fund.

Example 1 — Restricted Cash from Earnings

How much earnings cash remains in the Permanent Fund at year-end?

$
Solution

$9,000 − $7,500 = $1,500. The $150,000 principal is Nonspendable; the unspent earnings are Restricted.

Earnings cash = $1,500

Example 2 — Permanent Fund or Private-Purpose Trust?

Choose the fund.

Solution

The difference is who benefits: the government's own programs or outside parties.

Example 3 — Current or Advance Refunding?

Which refunding type fits?

Solution

An advance refunding uses an escrow agent to hold proceeds until the old debt is retired.
