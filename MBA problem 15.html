Why would a company in this position grant discounts of that size?

  1. To buy its way onto the shelf. A new entrant in an established category has to persuade grocers and foodservice distributors to give it space and to persuade shoppers to try an unfamiliar product, and price concessions do both. The cost is accepted as an investment in volume and distribution.
  2. Because the discounts are required by law in the food industry, which caps the price that may be charged to distributors.
  3. Because the company sells perishable goods below cost as a matter of policy, which is why it reports a gross loss.
  4. Because discounts are a form of advertising expense and are recorded as marketing costs rather than as a reduction of revenue.

      (c) The distributor concentration disclosure

      1. Because losing either distributor, or renegotiating with one on worse terms, would put a large share of revenue at risk at once. Investors price that concentration risk, and the disclosure tells them how exposed the company is to a handful of customer relationships.
      2. Because the SEC requires companies to name every customer that accounts for more than 1% of revenue so that investors can contact them directly.
      3. Because distributors that buy in volume are entitled to a share of the company’s profits, which must be disclosed as a related-party transaction.
      4. Because concentration among a few distributors is evidence of efficient operations, and companies disclose it to signal that their selling costs are low.
