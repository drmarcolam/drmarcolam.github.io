Inventory systems, ownership, and cost of goods sold

Before applying a cost flow method we must decide which goods belong in inventory and which costs are included.

  • Inventory is tangible property held for sale in the ordinary course of business, in production for sale, or to be consumed in production. A manufacturer has raw materials, work in process, and finished goods; a merchandiser has merchandise inventory.
  • Perpetual system: update Inventory and Cost of goods sold with each purchase and sale. Periodic system: record purchases in a Purchases account and determine ending inventory (and COGS) by a physical count at the end of the period.
  • Which goods to include: goods in transit belong to the party with legal title. FOB shipping point: title passes at shipment (buyer includes). FOB destination: title passes on delivery (seller includes until delivered). Goods on consignment remain in the consignor’s inventory.
  • Cost of goods sold (periodic) = beginning inventory + net purchases + freight-in − ending inventory. Net purchases = gross purchases − purchase returns − purchase discounts.

Cost flow assumptions

FIFO, LIFO, and average cost allocate the cost of goods available between ending inventory and cost of goods sold.

  • Specific identification: track the actual cost of each item. FIFO: first costs purchased are first costs sold; ending inventory is the most recent costs. LIFO: last costs purchased are first sold; ending inventory is the oldest costs. Average cost: weighted-average (periodic) or moving-average (perpetual) unit cost.
  • Cost of goods available = beginning inventory + purchases. Ending inventory + COGS = goods available.
  • FIFO gives the same result under periodic and perpetual. LIFO and average cost can differ between systems.
  • When prices are rising: FIFO gives lower COGS, higher income, higher ending inventory; LIFO gives higher COGS, lower income, lower taxes, and lower ending inventory.
  • Choice factors: income and taxes, cash flows, financial reporting, tracking physical flow, and the LIFO conformity rule (if LIFO is used for tax, it must also be used for financial reporting).

Example 7 — Effects of inflation on the cost flow choice

Assume purchase prices are rising throughout the period and inventory quantities are stable.
Need a hint?
Rising prices: the latest costs are the highest. FIFO puts them in ending inventory; LIFO puts them in cost of goods sold.

Solution

  • a. FIFO (lowest COGS).
  • b. LIFO (highest COGS means lowest taxable income).
  • c. LIFO matches current costs to revenue.
  • d. FIFO keeps recent costs on the balance sheet.
  • e. Use LIFO for financial reporting too.

LIFO reserve, ratios, dollar-value LIFO, and IFRS

Supplemental disclosures help analysts compare companies that use different inventory methods.

  • LIFO reserve = FIFO inventory − LIFO inventory. Analysts add it to LIFO inventory to estimate FIFO inventory. The change in the reserve adjusts COGS: FIFO COGS = LIFO COGS − increase in the reserve.
  • A LIFO liquidation occurs when inventory quantity falls and older, lower costs flow into cost of goods sold, increasing income (and tax).
  • Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ average inventory. Average days in inventory = 365 ÷ turnover. Gross profit ratio = gross profit ÷ net sales.
  • Dollar-value LIFO treats inventory as layers measured in dollars. Convert ending inventory at current cost to base-year cost (÷ cost index). If it exceeds the prior base-year amount, add a layer measured at the current-year index.
  • IFRS: LIFO is prohibited. Companies use FIFO or average cost (or specific identification).

Example 8 — LIFO reserve

Mitchell Peak Co. uses LIFO. It reports LIFO inventory of $500,000 and a LIFO reserve of $120,000 at year-end. The reserve increased by $30,000 during the year. LIFO cost of goods sold was $900,000. The tax rate is 25%.
Need a hint?
Add the reserve to LIFO inventory. FIFO COGS = LIFO COGS − increase in the reserve.

Solution

FIFO inventory = $500,000 + $120,000 = $620,000. FIFO COGS = $900,000 − $30,000 = $870,000 (pre-tax income would be $30,000 higher).

Cumulative taxes deferred: $120,000 × 25% = $30,000.

Example 9 — LIFO liquidation

Rutherford Rubber began the year with a LIFO layer of 2,000 units at $8. During the year, it purchased 10,000 units at $15 and sold 12,000 units. Tax rate: 25%.
Need a hint?
When sales exceed purchases, older layers flow to COGS at old costs. Compare the old cost with the current cost.
$
$

Solution

The 2,000 liquidated units cost $8 instead of the current $15, so COGS is lower by 2,000 × ($15 − $8) = $14,000 and pre-tax income is higher by that amount. Additional tax: $14,000 × 25% = $3,500. After-tax income rises $10,500. The increase is not from improved operations.

Wrap-up: Chapter 8 at a glance

  • Inventory systems: perpetual updates inventory with every transaction; periodic uses a count to determine COGS.
  • Ownership: include goods to which the company has title (FOB terms) and its own consigned goods; exclude goods held as a consignee.
  • Cost flow: FIFO, LIFO, and average cost; in rising prices, LIFO produces higher COGS, lower income and tax, and lower inventory.
  • Analysis: LIFO reserve converts LIFO to FIFO. Inventory turnover = COGS ÷ average inventory.
  • Dollar-value LIFO measures layers using price indexes. IFRS prohibits LIFO.
