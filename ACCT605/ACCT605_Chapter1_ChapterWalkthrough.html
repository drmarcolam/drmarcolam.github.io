The purpose of financial accounting

Start with who uses financial information, what they need it for, and why accrual accounting does a better job than cash-basis accounting over short periods.

  • Financial accounting provides financial information to external users, chiefly investors and creditors. The primary objective is to help them evaluate the amounts, timing, and uncertainty of future cash flows.
  • Rate of return = (dividends + change in share price) ÷ initial investment. Investors weigh expected return against risk.
  • Cash-basis accounting measures net operating cash flow (cash receipts minus cash payments). Over short periods it can give an uneven picture of performance.
  • Accrual accounting measures revenues (resources provided by operations) and expenses (resources consumed) when they occur, so net income is usually a better predictor of future cash flows. Over a company’s life the two totals agree; only the timing differs.

Example 1 — Rate of return for an investor

On January 1, , an investor buys 1,000 shares of Blue Ridge Outfitters stock for $20,000. During the year the company pays dividends of $600, and on December 31 the investor sells the shares for $21,400.
Need a hint?
Return has two parts: cash received during the year (dividends) and the change in share price. Divide the total by the amount invested.

Solution

Appreciation: $21,400 − $20,000 = $1,400. Total return: $600 + $1,400 = $2,000.

Rate of return = $2,000 ÷ $20,000 = 10%.

Investors and creditors supply capital only if they expect more cash back later, which is why financial accounting focuses on information that helps predict future cash flows.

Standard setting and high-quality reporting

Accounting standards come from a layered structure of government and private-sector bodies, and several safeguards support high-quality reporting.

  • SEC: the government agency with authority to set accounting standards for public companies. It has delegated standard setting to the private sector but retains the power.
  • FASB: seven full-time members; issues Accounting Standards Updates (ASUs) that are added to the Accounting Standards Codification, the single source of authoritative nongovernmental U.S. GAAP. GASB sets standards for state and local governments. EITF resolves narrow implementation issues.
  • IASB: issues International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), used or permitted in more than 120 jurisdictions.
  • Standard setting is a political process: changes can redistribute wealth, so interest groups lobby the FASB (for example, on employee stock options).
  • Management is responsible for applying GAAP. Auditors give an independent opinion on whether the statements are fairly presented. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act (2002) created the PCAOB, restricts auditor non-audit services, requires lead-partner rotation, and (Section 404) requires management and auditors to report on internal control.

Need a hint?
Think about public vs. private sector, U.S. vs. international, and business vs. government.

Solution

  • a. SEC. Congress gave it the authority; it delegated the work to the private sector.
  • b. FASB.
  • c. IASB.
  • d. PCAOB.
  • e. GASB. The FAF oversees and funds both the FASB and the GASB.

Example 4 — Reporting responsibilities and high-quality reporting

Choose the party that best fits each responsibility.
Need a hint?
Audits add credibility, but the audit is not the preparation of the statements.

Solution

  • a. Management. Management is responsible for the statements.
  • b. External auditor. A clean (unmodified) opinion says the statements present fairly, in all material respects, in conformity with GAAP.
  • c. PCAOB. Created by the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002 after Enron and WorldCom.
  • d. SEC.

Example 5 — Rules-based versus objectives-oriented standards

Marion Valley Manufacturing is reviewing its accounting for a complex transaction. Compare the two approaches to standard setting.
Need a hint?
An objectives-oriented (principles-based) approach relies on professional judgment. A rules-based approach relies on detailed bright-line tests.

Solution

  • a. Objectives-oriented. There are few rules to sidestep, as with the structured transactions at Enron.
  • b. Rules-based. Detailed rules give clear support for auditors and a safe harbor for preparers.
  • c. Objectives-oriented. Reliance on judgment can lead to different treatment of similar transactions.

Either approach can be abused. The key is whether management is committed to high-quality financial reporting, which leads to ethics.

The conceptual framework

The conceptual framework, sometimes called an “accounting constitution,” sets out what makes information useful, defines the building blocks of the statements, and states the assumptions underlying GAAP.

  • The conceptual framework is a coherent system of objectives and fundamentals that guides standard setting. It is not itself authoritative GAAP.
  • Fundamental qualities: relevance (predictive value, confirmatory value, materiality) and faithful representation (complete, neutral, free from error).
  • Enhancing qualities: comparability (including consistency), verifiability, timeliness, understandability. The cost effectiveness constraint says benefits of information must exceed its costs.
  • Elements: assets, liabilities, equity, investments by owners, distributions to owners, comprehensive income, revenues, expenses, gains, losses.
  • Underlying assumptions: economic entity, going concern, periodicity, monetary unit.

Need a hint?
Relevance asks whether the information makes a difference. Faithful representation asks whether it depicts reality fairly. Enhancing qualities make good information more useful.

Solution

  • b. Faithful representation.
  • c. Enhancing. Consistency supports comparability.
  • d. Cost effectiveness constraint.
  • e. Relevance. This is confirmatory value.
  • f. Enhancing. Verifiability is an enhancing characteristic.

Example 7 — Elements of financial statements

Identify the element for each item.
Need a hint?
Revenues and expenses come from ongoing major or central operations; gains and losses come from peripheral or incidental transactions.

Solution

  • a. Investment by owners (increases equity).
  • b. Distribution to owners (decreases equity).
  • c. Gain. Incidental to operations.
  • d. Revenue. Central operations.
  • e. Loss. Incidental to operations.
  • f. Asset.

Example 8 — Underlying assumptions

Identify the assumption behind each practice.
Need a hint?
Match the practice to the idea: who is reported on, how long the business lasts, how often reports are issued, and what unit is used.

Solution

  • a. Economic entity. Business and owner are separate.
  • b. Going concern. The business is assumed to operate long enough to use its assets; if liquidation were expected, assets would be measured at liquidation values.
  • c. Periodicity. The economic life is divided into artificial periods for timely information.
  • d. Monetary unit. The dollar is treated as stable in purchasing power.

Recognition, measurement, and disclosure

Once the elements are defined, GAAP must decide when to record them, at what amount, and how to describe them.

  • Recognition decides whether an item is recorded; measurement decides the amount; disclosure decides what else users need to know (parenthetical comments, notes, supplemental schedules), subject to cost effectiveness.
  • Mixed-attribute measurement: historical cost, net realizable value, current cost, present value of future cash flows, and fair value.
  • Fair value hierarchy: Level 1 quoted prices in active markets for identical items; Level 2 other observable inputs (similar items, observable market data); Level 3 unobservable inputs based on the company’s own assumptions.
  • Fair value option: companies may choose to report certain financial assets and liabilities at fair value, with changes included in net income.
  • Revenue/expense approach emphasizes recognizing revenues when earned and matching expenses. Asset/liability approach defines assets and liabilities first, and revenues and expenses follow from changes in them. GAAP has been moving toward the asset/liability approach.

Example 9 — Fair value hierarchy

Classify the inputs used to measure fair value.
Need a hint?
Level 1 is the most reliable (identical items, active market); Level 3 is the least observable.

Solution

  • a. Level 1. Quoted prices for identical assets in active markets.
  • b. Level 2. Observable inputs for similar items.
  • c. Level 3. Unobservable inputs reflecting the company’s own assumptions.

Opponents of fair value worry that Level 3 estimates may lack faithful representation and invite manipulation; proponents argue historical cost can be less relevant.

Ethics in accounting

Technical competence is not enough when a manager asks for an entry that misleads users. A simple framework helps you work through the decision.

  • Ethics is a system of moral principles used to judge right and wrong. Professions such as public accounting adopt codes of conduct (the AICPA Code, the IMA code).
  • Seven-step framework: (1) determine the facts; (2) identify the ethical issue and stakeholders; (3) identify the values involved; (4) specify alternative courses of action; (5) evaluate them against the values; (6) identify the consequences for stakeholders; (7) decide and act.
  • Advertising costs, like most similar costs, are expensed rather than deferred to match later sales; shifting expenses to an asset to reach a profit target misstates income.

Example 12 — An ethical dilemma

You work for Cashiers Valley Sporting Goods. In May and June the company spent $1,200,000 on a radio and TV campaign, all charged to advertising expense. Operating income before advertising for the quarter is $900,000. The CFO asks you to move the entire $1,200,000 to prepaid advertising so the quarter shows a profit, because the bank requires quarterly profits to keep the loan in good standing.
Need a hint?
Compute income both ways. Then think about who is affected (the bank, other lenders, investors) and which values (honesty, faithful representation) are involved.
$
$
$

Solution

Correct treatment: $900,000 − $1,200,000 = $(300,000) operating loss. With the CFO’s entry: $900,000 − $0 = $900,000, an overstatement of $1,200,000.

The request is not appropriate. The benefit of the advertising in a future month is uncertain, so the cost is expensed rather than deferred, and the purpose of the entry is to hide a loss from the bank. Stakeholders include the bank, other creditors, shareholders, and employees.

Alternatives (step 4) include: make the entry, refuse and explain GAAP, discuss with the audit committee, or disclose the loss to the bank and seek a covenant waiver. Information must be neutral and free from error, so you should not make the entry.

Wrap-up: Chapter 1 at a glance

  • Purpose: financial accounting supplies external users, mainly investors and creditors, with information to assess the amounts, timing, and uncertainty of future cash flows. Accrual accounting predicts those cash flows better than cash-basis accounting over short periods.
  • Standard setting: SEC (authority) → FASB (ASUs and the Codification) in the private sector; IASB issues IFRS; GASB covers governments; politics influences standards.
  • Quality safeguards: management prepares, auditors opine, the Sarbanes-Oxley Act created the PCAOB and internal-control reporting, and ethics codes guide professionals.
  • Conceptual framework: relevance and faithful representation are fundamental; comparability, verifiability, timeliness, and understandability enhance; cost effectiveness constrains. Elements, assumptions, recognition, measurement, and disclosure complete the structure.
  • IFRS: the IASB and FASB have converged on many topics, but differences remain; IFRS is not yet adopted for U.S. companies.
