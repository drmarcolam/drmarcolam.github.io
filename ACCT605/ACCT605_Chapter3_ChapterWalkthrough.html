The balance sheet and its classifications

The balance sheet organizes assets, liabilities, and equity so users can assess liquidity, solvency, and financial flexibility.

  • The balance sheet is a position statement: assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time. It helps users assess liquidity, solvency, and financial flexibility.
  • Limitations: many assets are reported at historical cost, not current value; the statement omits some resources (for example, internally developed intangibles); and it relies on estimates and judgments.
  • Current assets are cash and assets expected to be converted to cash, sold, or consumed within one year or the operating cycle, whichever is longer. Typical order: cash and cash equivalents, short-term investments, accounts receivable, inventories, prepaid expenses.
  • Noncurrent assets: investments and funds; property, plant, and equipment; intangible assets; other assets.
  • Current liabilities are expected to be settled with current assets or by creating other current liabilities. Everything else is long-term. Equity: paid-in capital, retained earnings, accumulated other comprehensive income, and treasury stock (a deduction).

Example 2 — Cash, cash equivalents, and restrictions

Cedar Hollow Foods reports the following at year-end: currency and coins $4,000; checking account $61,000; Treasury bill with a 60-day original maturity $30,000; 6-month certificate of deposit $25,000; compensating balance of $15,000 required by a loan agreement (held in the checking account, loan matures in 3 years); and a postdated customer check of $2,000 (not yet deposited, not included above).
Need a hint?
Cash equivalents are highly liquid investments with original maturities of three months or less. Legally restricted cash related to long-term borrowings is reported as noncurrent.
$

Solution

Cash and cash equivalents: currency $4,000 + checking $61,000 − restricted $15,000 + T-bill $30,000 = $80,000. The T-bill had an original maturity of three months or less, so it is a cash equivalent.

The 6-month CD has an original maturity of more than three months, so it is a short-term investment.

The compensating balance supports a long-term loan, so it is reported as a noncurrent asset (restricted cash). The postdated check is a receivable, not cash.

Disclosures, MD&A, and the audit

The notes, management’s discussion, and the auditor’s report supplement the statements so that users can interpret them.

  • Disclosure notes are an integral part of the financial statements. Typical notes: summary of significant accounting policies, subsequent events, related-party transactions, commitments and contingencies, and segment information.
  • Subsequent events: Recognized events provide additional evidence about conditions that existed at the balance sheet date (adjust the statements). Nonrecognized events arise after the balance sheet date (disclose only).
  • Related-party transactions must be disclosed. Commitments and contingencies are disclosed when appropriate.
  • MD&A (management’s discussion and analysis) covers liquidity, capital resources, and results of operations. The management report states management’s responsibility. Compensation disclosures cover executives.
  • Audit: an independent CPA expresses an opinion on whether the statements are presented fairly, in all material respects, in conformity with GAAP. An unqualified (clean) opinion is the best result. Auditors also report on internal control over financial reporting for public companies and communicate critical audit matters.

Example 5 — Subsequent events

Greenfield Mills has a December 31 year-end. The financial statements are issued on March 5. Classify each event that occurred in the new year.
Need a hint?
Does the event give more information about a condition that existed at the balance sheet date, or does it reflect a new condition that arose afterward?

Solution

  • a. Recognized. The bankruptcy confirms the customer’s condition at year-end, which affects the allowance for uncollectible accounts.
  • b. Nonrecognized. The fire is a new event; disclose it.
  • c. Nonrecognized. A bond issue after year-end is disclosed.
  • d. Recognized. The settlement provides evidence about a condition that existed at year-end.

Financial analysis and risk ratios

Analysts transform reported numbers into ratios to evaluate liquidity, solvency, and profitability.

  • Liquidity = ability to pay current obligations. Solvency = ability to meet long-term obligations.
  • Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities. Acid-test = (cash + short-term investments + net receivables) ÷ current liabilities.
  • Debt to equity = total liabilities ÷ shareholders’ equity. Times interest earned = (net income + interest expense + taxes) ÷ interest expense.
  • Return on assets = net income ÷ average total assets. Profit margin = net income ÷ net sales. Asset turnover = net sales ÷ average total assets. ROA = profit margin × asset turnover.
  • Financial analysis techniques: horizontal (trend), vertical (common-size), and ratio analysis.

Example 7 — Risk ratios

Pisgah Trails Inc. reports: total assets $900,000; total liabilities $540,000; shareholders’ equity $360,000. Net income $72,000, interest expense $18,000, income tax expense $30,000.

Solution

Times interest earned = ($72,000 + $18,000 + $30,000) ÷ $18,000 = $120,000 ÷ $18,000 = 6.67.

U.S. GAAP and IFRS

A few differences affect the balance sheet and segment disclosures.

  • Presentation: U.S. GAAP presents current items first (liquidity order). IFRS companies often present noncurrent items first, and the balance sheet may be presented as net assets.
  • Segments: IFRS 8 and U.S. GAAP (ASC 280) both use the management approach, with differences in required disclosures (for example, IFRS also requires segment liabilities if reported to the chief operating decision maker).
  • Under IFRS, current liabilities also include those a company does not have an unconditional right to defer settlement of for twelve months.

Wrap-up: Chapter 3 at a glance

  • The balance sheet lists assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time, classified as current or noncurrent. It has limitations, including historical-cost measurement.
  • Cash and cash equivalents include highly liquid investments maturing in three months or less when purchased; restricted cash tied to long-term debt is noncurrent.
  • Disclosures: notes (accounting policies, subsequent events, related parties), MD&A, and the audit report all help users interpret the statements.
  • Ratios: current ratio and acid-test for liquidity; debt to equity and times interest earned for solvency; profit margin, asset turnover, and ROA for profitability.
  • IFRS differs mainly in presentation order and some segment disclosures.
